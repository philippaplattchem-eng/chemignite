T1: Atomic Structure & the Periodic Table40 topics▶
1.1.1.1Changing Models of the Atom
  • To score on "give two differences between Dalton's model and today's model", state new positive content of the modern model, subatomic particles, the nucleus, electrons in shells, mostly empty space, or isotopes, not just the negative reverse of Dalton.
  • The most common command word on this topic is give, usually for 1–2 marks; explain questions ask you to link new experimental evidence to a change in the model.
1.1.2.1The Structure of an Atom
  • When asked to compare today's model with an older model (e.g. Dalton's), build the answer around the specification components rather than just repeating the stem.
1.1.2.2The Sub-atomic Particles
  • The negative sign on the electron's charge must always be written explicitly, "1" is not the same as "−1".
  • For a 2-mark "explain why an atom has no overall charge" answer, give both (a) equal numbers of protons and electrons and (b) the charges therefore cancel or balance.
  • In, examiners noted that successful answers named the particles specifically; vague references to "smaller particles" did not score.
1.1.3.1The Nucleus
  • Do not confuse "where most of the mass is" with "where the most particles are", there are still electrons present, they just weigh almost nothing.
  • The specification and 1.6 are foundation building blocks: they rarely appear alone but are constantly assumed in questions on atomic structure, isotopes, and the development of the atomic model.
1.1.3.2Mass Number & Atomic Number
  • The mass number is always a whole number because it counts particles, but the relative atomic mass on the periodic table is usually not a whole number because it is a weighted average across isotopes.
  • Two atoms of the same element always have the same atomic number, but they can have different mass numbers if they are different isotopes.
  • Changing the number of protons changes the element, this never happens in ordinary chemical reactions.
  • The most common Higher-tier mistake is treating an ion as an atom when calculating subatomic particles, always check the charge before working out electrons.
1.1.4.1Isotopes
  • Isotopes are different atoms of the same element containing the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons in their nuclei. Both halves are needed to score full marks on a definition.
  • The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons; it defines the element and never changes between isotopes of the same element.
  • Number of neutrons = A − Z. This rearrangement is Maths skill 3b and is the most-tested 1-mark calculation in Topic 1.
  • When asked "in terms of subatomic particles", name protons and neutrons explicitly. Naming "atomic number" and "mass number" only scores 1 of 2 marks.
1.1.5.1Calculating Relative Atomic Mass
  • If abundances are given as decimal fractions rather than percentages, divide by 1 instead of 100, check the form of the data before substituting.
  • Always show every step of your working, multiplication, sum, division by 100, so you can pick up error-carried-forward marks if a single arithmetic slip occurs.
  • Always present the final answer to the requested number of decimal places (commonly 2 d.p. on Edexcel), this is usually a stand-alone mark you can earn even if your earlier calculation is wrong.
  • Sense-check: Ar must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotopic masses, and must be closer to the more abundant isotope, if your answer is outside that range or skewed the wrong way, recheck your working.
1.2.1.1Mendeleev's Arrangement
  • The modern periodic table is ordered by atomic (proton) number rather than atomic mass, which removes the order anomalies Mendeleev had to fix manually.
  • In the exam, a "state one difference" question is worth 1 mark and a "describe two differences" question is worth 2 marks; choose differences from genuinely different categories (ordering property, element coverage, noble gases, transition-metal position).
  • The most common student error is saying Mendeleev used "atomic number", he used atomic mass; atomic number is the modern ordering property.
1.2.1.2Mendeleev's Predictions
  • Where the next known element didn't fit the pattern, he left a gap rather than forcing a poor match.
  • To answer a 2-mark "Describe" question on this topic, you must mention both ideas: that he left gaps AND that he used trends in neighbouring elements to predict the properties.
1.2.1.3Problems with Mendeleev's Table
  • The most common swap-pairs are argon/potassium (Ar 39.9 / 39.1), tellurium/iodine (Ar 127.6 / 126.9), and cobalt/nickel (Ar 58.9 / 58.7), in each case the lighter-Ar element actually has the higher atomic number.
  • Atomic number is a whole number that is fixed for every atom of an element, so it always gives a consistent ordering.
  • The cause of the swap-pairs is the relative abundance of isotopes: if a lighter element's isotopes happen to be unusually heavy, or a heavier element's isotopes are unusually light, the average masses end up in the "wrong" order.
  • Mendeleev sometimes swapped pairs by hand (e.g. tellurium before iodine) because the chemistry demanded it, even though mass alone did not justify the swap.
1.2.2.1The Modern Periodic Table
  • For example, all Group 1 elements (Li, Na, K…) have one outer electron, lose it to form 1+ ions, and react vigorously with water.
  • Confusing "group" with "period" is a known examiner-flagged error . Always state both explicitly: "in group X and in period Y".
1.2.2.2Metals and Non-Metals in the Periodic Table
  • Metals lose electrons from their outer shell to form positive ions (cations) such as Na⁺, Mg²⁺, and Al³⁺.
  • Metals lose electrons easily because they have few outer-shell electrons (1, 2, or 3) and these electrons are far from the nucleus and shielded, so weakly held.
  • Hydrogen is an exception: it sits in Group 1 but behaves as a non-metal, colourless gas, does not conduct, can gain or share electrons.
  • When asked to Explain the metal/non-metal classification of an element, always link the answer to the number of outer-shell electrons and whether they are lost or gained to reach a full shell.
1.2.3.1Electronic Configurations
  • Electrons always fill shells from the innermost outwards, because the inner shells are at lower energy and are more strongly attracted to the nucleus.
  • Common exam wording trap : saying an element has "four outer shells" is not credit-worthy, an atom has only one outer shell. Correct phrasing is "four shells" (justifies period) and "two electrons in its outer shell" (justifies group).
  • Common exam wording trap : always check whether the question asks for the period or the group, a significant share of students lose marks by giving the wrong one.
  • The specification is typically tested as a 1-mark "predict / give the electronic configuration" item; the specification is typically tested as a 2-mark "explain" item linking configuration to position.
1.3.1.1Ions
  • Many common ions (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, O²⁻, F⁻) reach the electronic configuration of a noble gas, a useful sanity check on your electron count.
  • The most common exam mistake is treating an ion as if it were a neutral atom, always check for the charge symbol before counting protons.
  • The second most common mistake is adjusting electrons in the wrong direction; writing "electrons = Z − charge (signed)" before you start avoids this.
  • Exam questions on this topic are usually short (1–3 marks) and appear early in Paper 1; the typical task is a calculate question asking for protons, neutrons or electrons given the mass number, atomic number and charge.
1.3.1.2Formation of Ions
  • Metals in groups 1 and 2 lose their outer electrons to form positive ions (cations); non-metals in groups 6 and 7 gain electrons to form negative ions (anions).
  • In an ionic compound the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge, this fixes the ratio of cations to anions in the formula.
  • To get marks on an "explain how ions form" question, you must say which atom loses electrons, which gains, how many, and what charge each ion ends up with, never just restate the starting electron configurations.
  • Ionic bonding is not the same as covalent bonding, atoms transfer electrons, they do not share them; dot-and-cross diagrams should show separate ions in brackets with charges outside, not a shared pair.
1.3.2.1Ionic Bonds
  • The total positive charge must balance the total negative charge, this controls the formula (NaCl: 1:1; MgO: 1:1; CaF₂: 1:2; Na₂O: 2:1).
  • When charges do not match (e.g. Ca²⁺ + F⁻), more than one of an ion is needed and each must be drawn separately with its own brackets and charge.
  • A common exam mistake is drawing a shared pair between a metal and non-metal, this is a covalent diagram and will not score; always show transfer for ionic compounds.
  • The same topic can be examined as a short explain question, a complete-the-diagram task, or as part of an extended response combining bond formation with lattice structure.
1.3.2.2Ionic Lattices
  • The forces act in all directions throughout the lattice, not just between one pair of ions.
  • In a 2-mark "describe the structure" answer, you must include both the arrangement (regular 3D lattice of alternating positive and negative ions) and the forces (strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely-charged ions).
  • The examiner feedback flagged that students routinely omit the word lattice and fail to mention that positive and negative ions alternate, including both phrases is the simplest way to upgrade an answer.
1.3.3.1Naming Ionic Compounds
  • An ionic compound's name is always written with the cation first (metal or ammonium) and the anion second.
  • Naming errors compound (no pun intended) into other questions, a wrong name carried into a word equation, a balanced equation, or a calculation often loses the marks for the equation and the calculation, even if the chemistry is otherwise correct.
  • Spelling matters: "sulfate" not "sulphate" is the spelling used in current Edexcel GCSE assessments, and Edexcel accepts both spellings on the mark scheme, but consistency within an answer is the safer habit.
1.3.3.2Deducing Formulae
  • Ionic compounds are electrically neutral, so the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge in the formula.
  • The six named polyatomic anions you must recognise are hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, plus the oxide O²⁻ and halides as monatomic anions.
  • A polyatomic ion taken more than once must be put in brackets before the subscript: Ca(OH)₂, Mg(NO₃)₂, (NH₄)₂SO₄, Fe₂(CO₃)₃.
  • Polyatomic ions taken only once do not need brackets: NaOH, K₂SO₄, NaNO₃.
1.4.1.1Covalent Bonding
  • Always use the phrase "shared pair of electrons" in your definition, dropping "pair" is a common reason to lose marks.
  • Bond-type confusion (e.g. drawing NaCl as a covalent molecule) is a recorded students error in past Edexcel papers; always check whether you are dealing with a metal-non-metal or a non-metal-non-metal pair before deciding the bond type.
1.4.2.1Dot & Cross Diagrams for Molecules
  • Show outer-shell electrons only, never draw inner shells in a GCSE molecular dot-and-cross.
1.5.1.1Classifying Substances
  • Giant covalent substances (diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide) have a huge network of atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout, very high melting points, insoluble in water, and do not conduct (except graphite, which has delocalised electrons in its layers).
  • The classic exam task is a 6-mark levelled question: deduce structure and bonding from a properties table, and link each property to the structure and bonding to reach level 3.
  • The most common mistake is mixing up the properties of ionic and simple molecular substances, always check the conductivity pattern first.
  • Always identify the bonding type for each substance before describing the properties, without classification, the maximum mark is capped at level 2.
1.5.2.1Comparing Ionic & Covalent Compounds
  • Ionic compounds do not conduct as solids because the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice.
  • Simple molecular compounds do not conduct electricity in any state because there are no ions and no delocalised electrons, the molecules themselves are uncharged.
  • Saying "the weak covalent bonds break" when CO₂ boils is the single most common Higher-tier error, it is the intermolecular forces that are overcome.
  • The charge carriers in molten or dissolved ionic compounds are ions, not electrons, saying "free electrons" is wrong for ionic compounds.
1.5.3.1Diamond & Graphite
  • The exam the key skill being tested is always the same: identical atoms in different arrangements give different properties, every full-mark answer must link structure to bonding to property.
1.5.4.1Fullerenes & Graphene
  • Solid C₆₀ has a low melting point because individual C₆₀ molecules are held together only by weak intermolecular forces, the covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong but do not break on melting.
  • Saying "the weak covalent bonds break" when C₆₀ melts is the same classic error as for CO₂, what's overcome is the intermolecular forces between molecules.
1.5.5.1Polymers
  • The named example you must know is poly(ethene), made from many ethene (C₂H₄) monomers.
  • At this topic you are not expected to draw the repeat unit, write a balanced equation, or distinguish addition from condensation polymerisation, that content belongs to Section 9.
  • The most common errors are confusing the monomer with the polymer, leaving out hydrogen atoms in mass calculations, and assuming water is released when ethene polymerises (it is not, this is addition polymerisation, and all atoms are conserved).
1.5.6.1Properties of Metals
  • The word most in the specification matters: mercury is a liquid metal, graphite is a conducting non-metal, and iodine is a shiny non-metallic solid.
  • On compare questions, always use comparative wording, higher, lower, stronger, more conductive. Words like "good" or "better" without an explicit "than" comparator do not score .
  • Never just quote numbers back from a data table without comparing them, the comparison itself is what earns the mark.
  • The charge carriers in a metal are electrons; in a molten or aqueous ionic compound they are ions. Do not mix the two pictures up.
1.5.7.1Limitations of Models
  • To answer a "limitations" question for full marks you must say both what each model does show and what it fails to show.
  • Dot and cross diagrams show electron transfer/sharing and the origin of bonding electrons, but do not show 3D shape, real bond angles, atomic sizes or electron motion.
  • 3D space-filling models show relative atomic sizes and the overall shape and volume of the molecule, but obscure the bonds and do not show electron density or motion.
  • The examiner feedback specifically flagged that students failed to explicitly state what models cannot show, describing only what is shown loses marks.
1.6.1.1Relative Formula Mass
  • The classic exam error is forgetting the subscript: Mr(CaCO₃) = 100, not 68. examiner feedback across and specifically flagged students "failing to multiply the relative atomic mass of oxygen by three".
  • Decimal Ar values (Cl = 35.5, Cu = 63.5) give decimal RFMs, do not round to a whole number.
  • Show each Ar × subscript multiplication explicitly in your working, this earns the working mark (typically 1 of 2 marks) even if the final addition slips.
  • RFM has no units, never write "g", "kg", or "g/mol" next to your answer.
1.6.2.1Empirical Formulae
  • The two formulae can be the same (H₂O, CO₂) or different (CH₂O vs C₆H₁₂O₆), they are the same whenever the molecular subscripts cannot be simplified further.
  • Always divide mass by Ar, never multiply. Multiplying mass × Ar is the most common Foundation error and gives a meaningless number.
  • Mass goes on top of the fraction, Ar goes on the bottom. Inverting this is the most common Higher-tier slip and gives the wrong final formula.
  • To find an empirical formula from a molecular formula: divide every subscript by their highest common factor (e.g. C₆H₁₂O₆ ÷ 6 → CH₂O).
1.6.3.1Determine an Empirical Formula
  • The magnesium ribbon must be cleaned with emery paper first to remove the oxide layer, otherwise the mass recorded as "magnesium" already includes some oxygen.
  • Major sources of error are escape of MgO smoke (lid open too long), incomplete reaction (not heated to constant mass), and uncleaned magnesium (oxide already present).
  • Safety: do not look directly at the burning magnesium, the bright light can damage your eyes. Use tongs and a heat-resistant mat for the hot crucible.
  • In the exam this topic most often appears as a 2–3 mark "describe" or "explain why" question on the procedure, sometimes followed by a numerical sub-part using sample mass data.
1.6.4.1Conservation of Mass
  • The law works because atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed, the same atoms appear on both sides of a balanced equation.
  • Saying "the mass decreases" is description and earns only 1 mark, you must explain why by referring to the gas leaving the open container.
  • The word evaporation is wrong for these reactions, the gas is produced by a chemical reaction, not evaporated from a liquid. Use "released", "given off" or "escapes".
  • A correct 2-mark "explain" answer always names the gas (CO₂ or O₂) and says it leaves the flask or is taken from the air.
1.6.5.1Reacting Masses
  • The balanced equation is the source of the ratio, coefficients give the ratio of moles (or "formula units") in which substances react and are produced.
  • At Higher tier, examiners explicitly recommend the moles method: full-mark answers in used it and laid out working clearly.
  • RFM of the product on its own is not the answer, this is the most common Foundation mistake. The full method needs both RFMs, the ratio, and the scaling step.
  • Always check the equation is balanced before you start, an unbalanced equation gives a wrong ratio and a wrong answer.
1.6.5.2Percentage Composition
  • The total mass of an element is Ar of the element × the subscript, not just Ar alone. For CaCO₃, the mass of oxygen is 3 × 16 = 48, not 16.
  • Always read the question for the significant-figures instruction before you start the calculation, both and, examiner feedback flagged sig figs as the most common reason for losing the final mark.
  • Do not round midway through your working, keep the long decimal and only round at the very end.
  • Typical exam mark allocation is 2–3 marks; the command word is almost always "Calculate" with a sig-fig precision instruction attached.
1.6.6.1Calculating Concentration
  • The single most common exam error is failing to convert the volume from cm³ to dm³ before substituting, this loses both the substitution and final-answer marks .
  • Always show the unit conversion as a separate line of working, it bags an isolated mark even if the rest of the calculation slips.
  • The "volume of solution" in the formula is the total final volume of the mixture, not just the volume of solvent added.
  • Always include the unit (g dm⁻³, g, dm³ or cm³) in your final answer, a number without a unit can lose the final-answer mark.
1.6.7.1The Mole
  • Always identify what kind of particle the question is asking about, atoms, molecules, formula units or ions, before applying the Avogadro constant.
  • Show full working line by line, examiners specifically rewarded clear setting-out on the paper, while common errors included arithmetic slips and missed unit conversions.
  • Write any answer that involves the Avogadro constant in standard form, particle counts almost always lie between 10¹⁸ and 10²⁵.
  • This is a Higher tier only topic, it appears on and as a 2–4 mark "Calculate" question, typically in the middle or later parts of the paper.
1.6.8.1Limiting Reagents
  • Reactants combine in the fixed ratio given by the coefficients in the balanced equation, never by their masses.
  • Then divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient in the balanced equation; the smaller number is the limiting reagent.
  • Explain-style answers must mention the balanced equation ratio, the reactant that runs out first, and the fact that excess reactant does nothing extra.
  • Always show your working clearly: equation, Mr values, moles of each reactant, divide-by-coefficient comparison, limiting reagent named, then product mass with units.
1.6.9.1Writing & Balancing Equations
  • When the question gives you the names of reactants and products in the stem, use those names in your word equation, the examiner feedback makes this explicit.
  • A symbol equation uses chemical formulae instead of names, and must include state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous.
  • You only ever change the coefficients (big numbers in front), never the subscripts (small numbers inside), changing a subscript changes the substance, not the balance.
  • The seven diatomic elements in uncombined form are H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂, writing them as a single atom is one of the most common balancing errors.
1.6.9.2Ionic Equations
  • The standard process is: full balanced equation → split (aq) ionic compounds → cancel ions identical on both sides → check atoms and charges balance.
  • Charge must balance just like atoms: the total charge on the left must equal the total charge on the right.
  • Strong acid + strong alkali neutralisation: the ionic equation is always H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l), no matter which acid and alkali.
  • Simply re-copying the full balanced equation in answer to an "ionic equation" question scores zero marks .
1.6.9.3Deducing Stoichiometry
  • Stoichiometry is the whole-number ratio in which substances react and are produced, the big numbers in front of each formula in a balanced equation.
  • The mole ratio of substances taking part in a reaction is identical to the ratio of coefficients in the balanced equation, because a mole is just a fixed count of particles.
  • Keep at least 3 significant figures during the moles step; do not round too early or the final ratio will go wrong.
  • Always atom-check at the end: substitute your deduced coefficients into the equation and verify the number of atoms of each element matches on both sides.
T2: States of Matter & Mixtures12 topics▶
2.1.1.1The Three States of Matter
  • Particle-model diagrams must show particles as identical circles, regular and touching for a solid, irregular but touching for a liquid, far apart and random for a gas, with arrows indicating movement.
  • State-of-matter questions almost always appear at the start of Foundation tier Paper 1, identify the solid at 20 °C from a melting/boiling point table) and use the command words Give, Name, Use and Predict.
  • When discussing the forces broken on changing state, you are always talking about the forces between particles, not the bonds inside them, covalent bonds inside molecules do not break on melting or boiling.
  • Always describe energy in relative terms (greater than / less than another state), never as an absolute number, the spec wording is "relative energy".
2.1.1.2Interconversions
  • The examiner feedback explicitly recommends practising interpretation of heating and cooling curves, examiners want you to read off melting and boiling points from plateaus, not just recite the six names.
  • The specification maps onto Mathematical Skill 5b, being able to visualise and represent particle arrangements in 2D/3D and read off temperatures and states from a labelled heating or cooling curve.
  • Questions on this topic appear almost exclusively at the front of Foundation Paper 1 as 1-mark Give/Name questions, easy marks if the six names and their directions are learned cold.
2.1.1.3Predicting Physical State
  • Always compare temperatures on a number line, –183 °C is colder than –114 °C, not warmer. The negative sign matters.
  • The smaller number in a "mp / bp" row is always the melting point; if the order looks reversed, check the column headings before trusting them.
  • Mercury and bromine are the two famous liquid at room temperature exceptions, do not assume "metal = solid" without checking the data.
  • The command words for this topic are nearly always predict or give the letter, keep the answer short and exactly in the format asked for.
2.2.1.1Pure Substance vs Mixture
  • Pure substances melt at one specific temperature (a sharp melting point); mixtures melt over a range of temperatures.
  • A sharp melting point only proves the sample is pure, to identify which pure substance it is, you must compare its melting point with a known literature value.
  • When answering "State whether the sample is pure or impure and justify" questions (ii)), always pair the observation (sharp temperature vs range) with the conclusion (pure vs impure), that pairing is what the mark scheme rewards.
2.2.2.1Simple Distillation
  • It works whenever the components have a very large difference in boiling point, so that only the lower-boiling component enters the gas phase at the heating temperature used.
  • The apparatus consists of a round-bottomed flask (with anti-bumping granules), a thermometer, a side-arm leading to a water-cooled condenser, and a beaker to catch the distillate. The collection end must be open to the atmosphere.
  • The thermometer bulb sits at the side-arm exit so that it measures the temperature of the vapour about to enter the condenser, not the temperature of the boiling liquid. This is the most frequently examined detail of the entire topic .
  • Do not heat to dryness and do not seal the receiving end, both errors can damage the apparatus.
2.2.2.2Fractional Distillation
  • The thermometer, mounted at the top of the column with its bulb level with the side-arm, signals which liquid is currently distilling, the reading stays close to that liquid's boiling point while it is being collected, then rises when the next fraction starts.
  • The three concepts examiners want to see in a 3-mark answer are heating, evaporation and condensing, tied together by the idea of a temperature gradient .
  • Always name the process ("fractional distillation") at the start of an extended answer, easy marks are lost when the technique is described but not named .
  • Choose fractional distillation (not simple) whenever the mixture has two or more liquids that both evaporate and you need to separate them from each other, simple distillation is for separating a single solvent from a solution.
2.2.2.3Filtration
  • The most common errors flagged by the Higher tier examiner feedback are closed funnels, missing filter paper and labelling the funnel as "filter".
  • Use the keywords dissolve, filter, filter paper, filtrate and residue in your written answers, examiners reward the right vocabulary used in the right place.
2.2.2.4Crystallisation
  • The most heavily examined misconception is heating the solution to dryness. You must heat only until the solution is saturated, the examiner feedback flagged this as the main reason students lost marks.
  • Drying is done by pressing crystals between filter papers or by leaving them in a warm oven on a low setting, either method scores credit .
  • Crystallisation is almost always paired with filtration in salt-preparation experiments: filtration removes the insoluble impurity (such as excess metal oxide or carbonate), and crystallisation then recovers the dissolved salt from the filtrate.
  • On the Foundation paper, crystallisation often appears as a 6-mark "describe in detail" question, ammonium sulfate by titration + crystallisation), these are levels-marked, so include every stage to climb into the top level.
2.2.2.5Paper Chromatography
  • Paper chromatography separates soluble substances in a mixture; substances that do not dissolve in the chosen solvent will not move from the baseline.
  • The baseline must be drawn in pencil, pencil graphite does not dissolve in the solvent, so it stays in place and does not contaminate the chromatogram.
  • The sample spots must be placed above the solvent level, otherwise the sample dissolves straight into the solvent in the tank instead of being carried up the paper.
  • The chromatography tank must be sealed to keep the atmosphere inside saturated with solvent vapour and prevent the solvent evaporating from the paper.
2.2.3.1Interpreting Chromatograms
  • An unknown substance is identified by running it on the same chromatogram, in the same solvent, at the same time as one or more known standards, and matching both Rf value and colour.
  • Rf values are always between 0 and 1, are dimensionless (no units), and are conventionally quoted to two significant figures in Edexcel mark schemes.
  • The Foundation paper specifically penalised students who could not recall the Rf formula or who failed to give the answer to 2 s.f., that exact value, 0.29, was the expected response.
  • If a spot does not move from the baseline in a particular solvent, the explanation needed for a 2-mark "explain" question is that a different solvent is required because the original solvent did not dissolve that substance.
2.2.4.1Core Practical Investigating Inks
  • A single coloured spot on the chromatogram means a single dye; multiple spots mean a mixture of dyes. A dye is identified by matching its position with a reference dye run on the same paper or by calculating its Rf value.
  • Rf = distance moved by dye / distance moved by solvent front, with both measured from the baseline. The answer is always between 0 and 1 and is quoted to 2 significant figures, examiners specifically flagged this rounding rule in the examiner feedback.
  • Independent variable: the ink. Dependent variable: the number and position of the coloured spots (or the Rf values). Control variables: solvent, paper type, run distance, temperature, spot size, that the tank is covered.
  • Safety: wear goggles, treat ethanol and other organic solvents as flammable (use a heating mantle if needed), always include anti-bumping granules, never seal the receiving end, and never heat the distillation flask to dryness.
2.2.5.1Purifying Water
  • Filtration removes insoluble solid particles only, it does not remove dissolved salts and it does not kill or remove bacteria. The Higher report explicitly penalises saying filtration removes bacteria or "impurities".
  • Water for chemical analysis must contain no dissolved salts, dissolved ions already in the water would react with test reagents and give a false positive (e.g. tap water gives a white precipitate with AgNO₃ even when the sample being tested has no chloride).
  • On an "explain why potable water is not pure water" question (iv)), pair (i) potable contains dissolved ions (cite the data table given) with (ii) pure water is only H₂O, both halves are needed for the 2 marks.
T3: Chemical Changes19 topics▶
3.1.1.1Defining Acids & Bases
  • Common laboratory acids: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃). Common alkalis: sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂), ammonia solution.
  • The ionic equation for neutralisation is always the same: H+ (aq) + OH− (aq) → H₂O (l). The spectator ions form the salt.
  • When asked to explain why an acid is acidic, you must name hydrogen ions (H+) explicitly, saying "it dissociates" alone is not enough .
  • When asked to explain a pH change during neutralisation, refer to H+ reacting with OH− (i.e. the neutralisation reaction itself), not just changing ion concentrations .
3.1.1.2The pH Scale
  • Universal indicator changes colour across the scale: red (strong acid) → orange → yellow → green (neutral) → blue → purple (strong alkali).
  • The pH of a solution can be measured with universal indicator (colour comparison) or a pH probe / meter (digital, more accurate, works on coloured solutions).
  • Common Paper 1 exam style: 1-mark recall ("Give the pH…") or graph-reading ("Use the graph to find the pH…").
3.1.1.3Indicators
  • An indicator is a substance that changes colour in acidic or alkaline solution; the three named for Edexcel GCSE are litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein.
  • Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in alkali, changing around pH 9, used in titrations where the end-point is alkaline.
  • Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators that gives a continuous range of colours from red (strong acid) through green (neutral) to purple (strong alkali); it is used to estimate pH.
  • Indicators change colour because their protonated and deprotonated forms have different colours; high H⁺ concentration shifts the colour toward the acid form, high OH⁻ concentration shifts it toward the alkali form.
3.1.10.1Solubility Rules
  • To predict a precipitate, write the two possible products formed by swapping partners and check each against the rules; if either is insoluble, that compound is the precipitate.
  • Common precipitates to recognise: AgCl (white), BaSO₄ (white), CaCO₃ (white), Cu(OH)₂ (pale blue), PbI₂ (yellow).
  • Examiners flagged in that students mixed up copper oxide and copper carbonate, both are insoluble bases that react with acid to give a blue solution, but only the carbonate fizzes (releases CO₂).
  • State symbols are essential, every equation involving these rules should show (aq) for soluble species and (s) for insoluble ones, with a space between the formula and the state symbol.
3.1.11.1Preparing an Insoluble Salt
  • An insoluble salt cannot be made by neutralising an acid in solution, you must use a precipitation reaction between two soluble salts.
  • Never use tap water for washing, it contains ions that would contaminate the pure salt.
  • Typical exam ask: "Describe" question, 2–4 marks, one mark per step (mix / filter / wash / dry), often coupled with obtaining the soluble by-product by evaporation.
3.1.2.1Hydrogen Ions & pH
  • This topic is Higher tier only and is examined on Paper 1 for both Separate Chemistry and Combined Science (1SC0).
  • In exam questions, work out the pH difference first, then raise 10 to that power, then check the direction demanded by the wording.
  • When writing about pH changes during a reaction, always refer to hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions by name, not "atoms", "particles", or just "acid" .
  • State that H⁺ ions are reacting with OH⁻ ions or being neutralised, describing the chemical action scores more marks than just saying "there are fewer of them".
3.1.3.1Core Practical Investigating pH
  • A pH probe is more accurate than universal indicator paper, which only gives whole-number pH values from a colour chart.
  • Graph-plotting marks reward correct axis choice, sensible scales using more than half the grid, accurate point plotting, and a smooth curve of best fit, not dot-to-dot straight lines.
  • The reaction with calcium oxide is exothermic; add slowly and wear goggles to avoid splashes from the warming mixture.
  • To gain the top explanation mark, you must state that the concentration of H⁺ ions decreases as base is added, not just that the pH changes.
3.1.4.1Acid Strength & Concentration
  • Common strong acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃. Common weak acids: ethanoic, citric, carbonic.
  • To score full marks on a "strong acid" definition, you must say it fully dissociates AND that it produces hydrogen ions (H⁺), the second half is where most marks are lost.
  • To score full marks on a "dilute" definition, you must say a small amount of acid AND in a large volume of water (or per unit volume of solution).
  • This topic is Higher tier only and appears on Paper 1, typically as a 2–4 mark "Explain" question.
3.1.5.1Bases
  • A base is any substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only (the specification).
  • An alkali is a soluble base, a base that dissolves in water to release hydroxide ions, OH⁻ (aq) (the specification).
  • Common alkalis include sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide (limewater), and ammonia solution.
  • Common insoluble bases include copper(II) oxide, iron(III) oxide, and most transition-metal hydroxides, these are bases but not alkalis.
3.1.6.1Reactions of Acids
  • Acids react with metal oxides to give a salt and water; the solid dissolves and the solution often changes colour.
  • The salt's anion always comes from the acid: HCl → chloride, H₂SO₄ → sulfate, HNO₃ → nitrate.
  • Always include correct state symbols ((s), (l), (g), (aq)) and balance metals → other non-metals → H → O.
  • When asked for an observation, describe what you would see (bubbling, solid dissolving, colour change), not the underlying chemistry.
3.1.7.1Test for Hydrogen & Carbon Dioxide
  • A "glowing splint" is the test for oxygen, not hydrogen, confusing the two is the single biggest cause of lost marks on this topic.
  • The balanced equation for the CO₂ test is Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O, the cloudiness comes from the insoluble white CaCO₃ precipitate.
  • A 2-mark "Describe" question on either test needs two separated marking points: the method/reagent and the positive observation.
  • The four GCSE gas tests (hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, chlorine) should be revised together as a comparison table, confusing the reagents loses marks.
3.1.8.1Core Practical Preparing Copper Sulfate
  • Heat the filtrate gently to concentrate it, then stop heating before the solution evaporates to dryness, this is the single most-penalised point in Edexcel exam reports.
  • Dry the crystals between filter paper or in a warm place, do not dry them in an oven, as this can drive off the water of crystallisation.
  • In the examiner feedback, students lost marks for saying "evaporate all the water" and for omitting the slow cooling stage, always include both "concentrate, do not evaporate to dryness" and "leave to cool and crystallise" in your answer.
  • The command word is almost always "Describe"; full procedure questions are worth up to 6 marks (ii)) while crystallisation-only questions are worth around 2 marks ).
3.1.9.1Prepare a Salt by Titration
  • Apparatus must be named correctly: burette (delivers acid in measured drops), pipette + pipette filler (delivers a fixed volume of alkali), conical flask (holds the alkali), white tile (background for the colour change), evaporating basin (for Stage 2).
  • A suitable indicator is phenolphthalein (colourless ↔ pink) or methyl orange (red ↔ yellow). Universal indicator is not suitable because its colour change is gradual.
  • Stage 1: pipette the alkali into a conical flask, add indicator, fill the burette with acid, record the initial reading, add acid drop-by-drop while swirling until the indicator changes colour, record the final reading. The volume added is the titre.
  • The two stages must be linked in any extended-response answer, the 1F examiner feedback explicitly criticised students who described only one stage.
3.2.1.1Electrolysis
  • An electrolyte is an ionic compound that is either molten or dissolved in water, its ions must be free to move.
  • Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity because their ions are locked in fixed positions in the lattice and cannot move.
  • The anode is the positive electrode; the cathode is the negative electrode. Remember PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.
  • At the cathode, cations gain electrons (reduction). At the anode, anions lose electrons (oxidation).
3.2.2.1Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions
  • Trap from : if a question asks for the electrode reaction, write the half-equation and name the species, do not drift into a long discussion of reactivity unless the question explicitly asks why a metal is or is not discharged.
3.2.3.1Electrolysis of Molten Compounds
  • At the anode, non-metal anions lose electrons (oxidation) and form the non-metal element, often a diatomic gas (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂, O₂).
  • Molten lead bromide (PbBr₂) is the demonstration named in the specification25e, done in a fume cupboard because Br₂ vapour is toxic.
  • Inert graphite electrodes are used so the electrodes themselves do not react with the products.
  • Electrons gained at the cathode must equal electrons lost at the anode when half-equations are combined.
3.2.4.1Electrolysis & Redox
  • The number of electrons in a half-equation equals the size of the ion's charge multiplied by the coefficient, e.g. 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ uses 2 electrons because two Br⁻ ions each lose one.
  • Common cathode reductions to know: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na; 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂; Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu; Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb; Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn; Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al.
  • Common anode oxidations to know: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻; 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻; 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻; 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻.
  • Always check atoms balance first, then balance charge with electrons, and never write electrons as e²⁻ or subtract them from one side.
3.2.5.1Core Practical Electrolysis of CopperII Sulfate
  • The supply must be DC, otherwise polarity flips and nothing is deposited.
  • With inert (carbon) electrodes: copper deposits on the cathode (mass increases), oxygen gas is given off at the anode, and the solution fades from blue to colourless.
  • Measure mass by weighing each electrode dry, before and after, on a balance reading to at least 0.01 g; dry gently to avoid knocking off freshly deposited copper.
  • Common students errors flagged in examiner feedback: thinking both electrodes gain mass, confusing electrons with ions, and failing to explain why the solution stays blue with copper electrodes.
3.2.5.2Explaining the Electrolysis of CopperII Sulfate
  • The blue colour of the solution does not fade, because Cu²⁺ ions are removed at the cathode at the same rate as they are added at the anode.
  • This is fundamentally different from electrolysis with inert electrodes (graphite/platinum), where O₂ is produced at the anode, the blue colour fades, and the solution becomes acidic.
  • Less reactive impurities (Ag, Au, Pt) do not dissolve. They drop from the anode and collect as anode sludge at the bottom of the cell, this is not impure copper, and is valuable enough to be sold separately.
  • Common exam errors flagged by examiners: claiming both electrodes gain mass; calling the anode sludge "impure copper"; confusing electrons and ions; failing to explain why the solution colour stays the same.
T4: Extracting Metals & Equilibria12 topics▶
4.1.1.1Reactivity of Metals
  • The more reactive a metal, the more readily its atoms lose their outer electrons to form positive ions (cations); the reactivity series is effectively an oxidation series.
  • Metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. K, Na and Ca react vigorously with cold water; Mg reacts very slowly with cold water but rapidly with steam; Al, Zn, Fe react only with steam; Cu, Ag, Au do not react.
  • Metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen, but only for metals above hydrogen. K, Na and Ca are far too reactive to test with acid safely in a school lab; Mg, Al, Zn and Fe react at decreasing rates; Cu, Ag and Au do not react.
  • A reactivity series can be deduced by mixing each metal with each salt solution, recording observations (colour change, metal coating, temperature change, bubbles), and ranking each metal by the number of salts it can react with.
4.1.2.1Displacement Reactions & Redox
  • At Higher tier, displacement reactions must be explained as redox reactions in terms of electron transfer, not oxygen transfer.
  • Observations of Zn + CuSO₄: blue solution fades to colourless, grey zinc becomes coated with brown/pink copper, slight temperature rise, and no bubbles or gas.
  • Spectator ions (e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻) do not gain or lose electrons, never include them in a half-equation.
  • To explain a redox reaction fully, you must state both the oxidation step and the reduction step and link each one explicitly to electron loss or gain.
4.1.3.1Sources of Metals
  • An ore is a rock containing enough of a metal or metal compound to make extraction economically worthwhile, the economic test is part of the definition.
  • The reason is low reactivity: these metals do not react with oxygen, water, or other elements in the environment, even over billions of years.
  • In a "Which metal is found uncombined?" MCQ, the correct option is always the least reactive metal on the list.
4.1.3.2Extracting Metals
  • Oxidation is the gain of oxygen and reduction is the loss of oxygen (the GCSE definition used in topic 4.5).
  • The extraction of a metal from its ore is always a reduction, the metal compound loses oxygen (or gains electrons) to become the metal element.
  • Metals more reactive than carbon (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) cannot be reduced by carbon and must be extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound.
  • To explain why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis rather than carbon, always link two ideas: aluminium is more reactive than carbon, and electrolysis (though expensive) is the only viable method.
4.1.4.1Bioleaching & Phytomining
  • Phytoextraction does not have higher yield or lower cost than traditional mining, this is the most common wrong answer in exams .
  • Vague answers like "better for the environment" or "causes less pollution" do not score, you must give specific advantages such as "less landscape damage" or "lower energy demand because no smelting is required".
4.1.5.1Recycling Metals
  • Recycling reduces the amount of metal waste sent to landfill, metals do not biodegrade, so they would otherwise stay there indefinitely.
  • Examiners reject vague answers like "better for the environment", "less pollution", "quicker", "easier" and "less waste", every advantage must be qualified with a specific consequence (e.g. "less waste metal goes to landfill").
  • A 2-mark "Give two advantages…" question needs two distinctly different, fully-qualified points, pair an environmental point with an energy or resource point rather than restating the same idea twice.
4.1.6.1Life Cycle Assessment
  • There are four stages in every LCA, always in this order: obtaining the raw materials, manufacturing the product, using the product, and disposing of the product when it is no longer useful.
  • You must consider all four stages together, a product that is "green" in one stage may be far worse in another (e.g. an electric car has higher manufacturing impacts but a much lower use-stage impact than a petrol car).
  • A common exam mistake is to give only two stages, and adjacent ones at that, make sure you list all four stages in the correct order.
  • The typical command word for this topic at Foundation tier is "List", usually for 2 marks; the answer is the four stages in order with no need for further explanation.
4.1.6.2Evaluating LCA
  • An LCA evaluation question almost always gives you a data table with products in one direction and stages (raw materials, manufacture, use, disposal) in the other.
  • Always add up the totals for each product as the first step of an evaluation, a per-stage comparison alone is incomplete.
  • Look for trade-offs: one product is usually better at some stages and worse at others. Naming the trade-off explicitly with figures earns marks.
  • Every evaluation point must be backed by a specific number from the table, not a generalisation like "X is more eco-friendly".
4.2.1.1Reversible Reactions
  • Hydrated copper(II) sulfate is the classic Edexcel example: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue) ⇌ CuSO₄ (white) + 5H₂O. Heat drives the forward (dehydration) reaction; adding water drives the reverse (rehydration) reaction and the blue colour returns.
  • Ammonium chloride is another standard demonstration: NH₄Cl (s) ⇌ NH₃ (g) + HCl (g). Heating decomposes the white solid into two colourless gases, which recombine on the cooler part of the tube to deposit white solid again.
  • The direction of some reversible reactions can be changed by altering conditions, most commonly temperature (heating vs cooling) and adding or removing a reactant or product.
  • If asked to devise an experiment to show a reaction is reversible (e.g. hydrated copper(II) sulfate), describe the practical steps, heat the blue solid until it turns white, then add water and observe the blue colour return. Do not answer in terms of equilibrium theory.
4.2.1.2Dynamic Equilibrium
  • The two essential marking points on "explain what is meant by dynamic equilibrium" are: (i) equal forward and reverse rates, and (ii) closed system / constant concentrations of reactants and products.
  • Two very common errors to avoid: writing that "the reaction stops" (it does not, it is dynamic), and writing that "the concentrations are equal" (they are constant, not equal).
  • The examiner feedback warned students not to apply equilibrium principles when a question asks them to describe a laboratory experiment showing reversibility, that needs practical detail, not equilibrium theory.
  • Foundation-tier questions on this topic are often "use words from the box" gap-fill style, 2 marks); Higher-tier questions are more likely to ask for a short prose explanation.
4.2.2.1The Haber Process
  • The balanced equation is N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g), learn the formula of ammonia exactly. Examiners flag NH₂ and NH₄ as the most common wrong answers .
  • The conditions used are: temperature 450 °C, pressure 200 atm, iron catalyst, these specific values must be recalled.
  • The reversible-reaction symbol ⇌ must appear in the equation, a single arrow (→) is wrong for the Haber process.
  • The full Le Chatelier reasoning for why each condition shifts the position of equilibrium is examined at Higher tier and is covered in this topic.
4.2.3.1The Position of Equilibrium
  • To predict a pressure shift, count moles of gas on each side of the balanced equation, then apply the "fewer-moles-when-compressed" rule.
  • At higher tier, full-mark explanations of pressure effects must address both the rate of attainment of equilibrium and the shift in position (decrease in number of gas molecules), .
  • Never apply Haber-Process reasoning to a non-Haber equilibrium, different equations have different exothermic directions and different gas-mole counts; you must work the prediction from the equation given .
  • The command word is almost always "explain" and the mark allocation is typically 4–6 marks at the end of the paper, plan your answer around four or more distinct linked points.
T5: Separate Chemistry 122 topics▶
5.1.1.1Transition Metals
  • The four typical properties of transition metals (the specification1C) are: (a) high melting point, (b) high density, (c) formation of coloured compounds, (d) catalytic activity of the metals and their compounds.
  • Typical aqueous solution colours to recognise: Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ pale green, Fe³⁺ yellow/orange-brown, Cr³⁺ green, Mn²⁺ pale pink.
  • Transition metals contrast sharply with Group 1 (alkali) metals: transition metals are hard, dense, high-melting, low-reactivity, and form coloured compounds; Group 1 metals are soft, low-density, low-melting, highly reactive, and form white/colourless compounds.
  • On a "state" question about a transition metal's position, write one short statement, "it is a transition metal" or "it is in the d-block". Do not write "in the middle", name only the period, or just say "it is a metal", these scored zero in .
5.1.2.1Oxidation of Metals
  • Rusting is the specific corrosion of iron in the presence of both oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide, flaky orange-brown rust.
  • More reactive metals (Na, K, Mg) corrode faster than less reactive metals (Cu, Ag); gold and platinum essentially do not corrode, which is why gold is used in jewellery.
  • In the Higher paper, examiners insisted on specific answers, "iron is reactive" did not score; you must refer to reaction with oxygen and/or water or to corrosion/rusting.
  • The same report warned students not to invent wrong reasons, iron does conduct electricity, and claiming it does not is a common scoring trap.
5.1.2.2Rusting of Iron
  • In "explain" questions, always link the prevention method back to the rusting requirement, e.g. "the oil layer stops oxygen reaching the iron, and oxygen is needed for rusting." Examiners deduct marks when this linkage is left implicit.
5.1.2.3Electroplating
  • At the anode, atoms of the plating metal lose electrons and dissolve into the electrolyte: M (s) → Mⁿ⁺ (aq) + n e⁻.
  • In exam answers, be specific, "protects from corrosion" earns the mark; just "protects" does not; "makes it stronger" is alloying, not electroplating; "cheaper" alone is too vague.
5.1.3.1Alloys
  • An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually metals, sometimes carbon), designed to improve a specific property, most often strength or corrosion resistance.
  • When asked why an alloy is used for a job, name a specific property (doesn't rust, stronger, harder, low density) and link it to the use. Do not write "cheaper", alloys are often more expensive than the pure metal.
  • Do not cite malleability as a reason an alloy is preferred over a pure metal, alloys are usually less malleable than the pure metal.
  • Typical 5.5C exam wording is "Explain, in terms of the arrangement of metal particles, why [alloy] is stronger than pure [metal]", worth 2–3 marks, command word explain, expects the particle model in both words and (often) a diagram.
5.1.3.2Steel Alloys
  • Stainless steel is also stronger than pure iron, both properties are needed for full marks when comparing stainless steel to iron in an exam.
  • The classic Edexcel question ) asks for two reasons cutlery is made of stainless steel rather than iron: most students give "doesn't rust" but forget "stronger/harder than iron".
  • Avoid the examiner-flagged wrong answers: "cheaper" (stainless steel is usually more expensive), "not staining" (too vague, say "doesn't rust"), and any answer based on malleability (not the key skill being tested).
5.1.4.1Using Metals
  • The uses of metals are chosen because their properties match the job, every exam answer must link a specific property to a specific use and explain why the link works.
  • Gold does not corrode or tarnish (jewellery, electrical contacts), is very malleable (gold leaf), has an attractive yellow colour (decoration), and is a reliable electrical conductor in high-end electronics.
  • Brass is a copper–zinc alloy: harder than copper with a gold-like colour, used for musical instruments, door handles, screws and decorative fittings.
  • The signature exam task for this topic is a 6-mark open-response on Foundation Paper 1, give one property, one use, and one reason for each named metal, covering all metals listed in the stem.
5.2.1.1Using Concentrations in moldm3
  • The two key equations are C (g dm⁻³) = m / V and C (mol dm⁻³) = n / V, where V is always in dm³.
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³, so any volume given in cm³ must be divided by 1000 before substituting into a concentration equation.
  • Use the 5-step recipe every time: identify what's given → convert volume → calculate Mr → substitute → check precision.
  • The biggest mark-loss source flagged by examiners is failing to divide volume by 1000, followed by dividing the wrong way round when switching between g dm⁻³ and mol dm⁻³.
5.2.2.1Core Practical Acid-Alkali Titration
  • Key apparatus you must be able to name: burette (acid), pipette with pipette filler (alkali), conical flask, white tile, funnel (for filling burette only), retort stand.
  • Phenolphthalein (pink → colourless) is the standard indicator for HCl + NaOH; methyl orange (yellow → red) is used when titrating a strong acid with a weak base such as Na₂CO₃; universal indicator is not suitable because the colour change is gradual.
  • If a question covers both titration and crystallisation (e.g. preparing a soluble salt from an alkali), you must describe both halves to score top-band marks, and the second titration to prepare the salt is done without indicator.
5.2.3.1Titration Calculations
  • Volumes must be converted from cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000 before substituting into n = c × V.
  • The full procedure has seven steps: mean titre → convert volumes → moles of the known → mole ratio from the balanced equation → moles of the unknown → final c or V → correct precision.
  • When a concentration is given in g dm⁻³, divide by Mr to convert to mol dm⁻³, mass ÷ Mr, never the inverse.
  • If a question asks for the answer in cm³, remember to convert dm³ back to cm³ (× 1000) at the end.
5.2.4.1Yield
  • Theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product predicted from the balanced equation; actual yield is the mass actually obtained from the experiment.
  • % yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100, both yields must be in the same units, and the answer is a percentage with no unit.
  • Actual yield is almost always less than theoretical yield.
  • If actual yield appears greater than theoretical yield, the product is not fully dried or contains impurities, never a reason from 5.12C.
5.2.5.1Atom Economy
  • Reactions with two or more products always have atom economy less than 100%, because at least one product is not the one you want.
  • Common exam trap : writing 100% because "both products are useful". The atom-economy formula uses the desired product only, not the total mass of all products.
  • Common exam trap : writing 80 when the question asks for 1 decimal place. One decimal place means one digit after the decimal point (80.5). Always re-read the rounding instruction at the very end.
  • Always show your working. A bare incorrect final answer scores zero, but full working with a small arithmetic slip can still pick up 2–3 of the 4 marks.
5.2.6.1Choosing a Reaction Pathway
  • In exam answers, always cite the data, link to a factor, acknowledge the trade-off, and conclude with a justified choice, examiners reward weighed reasoning over a bare verdict.
5.2.7.1Molar Volume
  • Molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of molecules of any gas at room temperature and pressure: 24 dm³ or 24 000 cm³ (provided in the question, you do not need to memorise the conditions).
  • Multiply by 24 when going from moles to volume; divide by 24 when going from volume to moles. The single most common exam error is doing this the wrong way round.
  • When all species are gases at the same conditions, volume ratios equal mole ratios, you can apply the balanced equation directly to volumes ).
  • For reactions involving air, remember that air is approximately 20% O₂ by volume, so multiply the volume of O₂ by 100/20 (= 5) to obtain the volume of air needed.
5.2.8.1Avogadro's Law
  • For a gas-only reaction at constant T and p, the volume ratio is identical to the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
  • You do not need the molar volume (24 dm³ mol⁻¹) for an Avogadro's law calculation, that conversion cancels out when both sides are gases.
  • A larger coefficient on the unknown side means multiply; a smaller coefficient means divide. Reflexively dividing was flagged as a common error in the examiner feedback.
  • Edexcel places these questions at the very end of Higher tier papers, typically awarding 2–4 marks, and the command word is almost always "calculate."
5.3.1.1Predicting the Conditions
  • Energy of particles depends on temperature only. Pressure and concentration increase collision frequency, not particle energy, writing the opposite is the single most-flagged error on this topic .
  • A 6-mark "Explain" question always wants both the rate-of-attainment effect and the yield effect, each with a reason, answers that cover only one half cap at around half marks .
  • The mark-scheme template for full-marks 6-mark answers: state rate effect → reason → state yield effect → reason, for each condition the question names.
5.3.1.2Choosing the Conditions
  • Industrial conditions are chosen to give an acceptable yield in an acceptable time at an acceptable cost, never to maximise yield alone.
  • To score full marks on a 6-mark "Explain" question you must cover both rate AND yield effects, examiners repeatedly note that students under-develop the yield side.
  • Always check the equation in the question, exo- vs endothermic and the mole change in gas tell you which way Le Chatelier's principle points; never just transfer Haber reasoning.
  • Edexcel routinely tests this topic using unfamiliar reactions, practise the reasoning, don't just memorise the Haber numbers.
5.3.2.1Fertilisers
  • Fertilisers may contain nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) compounds to promote plant growth (the specification22C).
  • Fertiliser salts must be soluble in water so plant roots can absorb the dissolved ions.
  • Ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate: NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃ (the specification23C). This is the main fertiliser salt for this topic.
  • When asked to suggest why ammonium salts are used as fertilisers, the marking point is that they are soluble in water so the NH₄⁺ ions can be absorbed through plant roots and provide nitrogen for growth, do not just write "it is a fertiliser".
5.3.2.2Making Fertilisers
  • Lab method: pipette 25 cm³ dilute H₂SO₄ into a flask, add indicator (methyl orange or phenolphthalein), titrate dilute ammonia from a burette until the colour changes, record the volume, then repeat without indicator using that volume, the second run gives a pure salt solution.
  • Similarities to remember: same reactants, same product, same neutralisation reaction.
  • Differences to remember: lab is small / batch; industrial is massive / continuous; lab buys in raw materials, industrial makes them on site; lab uses an indicator, industrial uses pH electrodes; per-gram cost is high in the lab and low industrially.
  • If a 2-mark question asks for "one similarity AND one difference", give one of each, examiners credit only one of each type. Many students lost this mark in .
5.4.1.1Electrochemical Cells
  • Half-equations for cells follow the same rules as for electrolysis, the electrons must balance and a half-equation must be a balanced equation, not words or state symbols alone.
  • Fuel cells differ from chemical cells because their reactants are supplied continuously from outside, so they do not go flat in the same way, they keep producing electricity as long as fuel flows.
  • Common commercial chemical cells include alkaline batteries (Zn/MnO₂), silver-oxide button cells, lead-acid car batteries and lithium-ion batteries.
  • The most common exam command word for higher-mark allocation questions on this topic is evaluate, usually comparing chemical cells with hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells, 6 marks).
5.4.2.1Fuel Cells
  • Water is the only product. This is the single most heavily examined fact for 5.26C and the most commonly mis-recalled, examiners flagged "hydroxide", "oxygen" and "hydrogen" as frequent wrong answers in .
  • On, expect a 1-mark recall question ).
  • On, expect questions worth 2–6 marks including writing balanced equations and half-equations; the examiner feedback flagged that many Higher students left these blank or wrote prose instead of equations, practise writing them as actual equations.
5.4.2.2Evaluating Fuel Cells
  • 5.27C is an "evaluate" topic, you must write strengths, weaknesses, and a justified conclusion, not a one-sided list.
  • The spacecraft question is the canonical 6-mark evaluation: two or three advantages, two or three disadvantages, and a conclusion.
  • The examiner feedback flags a common error, students answer "why hydrogen is a better fuel than hydrocarbons" instead of evaluating the fuel-cell device versus chemical cells. Read the question carefully.
T6: Groups in the Periodic Table15 topics▶
6.1.1.1Group Classification
  • Exam questions on this topic are usually 1-mark recall items on Foundation Paper 2; the marks are awarded for the exact group name, so avoid vague answers like "gases" or wrong terms like "halides".
6.1.1.2Physical Properties in Group 1
  • In Edexcel Paper 2, this content most often appears as a 1-mark MCQ with a "Which row…" stem, asking you to pick the row that correctly lists two Group 1 properties.
  • If you're asked to state a property of Group 1, default to soft or relatively low melting point, these are the spec-named pair and will always score.
6.1.1.3How Alkali Metals React with Water
  • Potassium reacts very vigorously, melts, whizzes, always ignites the hydrogen with a lilac flame, may pop or spit.
  • When describing observations, give one distinct observation per mark, synonyms like fizzing and effervescence only score once .
  • Common 2-mark "describe" questions and 6-mark extended-response questions on Group 1 with water both appear on Paper 2 .
  • Practise writing the balanced symbol equation with state symbols for each of Li, Na and K reacting with water, these are commonly examined directly.
6.1.1.4Trends in Group 1
  • The three metals named in the spec are lithium, sodium and potassium, you must know how each reacts with water.
  • In "describe what you would see" questions, two synonyms (e.g. fizzing and effervescence) only score one mark, always include observations from different categories (gas, movement, ignition, fate of metal, solution).
  • Typical mark allocation: 2 marks for predicting a single metal's behaviour, up to 6 marks for an extended question covering all of Li → Cs.
  • The most-tested command words here are describe and predict, practise writing both styles of answer.
6.1.2.1Group 1 Reactivity & Electronic Configurations
  • To score full marks, refer specifically to the outer electron, not "electrons" in general.
  • A typical 3-mark answer compares shell counts, links to distance/shielding, and concludes that the outer electron is lost more easily.
  • This is the mirror-image idea to Group 7, where reactivity decreases down the group because atoms there gain an electron rather than lose one.
6.2.1.1Physical Properties in Group 7
  • The halogens are Group 7 of the periodic table; the three you must recall by colour and state are chlorine, bromine and iodine.
  • Colour gets darker down the group: pale yellow fluorine → yellow-green chlorine → red-brown bromine → grey-black iodine → very dark astatine.
  • Mark schemes are strict on colours: vague answers like "dark" or "coloured" do not score; use the specific colour words yellow-green, red-brown, grey-black, purple vapour.
6.2.1.2Testing Chlorine
  • The bleached white observation is the part of the answer that proves the gas is specifically chlorine, turning red alone is not enough.
  • The paper must be damp because chlorine has to dissolve in water on the paper to form hydrochloric acid (HCl) and chloric(I) acid (HOCl); the HOCl is the bleaching agent.
  • Chlorine is the diatomic gas Cl₂; chloride is the dissolved Cl⁻ ion. They have completely different tests, never use silver nitrate to test for chlorine gas, and never use damp litmus to test for chloride ions.
  • Do not confuse the chlorine test with the carbon dioxide test (limewater turning milky), this confusion was specifically called out in the examiner feedback.
6.2.2.1Reactions with Metals
  • Halogens exist as diatomic molecules (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂), so symbol equations always need balancing for the X₂ unit.
  • Typical observations include a bright flame, white or coloured smoke, and a solid metal halide product; the coloured halogen gas or liquid is used up.
  • At Foundation tier you are most likely to see word equations and short recall ("State" / "Write") questions worth 1–2 marks; at Higher tier you should expect balanced symbol equations with state symbols and prediction tasks.
6.2.2.2Hydrogen Halides
  • Hydrogen and all the halogens (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂, F₂) exist as diatomic molecules, always write them with a subscript 2 in equations.
  • The balanced equations are H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr and H₂ + I₂ → 2HI.
  • Hydrogen halides are colourless gases at room temperature and dissolve readily in water.
  • The solution formed is not a hydroxide, "bromine hydroxide" is a common wrong answer; the correct name is hydrobromic acid.
6.2.2.3Displacement Reactions
  • The halide solutions used in the test (KCl, KBr, KI) are all colourless before the halogen is added; any colour at the end of the experiment comes from the halogen that has been displaced.
  • Chlorine is pale green/yellow, bromine is orange, and iodine in solution is brown, learn these colours, because identifying the displaced halogen by colour is how you earn observation marks.
  • Potassium ions are spectator ions, they do not displace anything, and writing that "potassium displaced the halogen" is a marked error in examiner feedback.
  • Iodine is the least reactive of Cl/Br/I, not the most, even though it gives the most strongly coloured (brown) solutions; the brown colour means iodine was produced, so it was the loser in the reaction.
6.2.3.1Halogen Displacement & Redox
  • Electrons must balance: the number lost in oxidation = the number gained in reduction.
  • Examiners reward answers written from practical experience of the displacement reactions; do not just copy descriptive details from the question stem.
6.2.4.1Group 7 Reactivity & Electronic Configurations
  • The electronic configurations you must know are F = 2,7; Cl = 2,8,7; Br = 2,8,18,7; I = 2,8,18,18,7.
  • The classic exam mistake is inverting the reasoning and treating Group 7 like Group 1; remember that gaining and losing electrons respond to shielding in opposite ways.
  • Edexcel often asks this as an "Explain" question worth up to 6 marks, sometimes combined with Group 1, and expects you to state the configurations, mention gaining an electron, and refer to distance from the nucleus and shielding in your answer.
6.3.1.1Group 0 (Noble Gases)
  • Noble gases are chemically inert: they do not normally react with any other element.
  • Because chemical reactions involve electron transfer or sharing, noble gases do not react.
  • For a 2-mark "explain" question, both marks are usually: (i) full outer shell, and (ii) does not need to lose / gain / share electrons (so does not react).
  • The command word state typically asks for a 1-mark recall; explain typically asks for a 2-mark reasoned answer linking electrons to inertness.
6.3.1.2Using Noble Gases
  • The noble gases are inert because they have a full outer electron shell, so they do not need to gain, lose or share electrons.
  • For an "Explain why…" question, always give both the property and the consequence, for example, "argon is inert, so it does not react with the hot filament".
  • Common mark losses come from naming the property without linking it to the use, or from confusing inert (does not react) with non-flammable (does not burn).
  • On Paper 2 this topic usually appears as a 1- or 2-mark short-answer question, keep your responses concise and explicit about the property–use link.
6.3.1.3Trends in Group 0
  • All Group 0 boiling points are negative °C, never quote a positive boiling point for a noble gas at GCSE.
  • For a "suggest" question, any value strictly between the two neighbouring values, with the correct unit, will usually score the mark.
  • The skill is flagged Maths 1d (orders of magnitude) and 2c (extracting information from tables), common on Paper 2, 1 mark each.
  • The canonical exam pattern is : given a partial table of noble-gas densities, suggest the density of krypton (1 mark).
T7: Rates of Reaction & Energy Changes14 topics▶
7.1.1.1Determining the Rate of a Reaction
  • Choose the measurement method to match the observable change: gas produced, precipitate formed, colour change, or mass loss.
  • Apparatus diagrams must show a sealed pathway from reaction flask to collection vessel, missing the bung is a very common mark-losing error.
  • Always convert time to seconds before substituting into the rate formula, and always quote the unit (cm³/s, g/s, or mol/dm³/s).
  • Colorimetry can be used to follow reactions where a coloured species appears or disappears, removing the subjectivity of "by eye" judgements.
7.1.1.2Core Practical Investigating Rate of Reaction
  • Method (a) needs a conical flask with a bung and delivery tube leading to a gas syringe (preferred) or an inverted measuring cylinder over water, without the bung, the gas escapes and no marks are awarded.
  • The independent variable is what you change, the dependent variable is what you measure, and control variables (volume of acid, mass of marble chips, observer) must be kept constant for a fair test.
  • Sulfur dioxide produced in method (b) is toxic, keep the room well ventilated and do not heat above 60 °C.
  • The biggest source of error in method (a) is gas escape before the bung is replaced; in method (b) it is the subjective endpoint, fixed by using a light sensor / data logger.
7.1.2.1Collision Theory
  • Activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum energy colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur; it is fixed for a given reaction.
  • Concentration, pressure and surface area change only the frequency, not the energy, of collisions. Mixing these up is the classic mark-losing mistake.
  • In "explain" answers, always use the words particles, collide / collisions, frequency and, where relevant, energy, examiners flag the absence of this vocabulary.
7.1.2.2Explaining Rates
  • Three of the four named factors, concentration, pressure, surface area to volume ratio, act only on frequency of collisions. They do not change the energy of the particles.
  • Always include the words "particles" and "frequency of collisions" in an explain-in-terms-of-particles answer, the examiner feedback flagged that very few students did this.
  • The canonical examiner trap is muddling frequency with energy of collisions. For concentration, pressure or surface area, do not write that "the particles have more energy".
  • Read the question stem carefully, if it asks about mass loss, volume of gas, or another quantity, do not default to writing about rate of reaction.
7.1.3.1Rate Graphs
  • A tangent must touch the curve at one point only, never cross it, and never be too short to read accurately.
  • The Foundation report flagged that many students lose marks by failing to level off, or levelling off at the wrong height, when sketching comparison curves.
  • Units of rate always come from the axes: cm³/s, cm³/min, g/s, or mol/dm³/s. Check the axis labels every time.
  • Typical exam mark allocation is 2–3 marks: one for reading the graph, one for the calculation, one for the final answer with units.
7.1.4.1Catalysts
  • A catalyst is not a reactant, it does not appear in the overall balanced equation and is often written above the arrow.
  • Catalysts do not change the amount of product made or the type of products; they only change how fast you get there.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts (usually proteins) that catalyse reactions in living organisms; yeast fermentation is a common GCSE example.
  • For a 1-mark recall question on the effect of a catalyst, "speeds up / increases the rate of reaction" is enough; for explanation marks, mention activation energy explicitly.
7.1.5.1Enzymes
  • An enzyme is a biological catalyst, a protein made by living cells that speeds up a specific reaction without being used up.
  • Enzymes obey the full catalyst definition: they lower the activation energy, are not consumed, and do not change the products.
  • The yeast is the source of the enzymes, saying "yeast catalyses the reaction" is loose; the chemistry mark is for identifying the enzymes within the yeast.
  • Temperature must be high enough for a reasonable rate (collisions, energy above E_a) but low enough that the enzyme is not denatured.
7.2.1.1Heat Energy & Temperature changes
  • Neutralisation, displacement and precipitation reactions are almost always exothermic in GCSE contexts, the solution gets warmer.
  • The most common examiner-flagged mistake is mixing up the terms exothermic and endothermic, especially saying that "energy is taken in" when the temperature has risen, avoid that phrasing entirely .
  • State your answer in terms of heat flow to or from the surroundings (the water of the solution), not vaguely "the reaction got hot".
  • The water in the solution is the thermometer's reference, its high specific heat capacity is what makes the temperature change a reliable proxy for the reaction's heat change.
7.2.1.2Exothermic Reactions
  • The most common GCSE examples are combustion, neutralisation, reactive metal + acid, reactive metal + water, displacement and respiration.
  • A common student misconception, flagged repeatedly by examiners, is to think that energy being "taken in" causes a temperature rise, the opposite is true: a temperature rise means energy is being given out by the reaction.
  • To score the full 2 marks on an "explain" question, link three things: the observation (e.g. temperature rose by X °C), the direction of heat transfer (from reaction to surroundings), and the classification (exothermic).
  • Exothermic-reaction questions appear mid-paper on Paper 2, with mark allocation of 1 mark for naming and 2 marks for justifying.
7.2.1.3Endothermic Reactions
  • Common endothermic examples to recognise: thermal decomposition of metal carbonates, dissolving ammonium nitrate (cold packs), citric acid + sodium hydrogen carbonate, and photosynthesis.
  • For a 2-mark "explain" question, you need both a temperature reference ("the temperature decreased…") and a classification with reason ("…so the reaction is endothermic because heat is taken in").
  • The dominant exam error flagged in the Foundation report is confusing endothermic with exothermic, students recognise the temperature change but then attach the wrong label. Memorise the pair as one unit: endothermic = in = colder.
  • Typical mark allocation are 1 mark to name the reaction type and 2 marks to fully justify the classification using the data and energy flow.
7.2.2.1Bonds & Energy Changes
  • The key skill being tested in "explain" questions is comparing the two energy totals, not just describing both processes, examiners explicitly note this is where most students lose the third mark.
  • Use precise terminology: energy absorbed, energy released, bond breaking, bond forming, exothermic, endothermic. Vague phrases like "uses up energy" lose marks.
  • A typical Higher-tier "explain" question on this topic is worth 3 marks and rewards one mark for bond breaking being endothermic, one for bond making being exothermic, and one for the comparison.
7.2.3.1Bond Energy Calculations
  • Always draw out the displayed (structural) formulae before counting bonds, molecular formulae hide individual bonds.
  • Count every bond, multiplying by the number of moles of that molecule in the balanced equation (e.g. 2H₂O contains four O–H bonds, not two).
  • The four marks on a typical Higher tier question correspond to: bonds-broken total, bonds-made total, subtraction, and correctly signed final answer.
  • Bond energies in data tables are average values, so a calculated ΔH will be close to, but not identical to, the experimental value.
7.2.4.1Reaction Profiles
  • The activation energy (Eₐ) is always measured from the reactants line up to the top of the curve, never from the products line, this is the most common error flagged by examiners.
  • A reaction profile must be drawn as a curve with a peak, not a straight line, a straight line makes it impossible to mark in the activation energy.
  • ΔH = energy of products − energy of reactants; if numerical values are given on the y-axis, you must be able to read them off and subtract.
  • Reaction profiles are a Paper 2 topic, typically worth 2–3 marks per question, with command words "draw and label" or "use words from the box".
7.2.4.2Activation Energy
  • Activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum amount of energy that colliding reactant particles must have for a reaction to occur.
  • For both exothermic and endothermic reactions, Eₐ is always positive and is always drawn from the reactant level up to the peak.
  • The most common examiner-flagged error is measuring Eₐ from the wrong starting line, always start the Eₐ arrow on the reactant energy level.
  • Drawing a straight line from reactants to products instead of a curve with a peak means there is no transition state shown, and the activation-energy mark cannot be awarded.
T8: Fuels & Earth Science20 topics▶
8.1.1.1Crude Oil
  • A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only the elements carbon and hydrogen, the word "only" is what earns the mark.
  • If asked for two uses of crude oil, give one fuel and one petrochemical product, two fuels will usually score only one mark.
  • Common exam errors flagged by examiners include calling diesel "petrol for cars" and being imprecise about which fraction goes with which use (kerosene → aircraft is the safest pairing).
  • This topic sits on Paper 2 at both tiers and underpins the rest of Section 8, fractional distillation, combustion, alkanes and cracking all build on the hydrocarbon definition.
8.1.2.1Fractional Distillation of Crude Oil
  • Crude oil is a mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons with very different chain lengths, and it must be separated to be useful.
  • Bitumen, the longest-chain fraction, never evaporates and is tapped off as a thick liquid from the bottom.
  • Common exam tasks include labelling a column diagram, sequencing fractions by boiling point or chain length, and a 2–4 mark "describe and explain" of the process.
  • The most common students error in this area is mixing up which fraction does which job, be specific: kerosene for aircraft, diesel for cars/lorries, petrol for cars. Do not write "petrol for cars" as a use of diesel.
8.1.2.2The Main Fractions
  • Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation into six main fractions you must be able to name and pair with their uses.
  • The classic exam slip is describing diesel oil as "petrol for cars", the 2019 Foundation examiner feedback flagged this as the most common error.
  • Recall of fractions and uses is examined on almost every Paper 2, usually as a 1–2 mark "state the use" question or a matching MCQ.
8.1.2.3Trends in Properties
  • In a "compare two fractions" question, use both answer lines, one for each fraction, and do not repeat the same idea on both lines.
  • When working from a graph, check axis labels and units before reading values; be careful when extrapolating past the plotted range.
  • Mark allocation are usually 2 marks per trend explained; expect this topic on Paper 2 of either Combined Science or Separate Chemistry.
8.1.3.1Homologous Series
  • In a 2-mark "Explain…" question on Paper 2, the marks are usually 1 for same general formula and 1 for differ by CH₂ (or equivalent paired reasoning).
  • The examiner feedback flagged that students lose marks by repeating the molecular formulae from the stem instead of comparing them, make the comparison explicit.
  • Use the general formula to deduce unfamiliar molecular formulae rather than memorising them: for the nth alkane, hydrogens = 2n + 2.
8.1.4.1Complete Combustion
  • Always write oxygen as O₂, never as a single O atom (this loses marks in symbol-equation questions).
  • In a word equation, the reactant is oxygen, never "air".
  • Do not mix words and formulae in the same equation, pick one or the other.
  • The "describe complete combustion" answer must include all three points: CO₂ formed, H₂O formed, energy released.
8.1.4.2Incomplete Combustion
  • Balanced equations for incomplete combustion follow the same rules as any other equation, balance C first, then H, then O₂ last. Oxygen is always O₂, never O.
  • CO is colourless and odourless, so it gives no warning, hence CO alarms in homes with gas boilers and wood burners.
  • A clean blue flame indicates complete combustion; a yellow, smoky flame indicates incomplete combustion (the yellow colour is glowing soot particles).
  • The standard 2-mark "CO toxicity" answer needs two points: (i) CO binds to haemoglobin (in place of oxygen), and (ii) less oxygen is carried by the blood to the body's tissues.
8.1.5.1Formation of Sulfur Dioxide
  • SO₂ comes from impurities in the fuel, whereas NOₓ comes from the nitrogen and oxygen in the air reacting at the high temperatures inside an engine, do not mix these up in an extended answer.
  • A short exam answer should mention the source (sulfur impurities in the fuel) and the product (sulfur dioxide); for 2 marks, add the equation or the words "reacts with oxygen on combustion".
  • In extended 6-mark "discuss" questions on the environmental impact of fuels, link SO₂ explicitly to acid rain and pair each pollutant with its consequence (CO₂ → global warming, CO → toxic, particulates → respiratory damage, NOₓ → acid rain).
  • The topic is examined on Paper 2 and on Combined Science (1SC0) Paper 2. Foundation and Higher students need to know it.
8.1.5.2Acid Rain
  • In Edexcel extended-answer questions on acid rain, full marks require formation chemistry + at least three distinct harms with chemistry-based explanations, vague statements about "damaging the environment" will not score.
  • Acid rain is examined on Paper 2 (Sections 8/9), typically as an extended 6-mark "Explain" or "Discuss" question, and is frequently set alongside other combustion pollutants (CO₂, CO).
8.1.5.3Formation of Nitrogen Oxides
  • At normal atmospheric temperatures, N₂ and O₂ do not react because the N≡N triple bond is very strong, giving the reaction a high activation energy.
  • A full-credit "Explain" answer must link the high temperature in the engine to overcoming the activation energy for N₂ and O₂ to react.
  • In extended 6-mark "Discuss" questions on vehicle emissions, you must name NOₓ as a specific pollutant and link it to acid rain, not to global warming (that is CO₂) or to toxicity (that is CO).
  • This topic is examined on Paper 2, typically as a 2-mark "Explain" question or as one strand of a 6-mark "Discuss" question on vehicle emissions.
8.1.6.1Hydrogen as a Fuel
  • Hydrogen does not occur naturally as H₂ on Earth and must be manufactured, usually by electrolysis of water or by steam reforming of methane.
  • Hydrogen must be stored compressed to very high pressure or cooled to −253 °C as a liquid; petrol is a liquid at room temperature and stores easily.
  • The specification uses the command word evaluate, you must give advantages, disadvantages and a comparative judgement, not a one-sided list.
  • On and 1SC0, this topic is examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
8.1.6.2Fossil Fuels
  • The most common Paper 2 questions on this topic are 1-mark "State" or "Identify" parts asking you to match a fuel to its source, or to give an example of a fossil fuel.
  • Use the exact spec-point names, petrol, kerosene, diesel oil, methane, to avoid losing easy marks.
8.1.7.1Cracking
  • The reactant in cracking is always a saturated alkane (general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂); the products are a shorter alkane plus one or more alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ), and sometimes hydrogen.
  • Alkanes and alkenes can be distinguished using bromine water, alkenes decolourise the orange solution, alkanes do not.
  • Mass is conserved in cracking: the total mass of products equals the mass of the reactant. Use moles = mass ÷ Mr and the molar ratio from the balanced equation to calculate product masses.
  • Keep cracking, fractional distillation and combustion clearly separated, examiners noted in that students confused cracking with the products of combustion.
8.2.1.1Earth's Early Atmosphere
  • When asked to suggest features of the early atmosphere from data, always link your answer to the evidence provided in the question.
  • Do not confuse the early atmosphere (CO₂-rich, no oxygen) with today's atmosphere (78% N₂, 21% O₂, 0.04% CO₂).
8.2.1.2How Carbon Dioxide Decreased
  • A full-mark "Explain how CO₂ decreased" answer must include cooling → oceans form → CO₂ dissolves → carbonates/fossil fuels form. Stopping at "CO₂ dissolved" is the most common partial-credit answer.
  • This topic is frequently combined with (oxygen increase by photosynthesis) in 6-mark extended-response questions on the evolution of the atmosphere.
8.2.1.3How Oxygen Increased
  • The balanced symbol equation is: 6CO₂ (g) + 6H₂O (l) → C₆H₁₂O₆ (aq) + 6O₂ (g).
  • The same reaction that releases O₂ also removes CO₂, both changes happen together, so a full answer must mention both gases.
  • Explain questions are typically worth 4–6 marks; a Level 3 answer must link photosynthesis to both the rise in O₂ and the fall in CO₂.
  • On 6-mark data-table questions, identify the gas trends from the data first, then explain each one using photosynthesis.
8.2.1.4The Test for Oxygen
  • Both halves, apparatus (glowing splint) and observation (relights), are required for full marks on a 2-mark "Describe" question.
  • Do not confuse the oxygen test with the hydrogen test: hydrogen uses a lit splint and gives a squeaky pop, not a relight.
  • Examiners at Foundation tier have flagged that the majority of students were unable to recall the test for oxygen in, treat this as easy marks only if you have memorised it precisely.
  • Use the exact word "relights" (or "rekindles"), vague descriptions such as "burns brighter" or "catches fire" may not score.
8.2.2.1Greenhouse Gases
  • The three greenhouse gases you must know are carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and water vapour (H₂O), all named in topic 8.24.
  • Nitrogen and oxygen, the most abundant gases in the atmosphere, are not greenhouse gases, because their diatomic molecules do not absorb infrared at the relevant wavelengths.
  • The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary: without it, average surface temperatures would be far below 0 °C. Do not confuse it with the enhanced greenhouse effect (human-caused).
  • A correct exam answer should always include: Sun → Earth absorbs → Earth re-radiates as infrared → greenhouse gases absorb → greenhouse gases re-emit back to Earth → Earth kept warm.
8.2.3.1Evaluating the Evidence
  • The Mauna Loa record (since 1958) is the gold-standard direct measurement of atmospheric CO₂; its remote, high-altitude location avoids local pollution and gives a global background value.
  • For an evaluate question, give the evidence for human-caused change, acknowledge the uncertainties, and reach a justified conclusion, answers that only describe or only dismiss will not score full marks.
8.2.3.2Human Activity & Climate Change
  • For the specification26b, evaluate every mitigation strategy against three criteria, scale, risk, and environmental implications.
  • Common errors to avoid: confusing the greenhouse effect with ozone depletion, writing "global warming" when "climate change" is asked for, and ignoring methane or livestock farming when describing human sources.
  • The mitigation half of the spec is most often tested with Evaluate, Suggest or Explain command words, strong answers acknowledge trade-offs rather than presenting any single solution as a complete fix.
T9: Separate Chemistry 231 topics▶
9.1.1.1Flame Tests
  • A flame test identifies a metal cation in a solid sample by heating it in a Bunsen flame and observing the colour produced.
  • The five flame colours you must learn from the spec are: Li⁺ red, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ orange-red, Cu²⁺ blue-green.
  • The test for any ion must be unique, otherwise two different ions could give the same result and could not be distinguished.
  • Procedure: clean a nichrome or platinum wire by dipping it in concentrated HCl and heating it in a Bunsen until the flame shows no colour; then dip in solid sample and hold in a non-luminous (blue) Bunsen flame.
9.1.2.1Tests for Cations
  • Coloured precipitates: Cu²⁺ → blue, Fe²⁺ → green (turns brown on standing in air), Fe³⁺ → orange-brown.
  • White precipitates: Al³⁺ and Ca²⁺ both give white precipitates, distinguish using excess NaOH: Al(OH)₃ dissolves to give a colourless solution (it is amphoteric); Ca(OH)₂ does not dissolve.
  • For NH₄⁺ specifically: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O, and the NH₃ must escape as gas, which is why warming is essential.
  • Ammonium vs aluminium is a known examiner trap . Aluminium gives a white precipitate soluble in excess NaOH; ammonium gives no precipitate at all but releases ammonia gas.
9.1.3.1Testing for Ammonia
  • The paper must be damp because litmus only changes colour when the substance being tested can dissolve in the water film on the paper.
  • The colour change happens because NH₃ is a weak base, it dissolves in the water on the paper, forming NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ ions and raising the pH above 7.
  • Ammonia is the only common gas at GCSE that turns damp red litmus paper blue, this makes the test diagnostic.
  • In Paper 2 Higher, this content typically appears inside a 5–6 mark qualitative analysis question; the paper highlighted ammonium/aluminium confusion as a common error.
9.1.4.1Tests for Anions
  • The halide test is: acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add silver nitrate → coloured precipitate confirms the halide.
  • The silver halide colour ladder: AgCl is white, AgBr is cream, AgI is yellow, learn these descriptors exactly; "off-white" and "pale yellow" for bromide are not accepted.
  • Never use H₂SO₄ for the sulfate test (it contains sulfate) and never use HCl for the halide test (it contains chloride, false positive).
  • For the carbonate test, effervescence alone is not enough, you must include the limewater step to confirm the gas is CO₂.
9.1.5.1Core Practical Identifying Ions
  • Always use a fresh sample of the unknown solution for each wet test, adding BaCl₂ contaminates the mixture with chloride, and adding AgNO₃ would then give a false positive.
  • The exam appearance is consistently a 6-mark extended-response question on Paper 2 in which a table of test results must be interpreted to identify two or three unknown ionic substances, and are the canonical past-paper examples.
  • Justify identifications with observations, every named ion must be tied to a specific row of the table (e.g. "blue precipitate with NaOH → Cu²⁺"), and at Higher tier balanced ionic equations are expected.
  • The examiner feedback flagged that this Core Practical causes great difficulty for students despite being a Core Practical, so preparing the test results, colours and reagent choices thoroughly is a high-value revision priority.
9.1.6.1Advantages of Instrumental Methods
  • Instrumental methods of analysis use a machine to detect or measure a substance, replacing tests that rely on the human eye observing a colour, precipitate or flame.
  • The three named advantages of instrumental methods on the Edexcel GCSE specification are sensitivity, accuracy and speed, learn all three by name.
  • Accuracy: instruments give numerical, calibrated readings, so the result does not depend on the observer's judgement of colour and is reproducible.
  • Instrumental methods can resolve mixtures of ions (e.g. distinguish sodium and potassium in the same sample) where a single flame test would be masked by the brighter colour.
9.1.6.2Flame Photometry
  • The Edexcel specification states no knowledge of how the instrument works is required, you are only assessed on reading and evaluating its data.
  • Flame photometry is more accurate, more sensitive, faster, and gives a numerical concentration, whereas a flame test gives only a colour observed by eye.
  • If a question says a flame test has already been done and asks how the ions are confirmed, the answer is use a flame photometer, not "do the flame test again". This was the most common mistake on, where the most common score was 0.
  • Always quote concentrations with the correct units (g/dm³, mol/dm³ or mg/dm³, check the y-axis label) and read graph values to the nearest small division rather than rounding aggressively.
9.2.1.1Alkanes
  • A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only carbon and hydrogen atoms, no other elements. Get this definition word-perfect: the examiner feedback flagged "hydrocarbon" as the most-confused term in Section 9.
  • In a displayed formula you must draw every C–C bond and every C–H bond as a separate line. Every carbon must have exactly four bonds.
  • Why alkanes are saturated (2-mark answer): (1) only single bonds between carbon atoms, so (2) each carbon already has the maximum number of hydrogen atoms and the molecule cannot add any more.
  • Because alkanes have no C=C double bonds, they are relatively unreactive and do not decolourise bromine water (a key contrast with alkenes).
9.2.2.1Alkenes
  • A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only hydrogen and carbon atoms, the word atoms (not molecules) matters in the definition.
  • When drawing a displayed formula, every covalent bond must be shown, including both lines of the C=C, all C–C single bonds, and every C–H bond. The total number of hydrogens must equal 2n.
  • Alkenes decolourise bromine water (orange → colourless) because the C=C adds Br₂ across the double bond, this is the chemical test that distinguishes alkenes from alkanes (covered in this topic.
  • Alkanes have only single C–C bonds and are saturated and unreactive by comparison, they do not react with bromine water.
9.2.2.2Bromine & Alkenes
  • Bromine water is an orange/brown solution of bromine dissolved in water, learn this colour exactly.
  • An alkene turns bromine water from orange/brown to colourless (the bromine is used up in the addition reaction).
  • The correct word for the final solution is colourless, not "clear". Clear just means transparent; colourless means no colour at all.
  • Always state the starting colour (orange/brown) of bromine water as well as the final colour, examiners penalise answers that skip the original colour.
9.2.3.1Combustion of Hydrocarbons
  • Oxygen in an equation must always be written as O₂, never as O, writing O (atomic oxygen) was a specific source of lost marks in .
  • The products of complete combustion are only CO₂ and H₂O, not ethanoic acid or any other partially oxidised species. The ethanoic-acid misconception was flagged by examiners.
  • At Higher tier, state symbols may be required, under combustion conditions all species are gases, so (g) is normally used throughout, including for water as steam.
  • Memorise the four alkane and three alkene equations in the tables above, they are high-frequency Paper 2 content and account for the majority of combustion-balancing marks at GCSE.
9.3.1.1Polymers
  • A polymer is a substance of high average relative molecular mass made up of small repeating units, get all three parts of that definition into any "what is a polymer" answer.
  • The repeating unit is written inside square brackets with an n outside the bracket to show that it repeats n times, dropping the n loses marks .
  • The word "average" appears in the definition because real polymer samples contain chains of slightly different lengths.
  • Polymers are single substances, not mixtures, the monomers have reacted, not just been mixed.
9.3.2.1Formation of Polythene
  • Inside the repeating unit the C=C of ethene has become a C–C single bond, drawing the double bond still in place is a common mark-loser.
  • Conditions and mechanisms are not required at GCSE, do not waste time writing about catalysts, temperatures or radicals.
  • This topic is Separate Chemistry only and is examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
  • Examiner feedback flag the missing "n" outside the bracket and missing brackets in polymer names as the most common errors, a clearly drawn diagram with all labels in place is the safest way to score.
9.3.2.2Other Addition Polymers
  • The repeating unit is always two carbons wide because it comes from opening out the single C=C bond in the monomer.
  • You must draw trailing bonds extending through the square brackets on both sides, they show where the unit links to the next one along the chain.
  • The subscript n outside the brackets is what makes the structure a polymer; missing it is a frequently penalised error .
  • Polymer names are written as poly(monomer), the brackets and any prefix (e.g. "tetra" in poly(tetrafluoroethene)) are part of the name and must not be dropped.
9.3.2.3Deducing Structures
  • The single chemical change to remember: the monomer's C=C double bond becomes a C–C single bond in the repeating unit of the polymer.
  • Be careful with polymer names, poly(tetrafluoroethene) needs the "tetra"; the 2019 Foundation Paper 2 examiner feedback flagged this as a common omission.
  • The same examiner feedback flagged students missing out the subscript n after the brackets when drawing poly(ethene), always check the n is there.
  • A fast self-check before moving on: count every H, C, Cl, F on both sides of your translation; if the totals don't match, redraw.
9.3.2.4Uses of Polymers
  • The question can be set in either direction: property → use, or use → property. Your answer must explicitly link the property to the use ("because… so that…").
  • Polymers are chemically inert because their chains are saturated, the C=C double bonds of the monomers are used up during polymerisation, so polymers do not decolourise bromine water and do not react readily with acids or alkalis at room temperature.
  • Examiners reported a common misconception that poly(ethene) "still contains many double bonds" and would react with bromine water faster than ethene, the correct answer is no colour change, because poly(ethene) is saturated.
9.3.3.1Condensation Polymers
  • This topic is Higher tier only and only examined on Paper 2 of Separate Chemistry; it does not appear in Combined Science.
  • The most common exam question is a 3-mark describe-the-differences item comparing addition and condensation polymerisation, practise three distinct comparison points to secure full marks.
9.3.4.1Problems with Polymers
  • Landfill space is finite, and non-biodegradable polymer waste accumulates rather than decomposing, taking up land and contributing to visual pollution and wildlife harm.
  • Sorting is labour-intensive, time-consuming and expensive, which is why much polymer waste still ends up in landfill or is incinerated rather than recycled.
  • In exam answers, always qualify your examples: name the specific gas (CO, HCl), name the property (non-biodegradable), and link each to a specific consequence (acid rain, toxic, greenhouse effect).
  • Vague answers like "causes pollution", "harms the environment", "releases harmful chemicals" or "causes landfill" will not score .
9.3.4.2Recycling Polymers
  • The spec command word for 9.24C is evaluate, you must give both advantages and disadvantages and reach a justified conclusion.
  • The three spec themes you must cover are economic implications, availability of starting materials, and environmental impact.
  • Vague answers do not score, the examiner feedback criticised responses like "causes pollution" or "harms the environment". Always qualify your point (e.g. "reduces landfill mass, because polymers are non-biodegradable").
  • For an "evaluate" answer, plan 3–4 advantages and 3–4 disadvantages across at least two of the three spec themes, then close with a one-sentence justified overall judgement.
9.4.1.1Alcohols
  • The four alcohols you must know are methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol, only the 1-ol isomers are assessed.
  • Every carbon makes 4 bonds, every oxygen makes 2 bonds, every hydrogen makes 1 bond, this rule lets you check any displayed structure.
  • The –OH group must be drawn as a separate O and H connected by a single line, not as a single "OH" cluster, the displayed formula requires every bond to be shown.
  • The –OH on carbon 1 rule applies to propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol, placing it in the middle of the chain is a wrong-isomer error.
9.4.2.1Core Practical Heat of Combustion of Alcohols
  • Mass burnt is the difference between the mass of the spirit burner before and after, not the total mass of the burner, confusing these is a top examiner-reported error.
  • The target is a temperature rise of 30 °C above the starting temperature, not a final reading of 30 °C on the thermometer; this is the other top examiner-reported error.
  • Safety: alcohols are highly flammable, cap the burner to extinguish, never blow out; wear goggles; use a heatproof mat; allow apparatus to cool before handling.
  • Experimental values are always lower than data-book values because of heat loss to surroundings, incomplete combustion, evaporation from the wick and warming of the calorimeter rather than the water.
9.4.3.1Carboxylic Acids
  • The four named carboxylic acids you must recall are methanoic (HCOOH), ethanoic (CH₃COOH), propanoic (C₂H₅COOH) and butanoic (C₃H₇COOH) acid.
  • The functional group of every carboxylic acid is –COOH (the carboxyl group), per the specification30C.
  • Hydrogen forms only one covalent bond, it can never be double-bonded to oxygen, so any structure showing H=O is wrong.
  • The most common Edexcel mistake is putting the C=O double bond on the wrong oxygen, the one carrying the H, instead of on the lone carbonyl oxygen.
9.4.3.2Making Ethanoic Acid
  • The symbol equation is CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O, [O] represents an unspecified oxidising agent (you do not need to recall reagents).
  • Common exam command words for this topic are "Name", "State" and "Give", usually 1-mark items on Paper 2.
  • This topic is Separate Chemistry only and is examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
  • Do not change the carbon count when answering: the oxidation product of an n-carbon primary alcohol is always the n-carbon carboxylic acid.
9.4.3.3Predicting Products
  • The relative formula mass increases by 14 each step up the series (the mass of –CH₂–); this is the fastest plausibility check on a predicted formula.
  • Complete combustion of any organic molecule in excess oxygen always gives carbon dioxide and water; balance for chain length only.
  • The Higher Paper 2 examiner feedback singled out students who named the –COOH group, named esters and sodium salts as the product types, and linked the +14 Mr pattern to trends in boiling point and density.
  • When answering "Predict and explain" questions, always name the functional group first, then give the product, then justify with the shared-reaction rule.
9.4.4.1Fermentation
  • Enzymes are proteins, and proteins denature (lose their shape and function) when heated too strongly, so high temperatures stop the reaction completely.
  • Fermentation is anaerobic, the absence of oxygen is what makes the yeast produce ethanol rather than respiring fully to CO₂ and water.
  • Compared to the hydration of ethene, fermentation uses a renewable feedstock, runs at low temperature, is a batch process, and produces an impure aqueous mixture of ethanol that must be distilled to be concentrated, see this topic.
  • The high-mark "explain why 30 °C not 80 °C" answer must contain two ideas: (a) yeast provides the enzymes, and (b) those enzymes would denature at 80 °C.
9.4.4.2Purifying the Product
  • The fractionating column must be vertical, between the flask and the condenser. Its position is the most common thing students get wrong.
  • "Pre-boiling the dilute ethanol" does not help: ethanol has the lower boiling point, so pre-boiling would lose ethanol, not water.
  • This topic is examined on Paper 2 only, Separate Chemistry only, it is not part of Combined Science (1SC0).
9.5.1.1Size of Nanoparticles
  • Above 100 nm you move into fine particles (PM2.5, up to ~2 500 nm) and coarse particles (dust, up to ~100 000 nm), which behave like bulk material rather than nano.
  • The size question is almost always at the start of Paper 2 and often feeds into a surface-area-to-volume-ratio calculation that follows.
  • For "Compare" questions, always quote two size values and state the relationship between them (e.g. "100 times larger", "two orders of magnitude bigger").
9.5.1.2Properties of Nanoparticles
  • Titanium dioxide nanoparticles are used in sunscreens because they absorb UV light from the sun while staying transparent to visible light, say "UV light", not just "light" or "sunlight" .
  • "Show that" questions only score full marks if every step of the working is written down, because the final answer is already in the question stem.
  • Expect this topic at the very beginning of Paper 2. Q1 or Q2, as either a short explanation question or a 3-mark calculation.
9.5.1.3Risks
  • Specific risks you should be able to name: penetration through the skin, passage into the bloodstream, catalysis of unexpected reactions inside the body, accumulation in cells, inhalation into the lungs, environmental release, and the novelty of the technology.
  • For full marks on an "Explain" item you must link a specific risk to a specific consequence, for example, "penetrate the skin and pass into the bloodstream where they may catalyse reactions in cells".
  • Vague answers like "harms the body" or "is bad for you" score zero, examiner feedback are explicit about this.
  • A half-formed answer such as "they can penetrate the skin" with no consequence scores only 1 of 2 marks.
9.5.2.1Ceramics, Polymers, Composites & Metals
  • The five material classes on this topic are clay ceramics, glass ceramics, polymers, composites and metals, and you must be able to compare their physical properties using a data table.
  • Composites combine a matrix (binder) with a reinforcement (fibres or particles) to give properties neither component has on its own, e.g. fibreglass (glass fibres in resin), CFRP, concrete (sand + gravel in cement), wood (cellulose fibres in lignin).
  • Hardness is highest in clay ceramics and alloys; brittleness is highest in ceramics and glass; metals and polymers deform rather than shatter.
  • In Paper 2, this topic is examined with "Compare", "State" and "Describe" command words around a data table, often as a single-mark material-selection question, cable coating).
9.5.3.1Effective Use of Materials
  • The "effective use of materials" question is a data-handling task: you are given a properties table and must pick the material whose property profile matches the demands of a stated use.
  • The two-step logic is: (a) decide which properties the job demands; (b) compare the data columns and choose the material that satisfies all the demands, not just one.
  • Full-mark answers quote specific numerical values from the data table, not just words like "high" or "low".
  • Common properties used: density, melting point, electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, hardness, tensile strength, corrosion resistance, cost.
