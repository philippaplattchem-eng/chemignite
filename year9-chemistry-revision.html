Unit 1

Chemical Reactions — Lessons 1–7

🔥 L1 Complete Combustion ▼

When fuels burn in plenty of oxygen they produce only carbon dioxide and water — shown by a blue, non-luminous flame.

Key Facts

  • Combustion is a reaction with oxygen releasing energy as heat and light.
  • Three things needed: fuel, oxygen, ignition source — the fire triangle.
  • Complete combustion requires a plentiful (excess) supply of oxygen.
  • Products: carbon dioxide and water only. Flame colour: blue, non-luminous.
  • A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen (e.g. methane CH4, propane C3H8, butane C4H10).
fuel + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Testing for the Products

✅ Quick check 1

Write the word equation for complete combustion of propane. Name both products.

propane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Products: carbon dioxide and water.
✅ Quick check 2

A student holds cobalt chloride paper near a candle flame. What do they observe and what does it confirm?

Paper turns from blue to pink. This confirms that water is a product of the combustion reaction.
💨 L2 Incomplete Combustion ▼

When oxygen is limited, combustion is incomplete. The flame turns yellow and produces toxic carbon monoxide and/or black soot instead of carbon dioxide only.

Causes and Products

With a limited oxygen supply, carbon cannot all be oxidised to CO2. Some becomes carbon monoxide (CO) or soot (C).

fuel + oxygen → carbon monoxide + water  (and/or soot)
⚠️ Carbon monoxide — a silent danger. CO is colourless and odourless — undetectable without an alarm. It binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells, preventing them from carrying oxygen. Even small amounts can be fatal.

✅ Quick check

A student closes the air hole on a Bunsen burner. The flame turns yellow and soot appears. (1) What type of combustion is this? (2) Give two reasons why carbon monoxide is particularly dangerous.

(1) Incomplete combustion — the closed air hole limits the oxygen supply.
(2) (i) CO is colourless and odourless, so it cannot be detected without a CO alarm. (ii) CO binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells, preventing them from carrying oxygen around the body.

✅ Quick check

A student only tests each jar size once. Why is this a weakness and how should they improve it?

Testing once means any random error (e.g. a draught, variation in candle size) cannot be identified. The student should repeat each jar size at least three times and calculate a mean — this reduces the effect of random errors and makes results more reliable.
⚖️ L4 Oxidation and Reduction ▼

Oxidation and reduction are always a pair — one substance gains oxygen while another loses it. A reaction where both happen is called a redox reaction.

ProcessDefinition
OxidationGain of oxygen
ReductionLoss of oxygen
RedoxA reaction in which both oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously
🔒 Key rule: Oxidation and reduction always occur together. When one substance gains oxygen, another must lose it.

How to Identify Them

  1. Find the substance that gains oxygen → it is oxidised
  2. Find the substance that loses oxygen → it is reduced

Worked Examples

copper oxide + carbon → copper + carbon dioxide
  • Carbon gains oxygen (becomes CO2) → carbon is oxidised
  • Copper oxide loses oxygen (becomes Cu) → copper oxide is reduced
lead oxide + hydrogen → lead + water
  • Hydrogen gains oxygen → oxidised
  • Lead oxide loses oxygen → reduced
✅ Quick check

Iron oxide + aluminium → aluminium oxide + iron. Which substance is oxidised and which is reduced? Explain how you know.

Aluminium is oxidised — it gains oxygen to form aluminium oxide.
Iron oxide is reduced — it loses oxygen to form iron.
🌠️ L5 Thermal Decomposition ▼

Heating some compounds breaks them apart. Metal carbonates always give a metal oxide and carbon dioxide — confirm the CO2 with limewater.

Thermal decomposition is the breaking down of a compound into simpler substances using heat. It is endothermic — continuous heat must be supplied.

metal carbonate → metal oxide + carbon dioxide
ReactantProductsObservation
Copper carbonate (green)Copper oxide (black) + CO2Green → black; limewater turns milky
Zinc carbonate (white)Zinc oxide (yellow/white) + CO2Colour change; limewater turns milky
Calcium carbonate (white)Calcium oxide (white) + CO2Little colour change; limewater turns milky
💡 Signs to look for: colour change of solid; gas produced (limewater turns milky); solid loses mass because CO2 escapes (total mass is still conserved).
✅ Quick check

(1) Write the word equation for thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate. (2) Why does the solid lose mass during this reaction?

(1) calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide
(2) CO2 is produced as a gas and escapes into the air. The mass of CO2 was part of the solid, so the solid gets lighter. Total mass is conserved — the CO2 has just left the solid.
⚡ L6 Displacement Reactions ▼

A more reactive metal can push a less reactive metal out of its compound. If the metal is lower in the reactivity series — no reaction occurs.

A displacement reaction occurs when a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in a compound.

Worked Example

Zinc in copper sulfate solution — zinc is more reactive than copper → reaction occurs.

zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper

Observations: blue solution turns colourless; pink/brown solid (copper) deposits on the zinc.

Copper + zinc sulfate: no reaction — copper is less reactive than zinc.

✅ Quick check

Magnesium is placed in iron sulfate solution. (1) Will a reaction occur? Explain. (2) Write the word equation.

(1) Yes — magnesium is above iron in the reactivity series, so it displaces iron.
(2) magnesium + iron sulfate → magnesium sulfate + iron
🏹 L7 Catalysts ▼

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up — it can be recovered unchanged at the end. Enzymes are biological catalysts.

📖 Learn this definition word for word:
A catalyst is a substance that speeds up the rate of a reaction without altering the products of the reaction, being itself unchanged chemically and in mass at the end of the reaction.

Four Key Properties

  1. Speeds up the reaction
  2. Does not change what products are formed
  3. Is not used up — recovered unchanged at the end
  4. Only a small amount is needed

Decomposition of Hydrogen Peroxide

hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen
CatalystObservation
No catalystVery slow — little or no fizzing
Manganese(IV) oxide (MnO2)Rapid fizzing; glowing splint relights
Liver / potato (contain catalase)Vigorous fizzing
💡 Test for oxygen: A glowing (not burning) splint relights in oxygen.

Industrial Examples

CatalystProcess
IronHaber process — making ammonia for fertilisers
Platinum + palladiumCatalytic converters in cars
EnzymesDigestion, fermentation, biological washing powders
✅ Quick check

A student says: "We need to buy more MnO2 after each reaction because it gets used up." Correct this statement using the definition of a catalyst.

The student is incorrect. A catalyst is unchanged chemically and in mass at the end of the reaction — it is not used up. The MnO2 could be filtered off and reused in the next experiment.

Representing Reactions — Lessons 10–16

✍️ L10 Word Equations ▼

Reactants go on the left of the arrow, products on the right. The arrow is not an equals sign — it means "reacts to form".

reactant(s) → product(s)
⚠️ Three mistakes to avoid:
  • Using = instead of →
  • Writing products on the left
  • Mixing words and formulae in the same equation (e.g. methane + O2 →…)

Key Reactions to Know

ReactionWord equation
Complete combustion of methanemethane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Incomplete combustion (CO)methane + oxygen → carbon monoxide + water
Reduction of copper oxidecopper oxide + carbon → copper + carbon dioxide
Thermal decomp. of copper carbonatecopper carbonate → copper oxide + carbon dioxide
Zinc displacing copperzinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper
Decomp. of hydrogen peroxidehydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen
✅ Quick check

Write word equations for: (1) propane burning completely in oxygen; (2) calcium carbonate being heated.

(1) propane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
(2) calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide

(1) A student writes O for oxygen gas. Why is this wrong? (2) How many atoms of each element are in CaCO3?

⚛️ L12 Ion Charges and Deducing Formulae ▼

Ionic compounds are always neutral — positive and negative charges must cancel. The swap-and-drop method uses ion charges to work out the formula automatically.

Ion Charges

Positive ions (cations)ChargeNegative ions (anions)Charge
Sodium Na++1Chloride Cl−−1
Potassium K++1Oxide O2−−2
Calcium Ca2++2Sulfate SO42−−2
Magnesium Mg2++2Carbonate CO32−−2
Aluminium Al3++3Hydroxide OH−−1

Iron(III) Fe3++3

The Swap-and-Drop Method

  1. Write the two ion symbols with their charges
  2. Swap the magnitudes only (ignore + or −) across as subscripts
  3. Simplify to the smallest whole-number ratio (e.g. Mg2O2 → MgO)
  4. Use brackets when a polyatomic ion appears more than once
IonsWorkingFormula
Mg2+ and Cl−Swap 2 and 1 → Mg1Cl2MgCl2
Al3+ and O2−Swap 3 and 2 → Al2O3Al2O3
Na+ and SO42−Swap 1 and 2 → Na2SO4Na2SO4
Ca2+ and OH−Swap 2 and 1 → brackets: OH appears twiceCa(OH)2
Mg2+ and O2−Mg2O2 → simplifyMgO
⚠️ Common mistakes:
  • Forgetting to simplify: Mg2O2 must be written MgO
  • Missing brackets: Ca(OH)2 not CaOH2
  • K2SO4 not KSO4 — two K+ needed to balance one SO42−

Use swap-and-drop to write the formulae of: (1) potassium chloride (K+ and Cl−), (2) iron(III) oxide (Fe3+ and O2−), (3) calcium hydroxide (Ca2+ and OH−).

(1) KCl
(2) Fe2O3
(3) Ca(OH)2 — brackets needed because OH− appears twice
📝 L13 Writing Symbol Equations ▼

Replace every name with its formula. Do this in two stages: get all formulae right first, then balance. Never change a subscript to balance — that changes the substance entirely.

StageWhat you do
Stage 1Replace each name with its correct formula → unbalanced symbol equation
Stage 2Add coefficients to balance (next lesson)
⚠️ Formula errors to avoid: O not O2  ·  H not H2  ·  CO3 not CO2 for carbon dioxide

Worked Examples

Methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

CH4 + O2 → CO2 + H2O  (unbalanced)

Copper carbonate → copper oxide + carbon dioxide (already balanced ✓)

CuCO3 → CuO + CO2 ✓

Zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper (already balanced ✓)

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu ✓
✅ Quick check

Write the unbalanced symbol equation for: (1) magnesium burning in oxygen; (2) hydrogen peroxide decomposing to water and oxygen.

(1) Mg + O2 → MgO  (unbalanced)
(2) H2O2 → H2O + O2  (unbalanced)
⚖️ L14 Balancing Equations ▼

Atoms can't be created or destroyed — so atom counts must match on both sides. Add numbers in front of formulae (coefficients). Never change the subscripts inside a formula.

⚖ Conservation of mass: Total mass of reactants = total mass of products. Atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed.

Rules

  1. Write the correct unbalanced symbol equation
  2. Count atoms of each element on both sides
  3. Add coefficients in front of formulae to make counts equal
  4. Never change subscripts — H2O ≠ H2O2 (that's a completely different substance)
  5. Check by counting atoms again

Worked Example — Combustion of Methane

CH4 + O2 → CO2 + H2O
ElementLeftRightAction
C11✓ balanced
H42Put 2 in front of H2O
O22+2=4Put 2 in front of O2
CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O ✓

Other balanced equations to know:

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO ✓
2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2 ✓

💧 L15 State Symbols ▼

State symbols are added after each formula to show physical state. The most important distinction is (aq) vs (l) — dissolved in water is not the same as liquid.

SymbolMeaningExample
(s)SolidCu(s), CuO(s)
(l)Pure liquidH2O(l)
(g)GasO2(g), CO2(g)
(aq)Dissolved in waterCuSO4(aq), NaCl(aq)
⚠️ (aq) does NOT mean liquid. It means dissolved in water. Water itself is H2O(l). Metal salts in solution are always (aq) — never (s).

Decision guide: dissolved in water → (aq)  ·  pure liquid → (l)  ·  gas → (g)  ·  solid → (s)

Examples

ReactionBalanced equation with state symbols
Combustion of methaneCH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)
Zinc + copper sulfateZn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)
Decomp. of copper carbonateCuCO3(s) → CuO(s) + CO2(g)
Decomp. of hydrogen peroxide2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g)
