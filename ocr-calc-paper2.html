24   Combustion Analysis — Empirical Formulae from Elemental Analysis

When an organic compound is burned completely in excess oxygen, the CO$_2$ and H$_2$O produced are collected and weighed. From these masses, the number of moles of C and H atoms can be found; any remaining mass is attributed to oxygen (or other heteroatoms).

Key Formulae

Moles of carbon$n(\text{C}) = n(\text{CO}_2) = \dfrac{m(\text{CO}_2)}{44.0}$
Moles of hydrogen$n(\text{H}) = 2\times n(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = \dfrac{2\times m(\text{H}_2\text{O})}{18.0}$
Mass of oxygen in compound$m(\text{O}) = m(\text{compound}) - m(\text{C}) - m(\text{H})$
Moles of oxygen$n(\text{O}) = \dfrac{m(\text{O})}{16.0}$
Empirical formulaFind simplest whole-number ratio C : H : O (divide by smallest)
Molecular formula$n = \dfrac{M_r(\text{compound})}{M_r(\text{empirical formula})}$, then multiply EF by $n$

Common Examiner Errors

• Forgetting to double $n(\text{H}_2\text{O})$ to get $n(\text{H})$ — each water molecule contains 2 H atoms.
• Finding mass of O by subtracting from $m(\text{CO}_2)+m(\text{H}_2\text{O})$ instead of from $m(\text{compound})$.
• Assuming the compound contains oxygen when it may contain only C and H — always check whether $m(\text{C})+m(\text{H})$ accounts for the full sample mass.

Worked Example

0.300 g of compound X is burned completely. The products are passed through anhydrous CaCl$_2$ (absorbs H$_2$O), then through NaOH(aq) (absorbs CO$_2$). The masses recorded are shown below. Given that $M_r(\text{X})=60$, find the molecular formula.

MeasurementMass / g
Mass of compound burned0.300
Mass of CO$_2$ absorbed0.440
Mass of H$_2$O absorbed0.180
Step 1 — Carbon: $n(\text{CO}_2)=0.440/44.0=0.01000$ mol $\Rightarrow$ $n(\text{C})=0.01000$ mol; $m(\text{C})=0.01000\times12.0=0.1200$ g
Step 2 — Hydrogen: $n(\text{H}_2\text{O})=0.180/18.0=0.01000$ mol $\Rightarrow$ $n(\text{H})=2\times0.01000=0.02000$ mol; $m(\text{H})=0.02000$ g
Step 3 — Oxygen: $m(\text{O})=0.300-0.1200-0.0200=0.160$ g; $n(\text{O})=0.160/16.0=0.01000$ mol
Step 4 — Ratio: C : H : O $=0.01000:0.02000:0.01000=1:2:1$ $\Rightarrow$ empirical formula $=\mathbf{CH_2O}$
Step 5 — Molecular formula: $M_r(\text{CH}_2\text{O})=12+2+16=30$; $n=60/30=2$ $\Rightarrow$ molecular formula $=\mathbf{C_2H_4O_2}$

Key Concepts

Molecular ion M$^+$$m/z$ of M$^+$ gives $M_r$ of the compound (assuming singly charged ion, $z=1$)
Base peakMost abundant fragment (tallest bar); assigned 100% relative abundance
FragmentationLoss of groups from M$^+$: $m/z(\text{fragment})=M_r-M_r(\text{group lost})$; common losses: $-15$ (CH$_3$), $-29$ (C$_2$H$_5$ or CHO), $-45$ (OC$_2$H$_5$), $-17$ (OH)
High-resolution MSGives exact $m/z$ to 4 decimal places, allowing molecular formula to be determined (see table of exact masses below)
Isotope patternsCl: M$^+$:M+2 $= 3:1$ (³⁵Cl:³⁷Cl);   Br: M$^+$:M+2 $\approx 1:1$ (⁷⁹Br:⁸¹Br)

Worked Example 4 — Relative Atomic Mass from Isotopic Abundances (M1.2)

A mass spectrum of bromine shows two peaks: $m/z = 79$ (50.7%) and $m/z = 81$ (49.3%). Calculate the relative atomic mass ($A_r$) of bromine.

Weighted mean formula: $A_r = \dfrac{(\text{isotopic mass}_1 \times \%\text{ abundance}_1) + (\text{isotopic mass}_2 \times \%\text{ abundance}_2)}{100}$
Substitute: $A_r = \dfrac{(79 \times 50.7) + (81 \times 49.3)}{100} = \dfrac{4005.3 + 3993.3}{100} = \dfrac{7998.6}{100} = \mathbf{80.0}$
Note: The % abundances must sum to 100. The answer (80.0) lies between the two isotopic masses, closer to the more abundant one.

26   Chromatography & Rf Values

Key Formula

Retention factor$R_f = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$
Range$0 \leq R_f \leq 1$ (dimensionless, no units)
GC retention timeRetention time = time taken for substance to pass through GC column; longer chain / higher $M_r$ → longer retention time

Common Examiner Errors

• Measuring the distance from the top of the plate rather than from the baseline (origin).
• Reporting $R_f > 1$ — this is impossible; recheck which distance is in the numerator.
• Confusing $R_f$ with retention time (GC) — $R_f$ applies to TLC/paper chromatography only.

Worked Example — TLC Plate

The TLC plate below shows two compounds A and B separated in the same solvent system. All measurements are from the baseline.

Solvent front distance = 200 mm

Compound A distance = 124 mm

$R_f(\text{A}) = 124/200 = \mathbf{0.62}$

Compound B distance = 76 mm

$R_f(\text{B}) = 76/200 = \mathbf{0.38}$

Since $R_f$ values differ, A and B are different compounds.

Mark Scheme

A4. $R_f$ values: $18/72=0.25$; $36/72=0.50$; $54/72=0.75$; $72/72=\mathbf{1.00}$ ✓✓; the spot at $R_f=1.00$ has traveled with the solvent front — it is non-polar / has no affinity for the stationary phase, or has not been retained at all; result should be treated with caution ✓
27   NMR — Integration Ratios & Chemical Shifts

Key Concepts

Chemical shift $\delta$Position of NMR peak relative to TMS (tetramethylsilane) at $\delta=0$ ppm; measured in ppm
Integration traceArea under each peak is proportional to the number of equivalent protons in that environment; the step heights of the integration trace give the ratio
Multiplicity (splitting)$n+1$ rule: a proton with $n$ equivalent neighbouring H gives $n+1$ lines (singlet, doublet, triplet, quartet…)
TMS referenceSi(CH$_3$)$_4$ — gives single sharp peak at $\delta=0$; inert, volatile, miscible with organic solvents

28   Back Titration & Volumetric Analysis of Organic Compounds

Key Methods

Back titration principleAdd a known, excess amount of reagent A to the analyte; titrate the remaining excess of A with standard solution B. Then: $n(\text{A reacted with analyte}) = n(\text{A added}) - n(\text{A in excess})$
When to useWhen the analyte reacts too slowly for a direct titration, or is insoluble (e.g. CaCO$_3$, aspirin in a tablet)
SaponificationEsters are hydrolysed with excess NaOH; excess NaOH titrated with HCl; moles of NaOH used in hydrolysis = moles of ester
Organic acid titrationSame method as inorganic (section 6): $n=cV$; account for polyprotic acids (citric acid = triprotic, $n_\text{H}=3$)

Common Examiner Errors

• Subtracting the wrong way round: $n(\text{reacted}) = n(\text{added}) - n(\text{excess})$ NOT the reverse.
• Forgetting that a diprotic/triprotic acid provides more than 1 mol H$^+$ per mole of acid.
• Not scaling up from aliquot to whole sample (see section 6).

Worked Example 1 — Back Titration (CaCO$_3$ in limestone)

2.52 g of an impure limestone sample is dissolved in 50.0 cm$^3$ of 1.00 mol dm$^{-3}$ HCl (excess). The excess acid is titrated with 0.500 mol dm$^{-3}$ NaOH; a mean titre of 14.40 cm$^3$ is used. Calculate the percentage of CaCO$_3$ in the limestone. ($M_r(\text{CaCO}_3)=100.1$)

Step 1 — HCl added: $n(\text{HCl added})=1.00\times(50.0/1000)=0.0500$ mol
Step 2 — Excess HCl: $n(\text{NaOH})=0.500\times(14.40/1000)=7.20\times10^{-3}$ mol; HCl:NaOH = 1:1 → $n(\text{HCl excess})=7.20\times10^{-3}$ mol
Step 3 — HCl reacted: $n(\text{HCl reacted})=0.0500-7.20\times10^{-3}=0.04280$ mol
Step 4 — CaCO$_3$: $\text{CaCO}_3+2\text{HCl}\rightarrow\text{CaCl}_2+\text{H}_2\text{O}+\text{CO}_2$; ratio 1:2 → $n(\text{CaCO}_3)=0.04280/2=0.02140$ mol; $m=0.02140\times100.1=2.142$ g
Step 5 — % purity: $\%=2.142/2.52\times100=\mathbf{85.0\%}$

Worked Example 2 — Ester Saponification (Back Titration)

0.880 g of ethyl ethanoate ($M_r=88.1$) is added to 25.0 cm$^3$ of 1.00 mol dm$^{-3}$ NaOH (excess). After complete hydrolysis, the excess NaOH requires 30.00 cm$^3$ of 0.500 mol dm$^{-3}$ HCl to neutralise. Verify the molar mass of the ester.

$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-\text{Na}^+ + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$ (1:1 ratio)
Step 1: $n(\text{NaOH added})=1.00\times0.0250=0.02500$ mol
Step 2: $n(\text{HCl})=0.500\times(30.00/1000)=0.01500$ mol = $n(\text{NaOH excess})$
Step 3: $n(\text{NaOH reacted})=0.02500-0.01500=0.01000$ mol = $n(\text{ester})$
Step 4: $M_r=m/n=0.880/0.01000=\mathbf{88.0}$ ✓ (agrees with $M_r(\text{ethyl ethanoate})=88.1$)

Q2. 0.630 g of citric acid (triprotic, $M_r=192$) is dissolved and titrated with 0.100 mol dm$^{-3}$ NaOH. Calculate the expected titre. $\text{H}_3\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}_7 + 3\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{Na}_3\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}_7 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$ [3]
Q3. Describe how a back titration would be used to determine the percentage of ethanoic acid in a sample of vinegar that also contains other acidic impurities that react with NaOH. Explain why a direct titration may still work for this purpose. [3]
Mark Scheme
A1. $n(\text{NaOH added})=0.100\times0.0250=2.50\times10^{-3}$ mol ✓; $n(\text{HCl})=0.0500\times(10.00/1000)=5.00\times10^{-4}$ mol $=n(\text{NaOH excess})$ ✓; $n(\text{NaOH reacted with aspirin})=2.50\times10^{-3}-5.00\times10^{-4}=2.00\times10^{-3}$ mol ✓; $m(\text{aspirin})=2.00\times10^{-3}\times180=0.360$ g $=360$ mg ✓; the tablet contains more aspirin than stated (360 mg vs 300 mg stated) / tablet is slightly over the stated dose ✓
A2. $n(\text{citric acid})=0.630/192=3.281\times10^{-3}$ mol ✓; $n(\text{NaOH})=3\times3.281\times10^{-3}=9.844\times10^{-3}$ mol ✓; $V(\text{NaOH})=9.844\times10^{-3}/0.100=\mathbf{98.4\ \text{cm}^3}$ ✓
A3. A back titration would not distinguish between ethanoic acid and other acidic impurities — it would measure all acid present ✓; a direct titration with NaOH similarly measures all acid, not just ethanoic acid — so neither method alone identifies the specific acid ✓; to determine ethanoic acid specifically, use GC or $^1$H NMR to identify the species, or compare with a known standard ✓
