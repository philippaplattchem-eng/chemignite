CP5 Investigating Rates of Reaction

Foundation Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Combined Science (1SC0) and Separate Chemistry (1CH0)

Paper: Paper 2

Specification: 7.1. Investigate the effects of changing the conditions of a reaction on the rates of chemical reactions by (a) measuring the production of a gas (in the reaction between hydrochloric acid and marble chips) and (b) observing a colour change (in the reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid).

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical investigates rates of reaction using two different methods. Method (a) collects the carbon dioxide gas produced when hydrochloric acid reacts with marble chips, used to study concentration and surface area. Method (b) times how long a black cross takes to disappear when sodium thiosulfate reacts with hydrochloric acid, used to study temperature. Examiner reports flag apparatus drawing and the "frequency of collisions" wording as the two areas where Foundation candidates most often lose marks.

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. Method (a): Hydrochloric Acid + Marble Chips

Apparatus for measuring the volume of carbon dioxide gas produced when hydrochloric acid reacts with marble chips (calcium carbonate). The bung is essential: without it, the gas escapes into the air.
  1. The reaction is: calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide.
  2. Carbon dioxide is the gas being measured. It is collected in a gas syringe, which records the volume in cm3.
  3. The conical flask must have a bung in the top, otherwise the gas escapes and is not collected.
  4. The volume of gas is recorded every few seconds, using a stopwatch.
  5. The volume of gas (y-axis) is plotted against time (x-axis). The curve is steep at the start, then bends, and finally levels off when the reaction has finished.
  6. This method is used to investigate the effect of concentration of acid and surface area of marble chips on the rate of reaction.

Part 2. Method (b): Sodium Thiosulfate + Hydrochloric Acid (Disappearing Cross)

Apparatus for the disappearing-cross experiment. The black cross is drawn on paper under the flask. A pale yellow precipitate of sulfur slowly clouds the mixture until the cross can no longer be seen.
  1. Sodium thiosulfate reacts with hydrochloric acid to make a pale yellow sulfur precipitate, which slowly clouds the solution.
  2. A piece of paper with a black cross on it is placed under the conical flask.
  3. The reactants are mixed and a stopwatch is started. The stopwatch is stopped when the cross can no longer be seen from above.
  4. The shorter the time, the faster the reaction.
  5. This method is used to investigate the effect of temperature on rate. A water bath warms the thiosulfate to different starting temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C).
  6. Sulfur dioxide gas is also released, so the experiment is done in a well-ventilated room.

Safety and Variables

  1. Wear safety goggles, hydrochloric acid is an irritant.
  2. Do not heat above 60 °C in the disappearing-cross experiment, because sulfur dioxide is a toxic gas and a respiratory irritant.
  3. The independent variable is what you change (e.g. concentration of acid, size of chips, or temperature).
  4. The dependent variable is what you measure (volume of gas, or time for the cross to disappear).
  5. Control variables are what you keep the same to make it a fair test (e.g. volume of acid, mass of marble chips, same observer for the cross).

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. What is meant by rate of reaction?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. How much reactant is used up or product made per unit time [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The total mass of product is the amount of product, not the rate. Rate is about how quickly that product is made, not how much.

C is incorrect. Temperature is a condition that affects the rate, not the rate itself.

D is incorrect. The energy released is the enthalpy change of the reaction (a different topic). It does not tell you anything about how fast the reaction is going.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Rate is always a "per unit time" quantity. The units of rate make this clear: g/s, cm3/s, or mol/dm3/s, all have "per second" baked in.

If a question asks you to explain why a reaction "slows down", do not just say "because reactants are used up". Add that there are now fewer particles per unit volume, so fewer collisions per second. This links your answer to rate.

Question 2. Which gas is produced from marble chips + HCl?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Carbon dioxide [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Hydrogen is produced when a metal (such as magnesium) reacts with an acid. Marble (calcium carbonate) is not a metal.

B is incorrect. Oxygen is not a product of acid + carbonate reactions. It is produced in some electrolysis experiments, not here.

D is incorrect. There is no chlorine gas given off. The chloride ions stay in solution as calcium chloride.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Marble chips are calcium carbonate (CaCO3). The general rule is: acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. The carbon dioxide is the gas you can collect, time, and graph.

Test for carbon dioxide: bubble through limewater. The limewater turns milky / cloudy.

Question 3. Why is the bung needed?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. To stop the gas escaping into the air, so all of it is collected [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The marble chips sit in the liquid at the bottom of the flask. They cannot fall out of the top.

C is incorrect. The bung does not affect the temperature of the acid. The reaction is not heated.

D is incorrect. Allowing extra air in would mix it with the gas being collected and the syringe reading would be wrong.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The 1CH0/2H June 2019 examiner report flagged this as one of the biggest mark losers in Paper 2 rate questions. Candidates who drew the apparatus without a bung lost marks because their set-up would not actually collect any gas.

When drawing apparatus, check that the path from flask to syringe is sealed: bung in place, delivery tube connected, no gaps.

Question 4. What does the shortest time tell you?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

A. The reaction is faster at higher temperatures [1 mark]

B is incorrect. Slower would mean a longer time, not a shorter one.

C is incorrect. The time clearly changes with temperature, so temperature does have an effect.

D is incorrect. The reaction has happened (the cross disappeared). Stopping would mean no precipitate is being made.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Shorter time = faster reaction. Time and rate are inversely related: as one goes up, the other goes down. A handy way to compare reactions quickly is to calculate 1 ÷ time, a bigger value means a faster rate.

Question 5. Which is the independent variable?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Size of the marble chips [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The volume of acid should be a control variable (kept the same in each repeat).

B is incorrect. The volume of gas collected is the dependent variable, what the student measures.

D is incorrect. The temperature should also be kept the same so that surface area is the only thing being changed.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Use the "I change, I measure, I control" rule:

  • I change = the independent variable
  • I measure = the dependent variable
  • I control = everything else, kept the same

Whatever the question asks you to investigate (e.g. "the effect of surface area on rate"), the named factor (surface area) is your independent variable.

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Apparatus needed to seal the flask. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The piece of apparatus is:

  • Bung / stopper [1 mark]

"Cork" or "lid" is not accepted. The standard chemistry term is "bung" (or "stopper"). A loose lid would not give a gas-tight seal.

"Delivery tube" is not accepted on its own. The delivery tube carries the gas to the syringe, but it is the bung that actually seals the flask.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The bung holds the delivery tube in place and seals the flask at the same time. Without it, the gas takes the easy escape route into the air and never reaches the syringe.

Question (b). Complete the word equation. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The completed equation is:

calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide

  • Calcium chloride [1 mark]
  • Carbon dioxide [1 mark]

"Calcium chlorine" is not accepted. The salt formed is a chloride, not chlorine.

"Carbon" alone is not accepted. The gas is carbon dioxide.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

For any acid + carbonate, learn the general equation: acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. Then work out the salt from the acid used (hydrochloric → chloride; sulfuric → sulfate; nitric → nitrate) and the metal in the carbonate (calcium here).

Question (c)(i). What does the result at 150 s show? [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one from:

  • The reaction has finished / is complete / has stopped [1 mark]
  • No more gas is being produced (the volume has stopped increasing)
  • One of the reactants has been used up

"The reaction is slow" is not accepted. The volume is not changing at all, so the reaction is not just slow, it has stopped.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

When two consecutive readings on a rate table are the same, the reaction has reached its end point (plateau). Either all the acid or all the marble has been used up, whichever one was the limiting reactant.

Question (c)(ii). Calculate the mean rate over the first 60 seconds. [2 marks]

Model Answer

To calculate the mean rate of reaction over the first 60 seconds:

  • Write down the formula:

    mean rate = volume of gas ÷ time

  • Read the volume of gas at 60 s from the table:

    volume = 42 cm3

  • Substitute the values and divide:

    mean rate = 42 ÷ 60

    mean rate = 0.70 [1 mark]

  • State the answer with the unit:

    mean rate = 0.70 cm3/s [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for the correct calculation: 42 ÷ 60 = 0.70.

1 mark for the correct unit: cm3/s (or cm3 per second).

An answer of "0.70" with no unit is not accepted for the second mark.

Answers of 0.7 cm3/s are accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Mean rate is total change ÷ total time. Take the volume of gas at the end of the interval (60 s here) and the volume at the start (0 cm3 at 0 s), subtract, and divide by the time interval.

The unit always comes from the axes: y-axis unit ÷ x-axis unit. Here that is cm3 ÷ s = cm3/s.

Question (d)(i). Effect of powdered marble chips on rate. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The effect on the rate is:

  • The rate of reaction increases / is faster / speeds up [1 mark]

"Stays the same" is not accepted. Surface area changes the rate.

"Less gas is made" is not accepted. The same mass of marble chips will make the same total volume of gas, just more quickly.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Same mass + same conditions + smaller pieces = faster rate, same total amount of product. The shape of the curve gets steeper at the start but levels off at the same height as before.

Question (d)(ii). Explain in terms of particles and collisions. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The explanation must include:

  • The powder has a larger surface area / more solid particles are exposed (to the acid) [1 mark]
  • So the frequency of collisions between the acid particles and the marble particles increases (more collisions per second), and the rate of reaction increases [1 mark]

"There is more powder" is not accepted. The question says the same mass is used, so there is the same amount.

"The particles have more energy" is not accepted. Surface area does not change the energy of the particles. Only temperature does.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report flagged that very few candidates used the words "particles" and "frequency of collisions" in their answers. These are the scoring words for this style of question. Use them.

Be careful: surface area is a frequency effect, not an energy effect. Writing that "the particles have more energy" is a classic trap that the 1CH0/2H June 2019 examiner report singled out as the difference between top and bottom of the mark band.

Question (e)(i). When to stop the stopwatch. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The student should stop the stopwatch:

  • When the cross can no longer be seen (through the solution) [1 mark]

"When the solution turns yellow" is not accepted. The cross may still be visible at this point.

"When the reaction has finished" is not accepted. The reaction carries on after the cross has disappeared, but the cross-disappearance is the agreed end point of the timing.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The disappearing-cross method uses sight to fix the end point of the timing. The cross becomes harder to see as the sulfur precipitate builds up. The end point is when the cross has just disappeared from view, looking down through the top of the flask.

Question (e)(ii). Two control variables. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two from (1 mark for each):

  • Volume of sodium thiosulfate solution
  • Concentration of sodium thiosulfate
  • Volume of hydrochloric acid
  • Concentration of hydrochloric acid
  • The same observer (same person looking at the cross)
  • The same darkness of cross / same paper
  • The same conical flask / same size of flask
  • The same viewing angle / viewing distance

[2 marks]

"Temperature" is not accepted. That is the independent variable in this experiment.

"Time" is not accepted. That is what is being measured (dependent variable).

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two safe control-variable answers are "same volume of acid" and "same concentration of thiosulfate". Both are quick to write and clearly fair-test factors.

Remember the "same observer" point, judging when the cross has disappeared is partly subjective, so one student should do all the judging to keep the measurement consistent.

Question (f). Two safety precautions. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two from (1 mark for each):

  • Wear safety goggles / eye protection (hydrochloric acid is an irritant)
  • Carry out the experiment in a well-ventilated room / use a fume cupboard (sulfur dioxide is toxic / irritant)
  • Do not heat above 60 °C (to limit sulfur dioxide release)
  • Asthmatic students should keep away (sulfur dioxide can trigger asthma)
  • Wash hands after handling the chemicals
  • Wipe up any spills of acid immediately with a damp cloth

[2 marks]

"Be careful" or vague answers are not accepted. The answer must give a specific precaution.

"Wear a lab coat" is not accepted on its own at GCSE level, it is general lab practice, not a specific precaution for this experiment.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The two safest precaution answers are "wear safety goggles" (eye protection from acid splashes) and "use a well-ventilated room" (deals with the toxic sulfur dioxide produced in this specific experiment). The second answer wins extra credit because it shows you understand the specific hazard.

Question (g). Why two students might get different times. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one from:

  • The endpoint of the experiment is subjective / each student judges the disappearance of the cross differently [1 mark]
  • The students have different eyesight
  • The students view the flask from different angles / different heights
  • The students start or stop the stopwatch at slightly different times (human reaction time)
  • The lighting in the room may have changed between trials

"They made a mistake" is not accepted. The question is asking about a known limitation of the method, not human error in general.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The disappearing-cross method relies on the human eye to decide when the cross has gone. This is what makes it cheap and easy to set up but also what limits its precision. A light sensor / colorimeter would remove the subjectivity.
