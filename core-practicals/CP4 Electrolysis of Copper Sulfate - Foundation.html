CP4 Electrolysis of Copper(II) Sulfate

Foundation Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Combined Science (1SC0) and Separate Chemistry (1CH0)

Paper: Paper 1

Specification: 3.31. Investigate the electrolysis of copper sulfate solution with inert electrodes and copper electrodes.

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical uses electricity to make chemical changes happen in copper(II) sulfate solution. The same blue solution is electrolysed twice, once with inert (carbon) electrodes and once with copper electrodes. The two setups give very different observations because the electrodes behave differently. The carbon electrodes do not react; the copper electrodes do.

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. Inert (Carbon) Electrodes

Apparatus for electrolysis with carbon (inert) electrodes. The two rods dip into the blue solution and are connected to a DC power supply.
  1. The blue solution is the electrolyte. It contains Cu2+ ions and water.
  2. The supply must be DC (direct current). One electrode is the cathode (negative), the other is the anode (positive).
  3. At the cathode (–), a layer of pink-brown copper metal forms. The cathode gains mass.
  4. At the anode (+), colourless oxygen gas is given off. Test it with a glowing splint, which relights.
  5. The blue solution gradually fades to colourless because the Cu2+ ions are being removed from the solution.

Part 2. Copper Electrodes

Apparatus for electrolysis with copper electrodes. The same blue solution is used, but the electrodes are now strips of copper metal.
  1. At the cathode (–), a layer of pink-brown copper forms, just as before. The cathode gains mass.
  2. At the anode (+), the copper electrode dissolves. The anode loses mass. No gas is produced.
  3. The blue solution stays the same colour. Cu2+ ions are added back into the solution at the anode at the same rate they are removed at the cathode.
  4. The mass gained at the cathode equals the mass lost at the anode. Copper is transferred from the anode to the cathode through the solution.
  5. This is the method used in industry to purify copper. The impure copper is the anode and the pure copper builds up at the cathode.

Safety and Method Tips

  1. Use a low-voltage DC supply (about 4–6 V). Higher voltages heat the solution and give faster gas evolution.
  2. Copper(II) sulfate solution is an irritant. Wear safety goggles. Avoid skin contact.
  3. Weigh the electrodes before and after. Rinse with distilled water and dry without rubbing (rubbing knocks off the freshly deposited copper).

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. What does electrolyte mean?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. A liquid or solution that conducts electricity because it contains ions [1 mark]

A is incorrect. This describes a metal (such as a copper wire), not an electrolyte. Metals conduct via electrons. Electrolytes conduct via ions in solution.

C is incorrect. An electrolyte is not a power source. It is the liquid in which the electrolysis takes place.

D is incorrect. The electrode that gains mass is the cathode, not the electrolyte.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

An electrolyte conducts electricity because it contains mobile ions. The ions move through the liquid and carry the charge.

For the electrolyte to work, it must be either dissolved in water (an aqueous solution like CuSO4 (aq)) or molten (like molten NaCl). Solid ionic compounds cannot conduct, because the ions are fixed in the lattice.

Question 2. What forms at the cathode with carbon electrodes?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. A layer of copper metal [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Oxygen is produced at the anode (positive electrode), not the cathode.

B is incorrect. With CuSO4 (aq), the Cu2+ ions are discharged at the cathode in preference to H+, so copper, not hydrogen, forms.

D is incorrect. No blue solid forms at the electrodes. The blue colour is from Cu2+ ions in solution.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The cathode (negative electrode) attracts positive ions. In CuSO4 (aq), the positive ions are Cu2+ (and a small amount of H+ from water). Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so the Cu2+ ions are discharged first.

The result is a layer of pink-brown copper metal coating the cathode.

Question 3. What happens to the colour with copper electrodes?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. The blue colour stays the same [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Fading happens with carbon electrodes, where Cu2+ is removed and not replaced. With copper electrodes, Cu2+ is replaced.

B is incorrect. The solution does not turn green. Copper(II) sulfate solution is always blue.

D is incorrect. The solution does not turn red. Red is the colour of copper metal (deposited on the cathode), not the solution.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The blue colour of CuSO4 (aq) comes from the Cu2+ ions. With copper electrodes, the Cu2+ concentration stays constant (deposited at cathode, replaced at anode), so the colour is unchanged.

Question 4. Mass changes with copper electrodes.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. The cathode gains mass and the anode loses mass [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Both electrodes do not gain mass. The anode loses mass because the copper of the electrode itself is being used up.

B is incorrect. Both electrodes do not lose mass. The cathode gains mass from copper being deposited on it.

D is incorrect. This is the opposite of what happens. The cathode gains the copper; the anode is the one that loses it.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The June 2019 Foundation examiner report flagged this as a common mistake. Many candidates wrongly thought both electrodes gained mass.

Remember the direction: copper moves from the anode (loses mass) to the cathode (gains mass) through the solution. The two changes are equal and opposite in size.

Question 5. Why use a DC supply?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. DC keeps the same electrode as the cathode all the time, so copper deposits steadily [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Cost is not the chemistry reason. The reason is to do with how electrolysis works.

B is incorrect. Both supplies can heat the wires slightly. Heating is not the reason for choosing DC.

D is incorrect. AC supplies are used in many chemistry experiments. They just do not work for electrolysis where you need steady deposition.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

In AC, the polarity flips back and forth (50 times per second in the UK). Each time the polarity flips, any copper deposited on the cathode would immediately start to dissolve again. With DC, the polarity is constant and copper builds up steadily.

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Name the type of electrical supply. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The type of electrical supply is:

  • DC / direct current [1 mark]

"AC" is not accepted. AC reverses polarity and would not allow copper to be deposited steadily.

"Battery" alone is not enough. A battery does supply DC, but the question is asking about the type of supply.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

DC means direct current. It comes from a battery or a DC power pack. The current flows in one direction only, so one electrode stays as the cathode throughout the experiment.

Question (b)(i). Substance at the cathode (carbon electrodes). [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The substance produced at the cathode is:

  • Copper / Cu [1 mark]

"Copper sulfate" is not accepted. Copper sulfate is the electrolyte, not the product.

"Hydrogen" is not accepted. The Cu2+ ions are discharged in preference to H+.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Copper is less reactive than hydrogen. In a copper sulfate solution, the Cu2+ ions are easier to discharge than the H+ ions from water, so copper is deposited at the cathode.

Question (b)(ii). Gas at the anode. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The gas produced at the anode is:

  • Oxygen / O2 [1 mark]

"Hydrogen" is not accepted. Hydrogen is produced at the cathode (negative electrode) in some electrolyses, not at the anode.

"Sulfur" or "sulfur dioxide" is not accepted. The sulfate ions are not discharged. Water is.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Whenever water is involved in electrolysis (any aqueous solution), oxygen is the most common gas at the anode. The OH− ions from water are discharged in preference to most other negative ions (except halides).

Question (b)(iii). Test for the gas and expected result. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The test and the result are:

  • Insert a glowing splint into the gas / test tube of the gas [1 mark]
  • The splint relights [1 mark]

"Lighted splint" is not accepted (this is the test for hydrogen, where the splint makes a squeaky pop).

"It catches fire" is not specific enough. The marker is looking for the word "relights".

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The test for oxygen is one of the standard four gas tests at GCSE. Glowing splint + relights = oxygen. Lighted splint + squeaky pop = hydrogen. Limewater + cloudy = carbon dioxide. Damp blue litmus + turns red then bleached = chlorine.

Question (c). Why does the blue colour fade with carbon electrodes? [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The blue colour fades because:

  • Cu2+ ions are removed from the solution / are deposited as copper at the cathode [1 mark]
  • The Cu2+ ions are not replaced (because the carbon electrodes do not react), so the concentration of Cu2+ in solution falls / the blue colour fades [1 mark]

"The solution turns colourless" alone is not enough. The answer must give a reason.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The blue colour of copper sulfate solution comes from the Cu2+ ions dissolved in the water. As more and more Cu2+ is deposited on the cathode, fewer remain in the solution, and the blue colour gradually fades.

Question (d)(i). Mass changes with copper electrodes. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

For the mass changes:

  • The cathode gains mass / increases in mass (a layer of copper builds up on it) [1 mark]
  • The anode loses mass / decreases in mass (the copper electrode dissolves) [1 mark]

"Both electrodes gain mass" is not accepted. The anode dissolves.

"Both electrodes lose mass" is not accepted. The cathode gains mass.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The mass gained at the cathode equals the mass lost at the anode. Copper is being transferred from one electrode to the other through the solution.

This is the principle used in industry to purify copper. The impure copper is the anode and pure copper builds up at the cathode.

Question (d)(ii). Why does the colour not change with copper electrodes? [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The colour does not change because:

  • Cu2+ ions are removed from the solution at the cathode (deposited as copper) [1 mark]
  • Cu2+ ions are added to the solution at the anode (from the dissolving copper anode), so the concentration of Cu2+ stays the same [1 mark]

"The copper electrodes do not change the solution" is not accepted. The electrodes do react. The reason the colour stays the same is that the loss and gain of Cu2+ balance out.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

This is the discriminator question for this practical. The examiner report flagged it as where most marks are lost on Foundation and Higher.

The key idea is the balance: Cu2+ leaves the solution at the cathode and Cu2+ enters the solution at the anode at the same rate, so the concentration is constant. Write both halves of the balance to score both marks.

Question (e). Calculate the change in mass of the cathode. [2 marks]

Model Answer

To calculate the change in mass of the cathode:

  • Write down the formula:

    change in mass = final mass − initial mass

  • Substitute the values:

    change in mass = 5.36 − 5.20

    change in mass = 0.16 [1 mark]

  • State the final answer:

    change in mass = +0.16 g (an increase of 0.16 g) [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for the calculation 5.36 − 5.20 = 0.16.

1 mark for the final answer with the correct unit (g) and direction (increase / + sign).

An answer of "0.16" without units is not accepted for the second mark.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

"Change in mass" is always final minus initial. A positive answer means an increase (gain in mass), and a negative answer means a decrease (loss of mass).

Always include the unit (g) and either a + sign or the word "increase" to make it clear which way the change goes.

Question (f). Two safety precautions. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two from (1 mark for each):

  • Wear safety goggles
  • Use a low-voltage DC supply (4–6 V)
  • Avoid skin contact with copper(II) sulfate solution (it is an irritant)
  • Switch off the power supply before removing the electrodes / before touching the apparatus
  • Keep flames away if testing for gases (oxygen supports combustion)
  • Wash hands after handling chemicals

[2 marks]

"Be careful" or vague answers without a specific precaution are not accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two safe answers are "wear safety goggles" (covers eye protection from splashes) and "use a low-voltage DC supply" (covers electrical safety). Both are quick to write and always relevant.
