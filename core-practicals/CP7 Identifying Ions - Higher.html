CP7 Identifying Ions in Unknown Salts

Higher Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, not assessed in Combined Science (1SC0)

Paper: Paper 2

Specification: 9.6C/9.7C. Identify the ions in unknown salts, using the tests for the specified cations and anions.

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical pulls together flame tests, NaOH cation tests, the ammonium test, and the halide / sulfate / carbonate anion tests into a systematic identification procedure for unknown ionic salts. The 6-mark extended-response question that almost always appears on Paper 2H expects you to identify the ions in each sample and justify each identification with a balanced ionic equation. The 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report flagged this practical as having "caused great difficulty for candidates", the canonical traps are choosing the wrong acid for an anion test, confusing precipitate colours (Fe2+ green vs Fe3+ brown), and failing to distinguish ammonium from aluminium when both can appear to give a white precipitate.

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. Systematic Order of Tests

  1. Flame test first, quick, non-destructive screen on a small scrape of solid. Identifies Li+, Na+, K+, Ca2+, Cu2+.
  2. NaOH on a fresh aqueous solution, identifies Al3+, Ca2+, Cu2+, Fe2+, Fe3+ from precipitate colour.
  3. Warm with NaOH, test gas with damp red litmus paper to confirm NH4+ (turns blue).
  4. Anion tests on a fresh sample of the dissolved salt: dilute HCl for carbonate / sulfate, dilute HNO3 for halides.

Part 4. Why the Acid Choice Matters

  1. Sulfate test: use HCl, never H2SO4. Sulfuric acid would itself provide SO42− ions and give a false positive.
  2. Halide test: use HNO3, never HCl (would add Cl− ions and give a false white precipitate) and never H2SO4 (concentrated H2SO4 would oxidise Br− and I−, destroying them).
  3. Why acidify at all? To remove any carbonate impurity, otherwise CO32− would form a white precipitate with both BaCl2 (BaCO3) and AgNO3 (Ag2CO3), masking the true result.

Part 5. Key Ionic Equations

  1. Cu2+ (aq) + 2OH− (aq) → Cu(OH)2 (s), blue precipitate
  2. Fe2+ (aq) + 2OH− (aq) → Fe(OH)2 (s), green precipitate
  3. Fe3+ (aq) + 3OH− (aq) → Fe(OH)3 (s), brown precipitate
  4. Ba2+ (aq) + SO42− (aq) → BaSO4 (s), white precipitate
  5. Ag+ (aq) + Cl− (aq) → AgCl (s), white precipitate
  6. NH4+ (aq) + OH− (aq) → NH3 (g) + H2O (l), ammonia released

Section A. Multiple Choice (5 marks)

1. A white solid gives a brick / orange-red flame in a flame test, and gives a white precipitate when sodium hydroxide solution is added to its aqueous solution. The white precipitate does not dissolve in excess sodium hydroxide. Which cation is present? [1 mark]

  1. Aluminium, Al3+
  2. Ammonium, NH4+
  3. Calcium, Ca2+
  4. Magnesium, Mg2+

2. Why is dilute hydrochloric acid used (and not dilute sulfuric acid) when testing for sulfate ions with barium chloride solution? [1 mark]

  1. Hydrochloric acid is more concentrated than sulfuric acid
  2. Sulfuric acid contains sulfate ions, which would give a false positive result
  3. Hydrochloric acid dissolves barium chloride more easily
  4. Sulfuric acid reacts with silver nitrate

3. Which balanced ionic equation correctly describes the formation of the precipitate when sodium hydroxide solution is added to a solution containing iron(III) ions? [1 mark]

  1. Fe2+ (aq) + 2OH− (aq) → Fe(OH)2 (s)
  2. Fe3+ (aq) + 2OH− (aq) → Fe(OH)2 (s)
  3. Fe3+ (aq) + 3OH− (aq) → Fe(OH)3 (s)
  4. Fe3+ (aq) + OH− (aq) → FeOH2+ (aq)

4. A student adds dilute nitric acid and then silver nitrate solution to a solution of an unknown salt. A pale precipitate forms. The student then adds dilute ammonia solution to the precipitate, and the precipitate dissolves. Which anion was in the original salt? [1 mark]

  1. Bromide, Br−
  2. Chloride, Cl−
  3. Iodide, I−
  4. Sulfate, SO42−

5. A student adds sodium hydroxide solution to a solution of an unknown salt. A white precipitate forms. When the mixture is warmed, no gas is released, but the white precipitate dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide. Which cation is in the salt? [1 mark]

  1. Aluminium, Al3+
  2. Ammonium, NH4+
  3. Calcium, Ca2+
  4. Magnesium, Mg2+

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. Brick-red flame + white precipitate that doesn't dissolve in excess NaOH.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Calcium, Ca2+ [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Aluminium gives no characteristic flame colour, and its white precipitate does dissolve in excess NaOH (forms a colourless aluminate solution).

B is incorrect. Ammonium gives no flame colour and no precipitate with NaOH at room temperature (only ammonia gas on warming).

D is incorrect. Magnesium gives no characteristic flame colour and its white precipitate does not dissolve in excess NaOH, but the orange-red flame in the question rules it out.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two pieces of evidence point to calcium: orange-red flame and white precipitate that does not dissolve in excess NaOH. Confirmation by two independent tests is the gold standard at Higher tier.

To distinguish calcium from aluminium when both give a white precipitate: add excess NaOH. Al(OH)3 dissolves; Ca(OH)2 does not.

Question 2. Why HCl (not H2SO4) for sulfate test.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Sulfuric acid contains sulfate ions, which would give a false positive result [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Concentration is not the issue, both acids are used dilute.

C is incorrect. Hydrochloric acid does not specifically help BaCl2 dissolve.

D is incorrect. The chemistry is unrelated to silver nitrate, this is the sulfate test, not the halide test.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Sulfuric acid is H2SO4, in solution it provides 2H+ and SO42−. If you used it to acidify the unknown, the SO42− in the acid would react with the BaCl2 in the test reagent and give a white precipitate of BaSO4, regardless of whether the unknown contained sulfate. So the test would always come back positive, even when sulfate is not present.

Question 3. Ionic equation for Fe3+ + NaOH.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Fe3+ (aq) + 3OH− (aq) → Fe(OH)3 (s) [1 mark]

A is incorrect. That is the equation for Fe2+, which gives a green precipitate, not brown.

B is incorrect. The charges do not balance: Fe3+ + 2OH− has a net +1 charge on the left and 0 on the right (since Fe(OH)2 has Fe2+, not Fe3+).

D is incorrect. The charges balance (3+ minus 1 = 2+) but Fe(OH)3 is the actual precipitate observed, not FeOH2+.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Check ionic equations balance for both atoms and charge. For Fe3+ + 3OH− → Fe(OH)3: atoms are 1 Fe, 3 O, 3 H on each side; charge is (3+) + 3(−1) = 0 on the left, 0 on the right (neutral solid).

The number of OH− ions always equals the charge on the metal cation: Fe2+ needs 2OH−, Fe3+ needs 3OH−, Al3+ needs 3OH−.

Question 4. Silver halide that dissolves in dilute ammonia.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Chloride, Cl− [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Silver bromide (AgBr, cream) dissolves only in concentrated ammonia, not dilute.

C is incorrect. Silver iodide (AgI, yellow) does not dissolve in dilute or concentrated ammonia.

D is incorrect. Sulfate is not tested with silver nitrate.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The silver halides have increasing insolubility down the group:

  • AgCl (white), dissolves in dilute ammonia
  • AgBr (cream), dissolves in concentrated ammonia
  • AgI (yellow), does not dissolve in concentrated ammonia

This ammonia confirmation step is essential when the colour is ambiguous (e.g. "very pale cream" could be Cl− or Br−) or when a mixture is suspected.

Question 5. White precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH, no gas on warming.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

A. Aluminium, Al3+ [1 mark]

B is incorrect. Ammonium gives no precipitate with NaOH at room temperature; on warming, ammonia gas is released and turns damp red litmus blue.

C is incorrect. Calcium hydroxide does not dissolve in excess NaOH.

D is incorrect. Magnesium hydroxide does not dissolve in excess NaOH.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Aluminium hydroxide is amphoteric, it reacts as a base with acids (dissolves in HCl) and as an acid with alkalis (dissolves in excess NaOH to form the aluminate ion AlO2− or [Al(OH)4]−). This is the only easy way to distinguish Al3+ from Mg2+/Ca2+ when all three give white precipitates with NaOH at first.

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Identify ions and name compounds X, Y, Z. [6 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

This is a 6-mark levelled-response question. Mark across the response, not by ion.

The full identifications:

  • X is iron(III) sulfate, Fe2(SO4)3. Evidence: brown precipitate with NaOH → Fe3+; white precipitate with acidified BaCl2 → SO42−.
  • Y is lithium ammonium sulfate (a double salt with two cations). Accept ammonium sulfate if the crimson flame is attributed to contamination rather than lithium. Evidence: crimson red flame → Li+; pungent gas turns damp red litmus blue → NH4+; white precipitate with acidified BaCl2 → SO42−.
  • Z is copper(II) iodide, CuI2. Evidence: blue precipitate with NaOH → Cu2+; yellow precipitate with acidified AgNO3 → I−.

Mark scheme (level descriptors):

  • Level 3 (5–6 marks): All three compounds correctly identified by name, with each identification justified by reference to two pieces of evidence (one for the cation, one for the anion) from Table 2.
  • Level 2 (3–4 marks): All three cations and/or anions identified, but justifications are incomplete or reference only one test per compound.
  • Level 1 (1–2 marks): Two or three ions identified correctly with minimal or no justification, or extensive listing of test results without identifying compounds.
  • Level 0: No relevant chemistry.

Iron alone (not iron(III)) caps at Level 2 because the oxidation state matters for the precipitate colour.

Copper alone is acceptable in place of copper(II) since Cu+ compounds are rare at GCSE.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The 1CH0/2H June 2024 Q8(c) and June 2019 Q8 were both 6-mark "identify the ions in unknown samples" questions, and the examiner report flagged that this Core Practical "caused great difficulty for candidates". The structure that scores top-band marks:

  1. Read the whole table before writing anything. Don't lock onto the first row.
  2. Identify each ion with a clear "observation → ion" link.
  3. Match cation + anion to give the full salt name (iron(III) sulfate, not "iron(III) and sulfate separately").
  4. Cross-check with a second test where possible, e.g. Y has both a crimson flame (Li+) and a damp-litmus-blue gas (NH4+), so it must contain both cations.

The June 2019 1CH0/2F report specifically warned about confusing Fe2+ (green) and Fe3+ (brown). Always write the oxidation state in brackets.

Question (b). Ionic equation for Fe3+ + OH−. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The balanced ionic equation with state symbols is:

Fe3+ (aq) + 3OH− (aq) → Fe(OH)3 (s)

  • Correct balanced equation (Fe3+ + 3OH− → Fe(OH)3) [1 mark]
  • All state symbols correct: (aq), (aq), (s) [1 mark]

An equation showing Fe(OH)2 instead of Fe(OH)3 scores 0, that is iron(II), which would give a green precipitate.

An equation with Fe3+ + 2OH− → ... scores 0, the charge is not balanced.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The number of hydroxide ions equals the cation charge: Fe3+ needs 3 OH−, just like Al3+. The precipitate is the neutral metal hydroxide solid: Fe(OH)3.

State symbols: ions in solution are (aq); the precipitate is (s).

Question (c). Ionic equation for NH4+ + OH−. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The balanced ionic equation with state symbols is:

NH4+ (aq) + OH− (aq) → NH3 (g) + H2O (l)

  • Correct balanced equation (NH4+ + OH− → NH3 + H2O) [1 mark]
  • All state symbols correct: (aq), (aq), (g), (l) [1 mark]

If NH3 is shown as (aq) instead of (g), no mark for state symbols, the gas being released is the observation being tested.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Ammonia must be a gas (g) here, that's the whole point of the test: NH3 is released as a gas and detected by damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube. If the reaction was done at room temperature without warming, the ammonia would mostly stay dissolved, but the gentle warming drives it out into the gas phase.

Question (d). Further test on yellow precipitate confirms the anion. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The further test confirms:

  • The precipitate is silver iodide / AgI (because it does not dissolve in either dilute or concentrated ammonia) [1 mark]
  • So the anion in compound Z is iodide / I− [1 mark]

"It is a yellow precipitate" alone is not accepted. The marker is looking for the ammonia confirmation step to rule out other halides.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

This is the discriminator step: the colour of the precipitate alone could be a "pale yellow" cream (AgBr) or a true yellow (AgI). The ammonia test settles it:

  • AgCl dissolves in dilute ammonia → chloride
  • AgBr dissolves in concentrated ammonia → bromide
  • AgI does not dissolve in concentrated ammonia → iodide
Question (e). Why dilute HNO3 (not HCl or H2SO4) for halide test. [3 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The explanation must include all three of:

  • The acid is added to remove any carbonate impurity (so that CO32− does not give a false white precipitate of Ag2CO3 with the silver nitrate) [1 mark]
  • Hydrochloric acid cannot be used because it contains chloride ions, which would react with silver nitrate to give a white precipitate of AgCl, a false positive for chloride [1 mark]
  • Sulfuric acid cannot be used because (concentrated) H2SO4 would oxidise Br− and I− ions (to bromine and iodine respectively), destroying the very ions being tested for [1 mark]

"HNO3 is the standard acid" or "it's the one we use" alone scores 0.

"H2SO4 would react with the silver nitrate" is not accepted. The issue is the oxidation of halide ions, not a reaction with the test reagent.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

This question expects a full three-way comparison: why this acid, why not each of the others. The three-mark structure:

  1. State why an acid is needed at all (to remove carbonate interference)
  2. Rule out HCl (adds Cl− → false positive)
  3. Rule out H2SO4 (oxidises Br− and I−)

This three-part justification is the Higher-tier expectation any time a question asks "why is reagent X used?" with multiple possible alternatives.
