CP4 Electrolysis of Copper(II) Sulfate

Higher Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Combined Science (1SC0) and Separate Chemistry (1CH0)

Paper: Paper 1

Specification: 3.31. Investigate the electrolysis of copper sulfate solution with inert electrodes and copper electrodes.

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical compares the electrolysis of the same electrolyte (aqueous copper(II) sulfate) using two different sets of electrodes: inert (carbon) electrodes and reactive (copper) electrodes. The contrast between the two setups is the whole point of the experiment: same solution, different electrodes, very different observations. The June 2019 and November 2021 examiner reports both flagged this practical as a weak area, especially the explanation of why the solution stays blue with copper electrodes.

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. Inert (Carbon) Electrodes

Apparatus for the electrolysis of copper(II) sulfate solution using passive (inert) carbon electrodes. The DC supply is connected so one electrode is the cathode (–) and the other is the anode (+).
  1. The electrolyte is copper(II) sulfate solution, CuSO4 (aq), which contains Cu2+ ions, SO42− ions, and water (a source of H+ and OH−).
  2. The supply must be DC (direct current), so one electrode is consistently the cathode and the other the anode. With AC the polarity reverses and nothing useful is deposited.
  3. With inert (carbon) electrodes: at the cathode (–), Cu2+ ions gain electrons and are deposited as a pink-brown layer of copper. The cathode increases in mass.
  4. At the anode (+), OH− from water is discharged in preference to SO42−, giving off colourless oxygen gas. Test with a glowing splint: it relights.
  5. The blue colour of the solution fades as electrolysis continues, because Cu2+ ions are removed from the solution at the cathode and are not replaced.

Part 2. Copper Electrodes

Apparatus for the electrolysis of copper(II) sulfate solution using active (copper) electrodes. This is the setup industry uses to purify copper.
  1. With copper electrodes: at the cathode (–), Cu2+ gains electrons and is deposited as copper. The cathode increases in mass, just as before.
  2. At the anode (+), the copper of the electrode itself is oxidised: Cu atoms lose electrons and dissolve as Cu2+ ions. The anode decreases in mass. No gas is produced.
  3. The blue colour of the solution stays the same. Cu2+ ions are added at the anode at the same rate they are removed at the cathode, so the concentration of Cu2+ in solution is constant.
  4. The mass gained at the cathode equals the mass lost at the anode. Copper is being transferred from one electrode to the other through the solution.
  5. This is the basis of industrial copper refining: impure copper is used as the anode and pure copper is used as the cathode. Pure copper deposits on the cathode; impurities fall to the bottom as anode sludge.

Variables, Maths and Errors

  1. Variables. Independent: current (or time). Dependent: change in mass of the cathode (or anode). Controls: volume and concentration of CuSO4 (aq), surface area of electrodes, distance between electrodes, temperature.
  2. Plotting mass change against current gives a straight line through the origin. The gradient is the mass deposited per amp at the fixed time used. Mass deposited is directly proportional to current.
  3. Common candidate errors flagged by examiner reports: thinking both electrodes gain mass; confusing electrons (move through the wires) with ions (move through the solution); not explaining why the solution stays blue with copper electrodes.
  4. Safety: low-voltage DC (4–6 V is enough); avoid skin contact with CuSO4 solution (irritant); dry the electrodes carefully before reweighing (without rubbing, which knocks off the freshly deposited copper).

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. Why must a DC supply be used?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. DC keeps each electrode consistently as the cathode or anode, so copper is deposited steadily [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The voltage from a DC supply is set by the equipment, not by the type of supply. The reason for choosing DC is not about voltage.

C is incorrect. Safety is not the chemistry reason. Both supplies can be dangerous at high voltages.

D is incorrect. Both supplies heat the solution slightly via the current. Heating is not the deciding factor.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

If the supply was AC, the polarity of each electrode would reverse 50 times per second. Copper deposited would immediately be re-dissolved, so no net change of mass would occur. DC keeps each electrode in one role, so deposition and dissolution can build up.

Question 2. What is produced at the anode with inert electrodes?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Oxygen gas [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Hydrogen is discharged at the cathode (negative electrode), not the anode. In this electrolyte, however, the Cu2+ ions are discharged at the cathode in preference to H+, so no hydrogen is actually produced here either.

B is incorrect. Copper is deposited at the cathode, not the anode.

D is incorrect. There are no chloride ions in copper(II) sulfate solution, so chlorine cannot be produced.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The anode attracts negative ions. In CuSO4 (aq), the choice is between OH− (from water) and SO42−. OH− is discharged in preference, giving oxygen gas.

Test for oxygen with a glowing splint: it relights.

Question 3. Why does the solution stay blue with copper electrodes?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Cu2+ ions are removed at the cathode at the same rate they are produced at the anode, so the concentration stays constant [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The copper electrodes are involved in the chemistry, not preventing it. The anode actually dissolves.

C is incorrect. There is no coating on the copper electrodes. They are pure copper.

D is incorrect. The temperature does change slightly, but the colour of the solution is determined by the Cu2+ concentration, not the temperature.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

This is the discriminator question flagged in the November 2021 examiner report. The mark is awarded for understanding the balance: Cu2+ leaves the solution at the cathode (deposited as copper), and Cu2+ enters the solution at the anode (from the dissolving copper electrode). Same rate at both, so the concentration is constant.

Question 4. Which half-equation is correct for the anode with copper electrodes?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Cu → Cu2+ + 2e− [1 mark]

A is incorrect. This is the half-equation for the cathode (reduction of Cu2+).

C is incorrect. This is the half-equation for the anode when inert electrodes are used (oxidation of water to give oxygen). It does not apply when the anode is itself copper.

D is incorrect. This half-equation is the reverse of A and shows reduction. The anode involves oxidation (loss of electrons), so this cannot be the answer.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Oxidation happens at the anode: the metal atoms lose electrons. The "OIL RIG" mnemonic helps: Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons), Reduction Is Gain.

The half-equation must balance: charge on the left (0 for Cu atom) must equal charge on the right (2+ minus the 2− from the two electrons, so 0). Both sides have 1 Cu and a net charge of 0.

Question 5. What does the straight line through the origin tell you?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Mass is directly proportional to current [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Inverse proportion would give a curve that falls towards zero as current rises, not a straight rising line.

C is incorrect. The straight line clearly shows a relationship: doubling the current doubles the mass deposited.

D is incorrect. A plateau would be a flat region. The line keeps rising in this practical, so no plateau is seen.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

A straight line through the origin is the signature of direct proportion. If you double the x-value, the y-value doubles. Here, doubling the current doubles the mass of copper deposited at the cathode in the fixed time.

This is why electrolysis is used in industrial copper refining. More current means more pure copper produced per minute.

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Two observations: one at the cathode, one in the solution. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one observation at the cathode (1 mark):

  • A pink-brown / red-brown layer of copper forms / coats the cathode
  • The cathode becomes coated in copper / gets thicker
  • The cathode increases in mass

Any one observation in the solution (1 mark):

  • The solution stays blue (the same shade as at the start)
  • The colour of the solution does not change / stays constant

[2 marks]

"The solution turns green / colourless" is not accepted with copper electrodes. (That observation would apply to inert electrodes.)

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two observations means two distinct ones. Mark them clearly in your answer: one for the cathode, one for the solution. Mixing them up loses marks.

The key thing about the copper-electrode version is that the solution stays the same blue. Make sure your observation mentions that the colour is unchanged, not just that it is blue.

Question (b). Half-equation at the cathode with state symbols. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The half-equation at the cathode is:

Cu2+ (aq) + 2e− → Cu (s)

  • Correct half-equation (Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu) [1 mark]
  • Correct state symbols: (aq) for Cu2+ and (s) for Cu [1 mark]

An equation with no electrons is not accepted. The cathode reaction is the gain of electrons.

"Cu+" in place of "Cu2+" is not accepted. Copper in this electrolyte exists as 2+ ions.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The half-equation at the cathode shows reduction: Cu2+ ions gain 2 electrons to become Cu atoms. Check that the charges balance (left: 2+ from Cu2+ minus 2 from 2e− = 0; right: 0 from neutral Cu atom).

State symbols: Cu2+ is in solution (aq), the electrons are inside the wire (no state symbol), and the deposited copper is solid (s).

Question (c). Explain in terms of ions and electrons why the copper anode decreases in mass. [3 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The explanation must include:

  • Copper atoms at the anode lose two electrons each / are oxidised [1 mark]
  • The copper atoms become Cu2+ ions, which dissolve / enter the solution [1 mark]
  • The half-equation Cu → Cu2+ + 2e− (loss of mass as the anode is consumed) [1 mark]

"The anode loses mass because electrons are removed" is not accepted. Electrons have almost no mass. The mass loss is from copper atoms leaving the solid electrode and entering the solution as ions.

The November 2021 examiner report flagged this exact misconception: candidates wrongly stated that the loss of electrons (rather than loss of atoms) causes the mass change.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The reason for the mass loss is not the electrons. Electrons are far too light to matter. The mass is lost because copper atoms leave the solid electrode and become Cu2+ ions in solution.

Structure your answer in three steps: atoms lose electrons, atoms become ions, ions dissolve. Then write the half-equation as your evidence.

Question (d)(i). Calculate the change in mass at each electrode. [2 marks]

Model Answer

To calculate the change in mass at each electrode:

  • Change in mass at the cathode = final mass − initial mass:

    Change in cathode mass = 5.78 − 5.62

    Change in cathode mass = +0.16 g [1 mark]

  • Change in mass at the anode = final mass − initial mass:

    Change in anode mass = 5.39 − 5.55

    Change in anode mass = −0.16 g [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for the cathode change (+0.16 g). Sign must be positive or labelled "increase".

1 mark for the anode change (−0.16 g). Sign must be negative or labelled "decrease".

Answers without sign or direction (e.g. "0.16 g" with no indication of gain or loss) are not accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

For "change in mass" calculations, always do final minus initial. A positive value means the electrode gained mass; a negative value means it lost mass. Always state the sign or write "increase" / "decrease".

Question (d)(ii). Comment on what these mass changes tell you about copper in the cell. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The comment should include:

  • The mass gained at the cathode is equal in size to the mass lost at the anode (both 0.16 g) [1 mark]
  • This shows that copper is being transferred from the anode to the cathode (through the solution) [1 mark]

"They are the same" alone is not enough. The answer must say that copper has been transferred from one electrode to the other.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

This is the principle behind industrial copper refining. The impure anode loses copper, which deposits as pure copper on the cathode. The mass balance in this experiment is your evidence that the copper is moving, not being created or destroyed.

Question (e). Predict and explain the colour change with inert electrodes. [3 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The prediction and explanation:

  • The blue colour fades / the solution becomes paler / the solution eventually becomes colourless [1 mark]
  • Cu2+ ions are still removed at the cathode (deposited as copper metal) [1 mark]
  • With inert (carbon) electrodes, the Cu2+ ions are not replaced at the anode (oxygen is discharged instead from OH− from water), so the concentration of Cu2+ falls and the blue colour fades [1 mark]

"The colour stays the same" is not accepted. With inert electrodes, the colour does change.

"The colour turns green / red" is not accepted. The blue colour fades to pale blue and eventually to colourless.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The two electrolyses look almost identical at the cathode (copper deposits in both). The difference is at the anode. With copper electrodes, copper dissolves and replenishes the Cu2+ concentration. With inert electrodes, oxygen is released instead and the Cu2+ concentration is not replenished.

That is why the blue solution fades with inert electrodes, but stays blue with copper electrodes.

Question (f). Why rinse but not rub the electrodes? [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one from:

  • Rubbing would knock off the freshly deposited copper from the cathode, lowering the measured mass [1 mark]
  • Rinsing washes away copper sulfate solution from the surface so the measured mass is only the electrode (and the deposited copper), not any solution clinging to it

"To clean the electrodes" alone is not accepted. The answer must explain the consequence of rubbing or not rinsing.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The freshly deposited copper on the cathode is soft and only loosely attached. Rubbing scrapes it off, which makes the measured mass change smaller than the true value.

Rinsing in distilled water (then drying without rubbing, or by air-drying) gives the most accurate final mass.
