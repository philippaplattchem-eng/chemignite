CP8 Heat of Combustion of Alcohols

Foundation Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, not assessed in Combined Science (1SC0)

Paper: Paper 2

Specification: 9.28C. Core Practical: Investigate the temperature rise produced in a known mass of water by the combustion of the alcohols ethanol, propanol, butanol and pentanol.

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical compares the energy released by four alcohols (ethanol, propanol, butanol and pentanol) by burning each one and measuring either how much fuel is needed to raise the temperature of water by a fixed amount, or how much the temperature rises when a fixed mass of fuel is burnt. The 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report flagged two repeating errors: candidates confusing "raise the temperature by 30 °C" with "raise the temperature to 30 °C", and confusing the mass of the burner with the mass of alcohol burnt.

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. The Aim

The aim is to compare how much energy is released when ethanol, propanol, butanol and pentanol are burnt. The version usually examined is: find the mass of each alcohol needed to raise the temperature of 100 cm3 of water by a fixed amount (e.g. 30 °C).

Part 2. Apparatus

Apparatus for the heat of combustion experiment, taken from 1CH0/2F June 2019 Figure 16. A spirit burner containing alcohol heats a fixed volume of water in the calorimeter above, with a thermometer recording the temperature rise.
  1. Spirit burner, small glass container holding the alcohol with a cotton wick.
  2. Calorimeter (a copper or metal beaker), holds the water that the heat is transferred into.
  3. Clamp and stand, holds the calorimeter at a fixed height above the burner.
  4. Thermometer, measures the temperature of the water before and after.
  5. Measuring cylinder, measures 100 cm3 of water into the calorimeter.
  6. Balance, weighs the spirit burner before and after burning, to the nearest 0.01 g.
  7. Heatproof mat, under the burner for safety.

Part 3. Method (with mass-loss measurement)

  1. Measure 100 cm3 of water into the calorimeter using a measuring cylinder.
  2. Record the initial temperature of the water with the thermometer.
  3. Weigh the spirit burner (with cap on) and record the initial mass of the burner.
  4. Place the burner under the calorimeter and light the wick.
  5. Stir the water gently. Stop the experiment when the temperature has risen by 30 °C.
  6. Cap the burner to extinguish the flame and reweigh it, record the final mass.
  7. Calculate the mass of alcohol burnt: mass burnt = initial mass − final mass.
  8. Repeat for each of the four alcohols (ethanol, propanol, butanol, pentanol) using a fresh volume of water each time.

Part 4. Variables

Variable typeIn this experiment
Independent variableThe alcohol being burnt
Dependent variableThe mass of alcohol burnt
Control variablesVolume / mass of water; distance from wick to calorimeter; same calorimeter; same temperature rise (e.g. 30 °C)

Part 5. Safety

  1. Alcohols are highly flammable, keep stoppers on burners when not in use.
  2. Tie back long hair; remove loose clothing.
  3. Use a heatproof mat under the apparatus.
  4. Wear safety goggles.
  5. Cap the burner to extinguish the flame, never blow it out, as this can spread alcohol vapour.
  6. Allow the apparatus to cool before handling.

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. Equipment for measuring temperature.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

D. Thermometer [1 mark]

A is incorrect. A balance measures mass, not temperature.

B is incorrect. A measuring cylinder measures volume.

C is incorrect. A stopwatch measures time.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Match the quantity to the right instrument: mass → balance, volume → measuring cylinder / pipette / burette, temperature → thermometer (or temperature probe), time → stopwatch. A "temperature probe" is also accepted as a modern equivalent.

Question 2. Mass of alcohol burnt.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

A. 2.10 g [1 mark]

Working: 165.20 − 163.10 = 2.10 g

B is incorrect. 2.20 would come from a calculation slip.

C is incorrect. 165.20 is the whole burner mass before, not the mass of alcohol burnt.

D is incorrect. 328.30 would come from adding the two masses instead of subtracting.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

This is the classic "mass of burner" vs "mass of alcohol" confusion flagged in the 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report. The balance weighs the whole burner (metal + wick + cap + alcohol inside), so the mass of alcohol burnt is the difference between the readings before and after, not either reading on its own.

Always write: mass of alcohol burnt = initial mass − final mass.

Question 3. At what temperature to stop.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. 50 °C [1 mark]

The water starts at 20.0 °C and needs to rise by 30 °C, so the final temperature is 20.0 + 30 = 50.0 °C.

A is incorrect. 10 °C is below the starting temperature, impossible if the water is being heated.

B is incorrect. 30 °C is the temperature rise, not the final temperature. The water would only be 10 °C above its starting point at this thermometer reading.

D is incorrect. 60 °C would be a 40 °C rise, not 30.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report specifically flagged this as a top mark loser: "many candidates answered by stating 'stop heating the water once it had reached 30 °C', as opposed to a temperature increase of 30 °C."

Use the formula: final temperature = starting temperature + temperature rise. Here, 20 + 30 = 50 °C.

Question 4. Trend in mass burnt as carbon number increases.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. The mass burnt decreases as the number of carbon atoms increases [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The longer-chain alcohols release more energy per gram, so less mass is needed for the same temperature rise, mass burnt decreases.

C is incorrect. The mass burnt clearly changes from one alcohol to the next.

D is incorrect. The trend is monotonic (ethanol > propanol > butanol > pentanol), not peaked at propanol.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Longer-chain alcohols have more C–C and C–H bonds, so each gram of the alcohol releases more energy when burnt. Less mass is therefore needed to produce the same temperature rise in the same volume of water.

Question 5. Why the temperature rise is too low.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Heat is lost to the surroundings, so not all the energy from the flame heats the water [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Incomplete combustion is a smaller effect than heat loss.

B is incorrect. A working thermometer gives a reliable reading; the problem is that less energy actually reaches the water.

D is incorrect. Starting temperature does not affect the magnitude of the rise.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

In a school version of this experiment, only about 20–40% of the heat from the flame actually heats the water. The rest is lost to: the air (convection), the metal calorimeter and clamp (conduction), and the surroundings (radiation). Using a lid and a draught shield reduces these losses significantly.

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a)(i). Equipment for measuring 100 cm3 of water. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The correct piece of equipment is:

  • Measuring cylinder [1 mark]

"Burette" or "pipette" is also accepted. Both measure volume accurately, although a measuring cylinder is the standard choice at this scale.

"Beaker" is not accepted. Beakers do not have accurate graduations.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

For 100 cm3 of water, a 100 cm3 measuring cylinder is the standard choice. Pipettes are usually used for smaller, precise volumes (25 cm3), and burettes are reserved for titrations. A beaker with rough graduations is too inaccurate.

Question (a)(ii). Equipment for measuring the mass of the burner. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The correct piece of equipment is:

  • Balance / mass balance / scales [1 mark]

"Ruler" is not accepted. Rulers measure length.

"Measuring cylinder" is not accepted. That measures volume.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Mass → balance. The balance used should read to at least 0.01 g (2 decimal places) so that the mass burnt (typically about 1–2 g) can be measured with sensible precision.

Question (b). Mass of butanol burnt. [2 marks]

Model Answer

To calculate the mass of butanol burnt:

  • Write down the formula:

    mass burnt = initial mass − final mass

  • Read the values from Table 1:

    initial mass = 172.55 g, final mass = 171.25 g

  • Substitute the values:

    mass burnt = 172.55 − 171.25

    mass burnt = 1.30 [1 mark]

  • State the answer with the correct unit, to 2 decimal places:

    mass of butanol burnt = 1.30 g [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for the correct subtraction (172.55 − 171.25 = 1.30).

1 mark for the final answer with correct unit g, to 2 decimal places.

"1.30" with no unit is not accepted for the second mark.

"1.3" with the unit is accepted with a slight penalty (loses the 2-decimal-place precision mark in some schemes; here we accept it).

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The mass burnt is always initial minus final because the burner gets lighter as alcohol is burnt away. Match the decimal places of the data in the table, if the data is to 2 decimal places, give your answer to 2 decimal places.

Question (c). Trend in mass burnt with carbon number. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The trend description:

  • The mass of alcohol burnt decreases as the number of carbon atoms increases [1 mark]
  • Supported by reference to data: e.g. ethanol (2 C) needs 2.10 g, pentanol (5 C) needs only 1.10 g (or any other valid data comparison) [1 mark]

"It goes down" alone is acceptable for the first mark but is improved by stating the direction explicitly (decreases).

"The mass changes" without a direction is not accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

"Describe the trend" answers need a direction (increases / decreases) and ideally a data reference (e.g. specific values from the table). At GCSE, a model trend answer has two parts: a sentence about the trend, plus a sentence picking out specific numbers as evidence.

Question (d)(i). Stop temperature. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The correct stop temperature is:

  • 50.0 °C / 50 °C [1 mark]

"30 °C" is not accepted. That is the temperature rise, not the final temperature.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Starting temperature 20.0 °C plus a rise of 30 °C gives a final temperature of 50.0 °C. Look at the "Final water temperature" column in Table 1, it's 50.0 °C for every alcohol, which confirms this.

Question (d)(ii). Why 30 °C is the rise, not the final temperature. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one sensible explanation from:

  • The water starts at 20 °C, so a final reading of 30 °C would only be a 10 °C rise [1 mark]
  • The 30 °C is the size of the temperature change / increase, not where the reading ends up
  • If the experiment was stopped at 30 °C, the water would not have absorbed enough energy for a fair comparison between alcohols
Examiner Tips and Tricks

Words to look out for in the question:

  • "rise by 30 °C" or "increase by 30 °C" → the rise amount
  • "reach 30 °C" or "to 30 °C" → the final temperature

This is one of the most common slip-ups in the whole spec, per the 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report.

Question (e). Two features of a good graph. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two features from (1 mark each):

  • Axes labelled with the quantity and units (e.g. "mass of alcohol burnt / g" and "number of carbon atoms")
  • Linear scale that uses more than half of the grid (no big empty space)
  • Points plotted accurately (e.g. small crosses) to ± half a square
  • A smooth curve / line of best fit through the points
  • A clear title for the graph

[2 marks]

"Make it neat" or "use pencil" alone are not accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The reliable two-mark answer: axes labelled with units and line/curve of best fit drawn smoothly through the points. These two features cover most mark schemes.

Question (f). Two safety precautions. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two from (1 mark each):

  • Wear safety goggles / eye protection
  • Tie back long hair
  • Use a heatproof mat under the burner
  • Cap the burner to extinguish (never blow out)
  • Allow apparatus to cool before handling
  • Keep stoppers on alcohol burners when not in use (alcohols are flammable)
  • Refill burners away from any open flame

[2 marks]

"Be careful" is not accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The two safest answers for this experiment: wear safety goggles (eye protection from sudden flares and hot water splashes) and tie back long hair (open flame is the main hazard).

Question (g)(i). Why experimental value is lower than data book. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one from:

  • Heat is lost to the surroundings (air, beaker walls) [1 mark]
  • Not all of the energy from the burning alcohol goes into the water
  • Incomplete combustion of the alcohol (soot / yellow flame) means less energy is released
  • Some alcohol evaporates from the wick without burning

"The thermometer is wrong" is not accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The most accepted answer is heat lost to the surroundings. Most of the energy from the flame heats the air, the calorimeter walls and the clamp, not just the water. Less than half of the energy typically reaches the water in a school experiment.

Question (g)(ii). One improvement. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one sensible improvement from:

  • Use a lid on the calorimeter [1 mark]
  • Use a draught shield around the burner and calorimeter
  • Insulate the sides of the calorimeter
  • Use a polished metal calorimeter (reduces heat lost by radiation)
  • Reduce the distance between the wick and the calorimeter base

"Use more water" is not accepted. This would lower the temperature rise per gram, not improve accuracy.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Improvements always tie back to reducing heat loss: lid (stops hot air rising out), draught shield (stops draughts cooling the flame), insulation (stops conduction). All make more of the flame's energy actually go into heating the water.

Question (h). Why cap the burner (not blow it out). [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one from:

  • Blowing out the flame could spread alcohol vapour, which is flammable / could catch fire [1 mark]
  • Capping the burner stops the alcohol from evaporating further so the final mass is recorded accurately
  • Capping is safer / does not risk relighting
Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two reasons combined: safety (alcohol vapour is flammable; blowing on a flame could disperse the vapour into a fireball) and accuracy (capping prevents further evaporation so the mass loss is only what burnt, not what evaporated).
