CP7 Identifying Ions in Unknown Salts

Foundation Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, not assessed in Combined Science (1SC0)

Paper: Paper 2

Specification: 9.6C/9.7C. Identify the ions in unknown salts, using the tests for the specified cations and anions.

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical pulls together every ion test in Topic 9.1 (flame tests, the sodium hydroxide test for cations, the ammonium test, and the wet tests for halides, sulfates and carbonates) into a systematic procedure for identifying an unknown ionic compound. The June 2019 1CH0/2F examiner report flagged this as a Core Practical that "caused great difficulty for candidates", with the most common errors being wrong flame colours, wrong precipitate colours, and choosing the wrong acid for the anion test.

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. Flame Tests, Cations

CationFlame colour
Lithium, Li+Crimson red
Sodium, Na+Yellow
Potassium, K+Lilac
Calcium, Ca2+Orange-red
Copper(II), Cu2+Blue-green

To carry out a flame test: dip a clean nichrome wire in concentrated HCl, then in a tiny scrape of the solid, and hold it in a roaring (non-luminous) Bunsen flame. Observe the colour.

Part 2. Sodium Hydroxide Test, Cations

Dissolve a fresh sample of the salt in distilled water, then add a few drops of sodium hydroxide solution.

CationPrecipitate colour with NaOH (aq)
Aluminium, Al3+White (dissolves in excess NaOH)
Calcium, Ca2+White (does not dissolve in excess NaOH)
Magnesium, Mg2+White
Copper(II), Cu2+Blue
Iron(II), Fe2+Green
Iron(III), Fe3+Brown

Part 3. Ammonium Ion Test

Dissolve a fresh sample of the salt in distilled water, add sodium hydroxide solution, and warm gently. Hold a piece of damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the test tube.

  1. If ammonia gas is released, the litmus paper turns blue. This shows the salt contains the ammonium ion, NH4+.

Part 4. Anion Tests

AnionTestPositive result
Carbonate, CO32−Add dilute hydrochloric acid; bubble any gas through limewaterBubbles released; limewater turns milky
Sulfate, SO42−Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solutionWhite precipitate
Chloride, Cl−Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solutionWhite precipitate
Bromide, Br−Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solutionCream precipitate
Iodide, I−Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solutionYellow precipitate

Part 5. Safety

  1. Silver nitrate solution stains skin and clothing dark brown / black, wear gloves and a lab coat.
  2. Dilute acids (HCl, HNO3) and sodium hydroxide are irritants, wear safety goggles.
  3. The ammonia gas produced in the ammonium test is a respiratory irritant, use a well-ventilated area.
  4. Always use a fresh sample of unknown for each wet test, otherwise the reagents from earlier tests will contaminate later tests and give false positives.

  1. Aluminium
  2. Copper(II)
  3. Iron(II)

  1. Bromide
  2. Carbonate
  3. Chloride
  4. Sulfate

4. A student tests for ammonium ions in a salt. After adding sodium hydroxide solution and warming gently, the student holds damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the test tube. What would be observed if ammonium ions are present? [1 mark]

  1. The litmus paper turns blue
  2. The litmus paper turns red
  3. The litmus paper turns white / bleaches

  4. Nitric acid produces a brighter colour with silver nitrate

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. Yellow flame.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

D. Sodium [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Calcium gives an orange-red flame, not yellow.

B is incorrect. Copper(II) gives a blue-green flame.

C is incorrect. Potassium gives a lilac flame.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Memorise all five flame colours: Li = crimson red, Na = yellow, K = lilac, Ca = orange-red, Cu = blue-green. The 1CH0/2F examiner reports flag flame colours as a common error, students often confuse Li (crimson red) with Ca (orange-red) when describing them simply as "red".

Question 2. Blue precipitate with NaOH.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Copper(II) [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Aluminium gives a white precipitate (which dissolves in excess NaOH).

C is incorrect. Iron(II) gives a green precipitate.

D is incorrect. Iron(III) gives a brown precipitate.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The cation precipitate colours with NaOH follow the colour of the metal ion in solution. Cu2+ is blue in solution → Cu(OH)2 is blue. Fe2+ is pale green → Fe(OH)2 is green. Fe3+ is brown / orange → Fe(OH)3 is brown.

Question 3. Cream precipitate with AgNO3.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

A. Bromide [1 mark]

B is incorrect. Carbonate is tested with dilute HCl and limewater, not silver nitrate.

C is incorrect. Chloride gives a white precipitate with silver nitrate (not cream).

D is incorrect. Sulfate is tested with dilute HCl and barium chloride, not silver nitrate.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The silver halide precipitate colour mnemonic: white → cream → yellow as you go down Group 7 (Cl → Br → I). The colours get more intense as the halide gets bigger.

Question 4. Test for ammonium ions.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

A. The litmus paper turns blue [1 mark]

B is incorrect. The litmus paper is already red, if ammonium is absent, it stays red, but does not get redder.

C is incorrect. Bleaching is the test for chlorine gas, not ammonia.

D is incorrect. If ammonium is present, the litmus turns blue. Only if ammonium is absent does the litmus stay red.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Ammonia is the only common alkaline gas you meet at GCSE. It turns damp red litmus paper blue. The litmus paper must be damp, the water dissolves the ammonia gas to release the OH− ions that change the colour.

Question 5. Why nitric acid (not HCl) for chloride test.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Hydrochloric acid would add chloride ions to the mixture and give a false positive [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Both acids would dissolve typical chloride salts.

C is incorrect. Cost is not the deciding factor.

D is incorrect. The colour of the silver chloride precipitate is the same regardless of which acid is used to acidify it.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Acid choice in ion tests is always about avoiding interference:

  • Chloride / bromide / iodide tests: use HNO3 (HCl would add Cl− and give false positive)
  • Sulfate test: use HCl (H2SO4 would itself supply SO42− and give false positive)

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Identify the cation in each salt. [4 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The cations are:

  • P: Sodium / Na+ (yellow flame) [1 mark]
  • Q: Lithium / Li+ (crimson red flame) [1 mark]
  • R: Iron(II) / Fe2+ (green precipitate with NaOH) [1 mark]
  • S: Copper(II) / Cu2+ (blue-green flame + blue precipitate with NaOH) [1 mark]

Accept the ion formula (e.g. Na+) or the metal name, either is enough.

For R, "iron" alone is not accepted, "iron(II)" or Fe2+ is needed because Fe3+ gives a brown precipitate, not green.

For S, "copper" is accepted, but "copper(II)" is preferred.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

For each salt, find the row where the evidence is most distinctive:

  • If a salt gives a strong flame colour, the cation is normally one of the five flame-test cations
  • If no flame colour, look at the NaOH row

Salt S gives both a blue-green flame and a blue precipitate with NaOH, both point to copper(II), so this is a confirmed identification.

Question (b). Identify the anion in each salt. [4 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The anions are:

  • P: Chloride / Cl− (white precipitate with acidified silver nitrate) [1 mark]
  • Q: Sulfate / SO42− (white precipitate with acidified barium chloride) [1 mark]
  • R: Sulfate / SO42− (white precipitate with acidified barium chloride) [1 mark]
  • S: Iodide / I− (yellow precipitate with acidified silver nitrate) [1 mark]

Accept the ion formula (e.g. Cl−) or the anion name.

"Chlorine" is not accepted. The ion is chloride.

"Sulfide" is not accepted in place of "sulfate".

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Watch the spelling carefully: chloride (not chlorine), sulfate (not sulfide / sulfite), bromide (not bromine). The "-ide" ending means a simple anion of the element; "-ate" means it contains oxygen.

Question (c). Name of salt R. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The name of salt R is:

  • Iron(II) sulfate [1 mark]

"Iron sulfate" is not accepted. The (II) is needed because Fe3+ would give a different precipitate colour.

"Iron sulfide" is not accepted. The anion is sulfate, not sulfide.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Naming an ionic compound: cation name + anion name, with the oxidation state in brackets for transition metals (e.g. iron(II), iron(III), copper(II)). Always check that the cation name matches the colour you observed.

Question (d). Why use a fresh sample for each wet test. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The fresh sample is needed because:

  • Adding a reagent (e.g. barium chloride) contaminates the solution with ions from that reagent (e.g. chloride) [1 mark]
  • If the same sample is then tested for another anion (e.g. chloride with silver nitrate), the contamination would give a false positive result [1 mark]

"To make it accurate" alone is not accepted. The answer must mention contamination/false positives.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

This is the most common procedural mistake students make: re-using the same test tube of solution for two consecutive wet tests. After adding barium chloride for the sulfate test, the solution has Cl− in it from the BaCl2, so a silver-nitrate test for chloride would always come back positive, but the chloride is from your reagent, not your salt.

Question (e). Safety precaution for silver nitrate. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one from:

  • Wear gloves to avoid skin staining [1 mark]
  • Wear a lab coat to protect clothing from staining
  • Wipe up any spills of silver nitrate immediately
  • Wear safety goggles
  • Wash hands thoroughly after the practical

"Be careful" is not accepted. The answer must give a specific precaution.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Silver nitrate stains skin and clothing dark brown or black, not because it's burning, but because the silver ions react with proteins in skin and dyes in fabric to form metallic silver, which is impossible to wash off. Stains can last for weeks. Wear gloves.

Question (f). Name and formula of salt P. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The name and formula are:

  • Name: Sodium chloride [1 mark]
  • Formula: NaCl [1 mark]

"NaCl2" or "Na2Cl" is not accepted. The charges (+1 and −1) balance with one of each ion.

"Sodium chlorine" is not accepted. The anion is chloride.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Sodium chloride is one of the most common salts you'll see in chemistry, it's table salt. Its formula NaCl is one of the first you should commit to memory: Na is +1, Cl is −1, so one of each.

Question (g). Confirm the cation in Q. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one sensible confirmation from:

  • Repeat the flame test with a clean nichrome wire (to check the crimson red is reproducible) [1 mark]
  • Compare with a known lithium sample, both should give a crimson red flame
  • Use flame photometry to compare the spectrum with a known lithium standard
  • (Less commonly cited) Add NaOH solution, lithium ions do not give a precipitate, ruling out other cations

"Try it again" alone is not enough. The answer should specify a chemical test or its outcome.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The simplest confirmation for a flame-test cation is to repeat the test with a freshly cleaned nichrome wire. Contamination from a previous sample (especially sodium) is the main reason flame tests are unreliable, so a second clean run confirms the colour.

Flame photometry is the instrumental version, it measures the exact wavelengths of light emitted, and can be compared to a database of known cation emission spectra.
