CP3 Preparing Copper Sulfate

Foundation Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Combined Science (1SC0) and Separate Chemistry (1CH0)

Paper: Paper 1

Specification: 3.17. Investigate the preparation of pure, dry hydrated copper sulfate crystals starting from copper oxide, including the use of a water bath.

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical makes a sample of pure, dry, blue copper(II) sulfate crystals. The starting materials are copper oxide (an insoluble black powder) and dilute sulfuric acid. The method has five clear steps: warm the acid, add the copper oxide, filter the mixture, heat the filtrate to concentrate it, and leave it to cool so crystals form.

Key Revision Notes

The five-step method for making copper sulfate crystals from copper oxide and dilute sulfuric acid.
  1. The reaction is: copper oxide + sulfuric acid → copper sulfate + water. The equation is: CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O.
  2. The black copper oxide powder disappears as it reacts. The colourless acid turns blue because of the copper ions in solution.
  3. The acid is warmed using a water bath. A water bath heats the acid gently and evenly, and the temperature cannot go above 100 °C.
  4. Copper oxide is added a little at a time until it is in excess. You can see excess as black solid left at the bottom of the beaker after stirring.
  5. Adding excess copper oxide makes sure all the acid is used up, so the crystals are not contaminated with acid.
  6. The excess copper oxide is removed by filtration. The black solid stays on the filter paper. The blue copper sulfate solution passes through.
  7. The blue filtrate is heated gently to concentrate it. Stop heating before all the water has gone. Heating to dryness gives a white powder, not blue crystals.
  8. The concentrated solution is left to cool slowly. As it cools, blue crystals of copper sulfate form.
  9. The crystals are dried by patting them between filter papers or by leaving them in a warm place.
  10. Safety: wear goggles. Dilute sulfuric acid is corrosive. Use tongs to handle hot glassware. Do not breathe in the copper oxide powder.

Section B. Structured Question (15 marks)

A student makes pure, dry copper sulfate crystals from copper oxide and dilute sulfuric acid. She follows the steps below.

  1. Warm the dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker placed in a water bath.
  2. Add copper oxide one spatula at a time, stirring, until no more reacts.
  3. Filter the mixture.
  4. Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin until the solution is concentrated.
  5. Leave the concentrated solution to cool and crystallise.

(a) Complete the word equation for the reaction:

copper oxide + sulfuric acid → ______ + ______ [2 marks]

(b) State two observations the student would make as the copper oxide reacts with the dilute sulfuric acid. [2 marks]

(c) Give one reason why the dilute sulfuric acid is warmed using a water bath rather than a Bunsen burner. [1 mark]

(d) The student keeps adding copper oxide until some black solid is left at the bottom of the beaker, even after stirring. Explain why she does this. [2 marks]

(e) Name the piece of apparatus used to remove the excess copper oxide from the mixture. [1 mark]

(f) Describe how the student obtains pure, dry crystals of copper sulfate from the blue filtrate. [4 marks]

(g) Give two safety precautions the student should take during this practical. [2 marks]

(h) The student's final crystals are blue. Suggest why hydrated copper sulfate crystals are blue. [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. Which two reactants are used?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Copper oxide and dilute sulfuric acid [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Copper metal does not react with dilute hydrochloric acid (copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series).

B is incorrect. Hydrochloric acid would give copper chloride, not copper sulfate.

D is incorrect. Nitric acid would give copper nitrate, not copper sulfate.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The salt produced takes its name from the acid used. Sulfuric acid gives a sulfate. Hydrochloric acid gives a chloride. Nitric acid gives a nitrate.

Question 2. What colour does the solution change to?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Blue [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Green solutions are typical of nickel(II) ions or some chromium ions, not copper(II).

B is incorrect. Red is the colour of copper metal, not copper(II) ions in solution.

D is incorrect. Yellow could be iron(III), not copper(II).

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Copper(II) ions in solution are always blue. Watching the colourless acid turn blue is your sign that the reaction is happening.

Question 3. How does the student know the copper oxide is in excess?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Some black solid is left at the bottom of the beaker, even after stirring [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The mixture turns blue, not red, and the colour change does not show excess.

B is incorrect. No gas is produced in this reaction.

D is incorrect. The mixture stays warm, but cooling would not indicate excess.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The visible sign of excess is unreacted black solid sitting on the bottom of the beaker. The acid has used up all the H+ ions it can supply, so any more CuO added has no acid left to react with and stays as solid.

Question 4. How is the excess copper oxide removed?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Filtration [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Distillation separates liquids by boiling, not solids from liquids.

C is incorrect. Chromatography separates dyes, not solids from solutions.

D is incorrect. Crystallisation comes later in this method, after filtration, to form the salt crystals.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Whenever you need to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid, the answer is filtration. The solid sits on the filter paper as residue and the liquid passes through as filtrate.

Question 5. Why heat just to concentrated, not dry?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. To keep the water of crystallisation, so the crystals stay blue [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Saving energy is not the main reason. The chemistry reason is about the water of crystallisation.

C is incorrect. The evaporating basin can withstand the heat. The reason to stop heating is about the product.

D is incorrect. The leftover liquid is acidic copper sulfate solution and should never be drunk.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Hydrated copper sulfate (with water of crystallisation built into the crystal) is blue. If you heat it to dryness, the water is driven off and you are left with anhydrous (water-free) copper sulfate, which is white.

The November 2021 examiner report flagged this as one of the most common mistakes. Always say "heat to concentrate the solution, then leave to cool".

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Complete the word equation. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The complete word equation is:

copper oxide + sulfuric acid → copper sulfate + water

  • Copper sulfate [1 mark]
  • Water [1 mark]

Accept "copper(II) sulfate". Do not accept "copper".

"Hydrogen" is not accepted in place of water. Acid + metal oxide gives salt + water, not salt + hydrogen.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Acid + metal oxide always gives a salt and water. Acid + reactive metal gives salt and hydrogen. Knowing which pattern goes with which reactant pair stops mix-ups.

Question (b). Give two observations. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two from (1 mark for each):

  • The black copper oxide powder disappears / is used up
  • The colourless solution turns blue / a blue solution forms
  • The mixture warms up slightly
  • Once all the acid has reacted, some black solid is left in the beaker

[2 marks]

"Bubbles form" is not accepted. There is no gas produced in this reaction.

"The colour changes" is too vague. Specify the colours: colourless to blue.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

"Observations" means things you can see with your eyes. Stick to colour and disappearance of the solid. Both are easy ways to score.

Question (c). Why is a water bath used? [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any one from:

  • To heat the acid evenly / to avoid hot spots [1 mark]
  • To stop the acid from spitting / boiling violently
  • The temperature cannot exceed 100 °C, so the acid cannot boil dry

"It is safer" alone is not enough. The answer must say what safety risk the water bath avoids.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

A water bath gives gentle, even heating. Direct heat from a Bunsen flame can make hot acid spit out of the beaker, which is dangerous.

Question (d). Why add excess copper oxide? [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The student adds copper oxide in excess because:

  • To make sure all the dilute sulfuric acid reacts / is used up [1 mark]
  • So no acid is left in the solution, which would contaminate the final crystals [1 mark]

"To use up the acid" alone is not enough for both marks. The reason it matters (no acid contamination) must be given.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two marks here: one for what the excess does (uses up the acid), and one for why that matters (so no acid is left in the crystals).

Question (e). Name the apparatus used to remove excess copper oxide. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The apparatus used is:

  • Filter funnel / filter funnel and filter paper / filtration apparatus [1 mark]

"Filter" alone is accepted, but "filtration" is the name of the technique, not a piece of apparatus.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Filter funnel with filter paper is the standard answer. The solid stays on the paper. The liquid passes through into the flask or beaker below.

Question (f). Describe how to obtain crystals from the filtrate. [4 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The description must include:

  • Heat the filtrate gently (in an evaporating basin / using a water bath) [1 mark]
  • Evaporate some of the water / heat to concentrate the solution. Stop heating before the solution dries out [1 mark]
  • Leave the concentrated solution to cool slowly so crystals form [1 mark]
  • Dry the crystals (between filter papers / in a warm place / in a desiccator) [1 mark]

"Evaporate all the water" is not accepted. This destroys the water of crystallisation and gives a white powder, not blue crystals.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The two key words are concentrate and cool. Heat to concentrate (not to dryness), then cool slowly. That gives the marker both halves of the right answer and avoids the November 2021 examiner-report trap.

Question (g). Give two safety precautions. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two from (1 mark for each):

  • Wear safety goggles
  • Use tongs to handle hot glassware / the evaporating basin
  • Avoid breathing in the copper oxide powder
  • Wash hands after handling chemicals
  • Use a heatproof mat under the apparatus

[2 marks]

"Be careful" or "do not run in the lab" without a specific precaution are not accepted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two safe answers here are "wear safety goggles" (acid splashes are an eye risk) and "use tongs for the hot evaporating basin" (avoids burns). Both are quick to write and always relevant.

Question (h). Why are hydrated copper sulfate crystals blue? [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Hydrated copper sulfate crystals are blue because:

  • They contain water of crystallisation / water molecules in the crystal structure (which gives them the blue colour) [1 mark]

"Because copper is blue" is not accepted. Copper metal is reddish-brown. It is the copper(II) ions with water of crystallisation that are blue.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

If you remove the water of crystallisation (by heating to dryness), the crystals turn white. This is why the heating step has to stop before all the water has gone.
