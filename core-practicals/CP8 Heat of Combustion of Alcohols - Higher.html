CP8 Heat of Combustion of Alcohols

Higher Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, not assessed in Combined Science (1SC0)

Paper: Paper 2

Specification: 9.28C. Core Practical: Investigate the temperature rise produced in a known mass of water by the combustion of the alcohols ethanol, propanol, butanol and pentanol.

Total marks: 21

This Core Practical compares the energy released per gram of fuel for the alcohols ethanol, propanol, butanol and pentanol by burning each one and measuring either the mass of fuel needed for a fixed temperature rise, or the temperature rise for a fixed mass of fuel. Higher-tier questions test the full extended-response method (6 marks), data-handling and plotting, balanced symbol equations for combustion, and quantitative reasoning about the trend along the homologous series. The 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report flagged two repeating errors: confusing "raise by 30 °C" with "raise to 30 °C", and confusing the mass of the burner with the mass of alcohol burnt.

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. Aim and Approach

Compare the energy released by ethanol (2 C), propanol (3 C), butanol (4 C) and pentanol (5 C). Two equivalent versions are used:

  • Constant Î”T, measure mass: burn each alcohol until the water rises by a fixed amount (e.g. 30 °C), then find the mass of fuel needed.
  • Constant mass, measure Î”T: burn the same mass of each alcohol and record the temperature rise produced.

Part 2. Apparatus

Apparatus for method (a), taken from 1CH0/2F June 2019 Figure 16. The spirit burner heats a fixed volume of water in the calorimeter; the thermometer records the temperature rise; the balance (not shown) records the mass loss of the burner.
  1. Spirit burner with cap, containing the alcohol
  2. Copper calorimeter or thin-walled metal beaker, clamped above the burner
  3. Thermometer (0–110 °C) or temperature probe + data logger
  4. Measuring cylinder (100 cm3) for the water
  5. Balance (to 0.01 g) for the burner
  6. Stirring rod, lid (with hole for thermometer), draught shield, heatproof mat

Part 3. Method (constant Î”T version)

  1. Measure 100 cm3 of water into the calorimeter and record the initial temperature Tinitial.
  2. Weigh the spirit burner (with cap, containing alcohol) on the balance and record the mass mbefore.
  3. Position the burner under the calorimeter at a fixed distance (e.g. 5 cm from the wick to the base). Light the wick.
  4. Stir the water continuously. Cap the burner the moment the temperature has risen by exactly 30 °C (so Tfinal = Tinitial + 30 °C).
  5. Reweigh the burner with cap on, record mafter.
  6. Calculate mass burnt = mbefore − mafter.
  7. Repeat with each of the four alcohols, using fresh water each time and keeping the same volume of water, distance from wick, and target temperature rise.
  8. Repeat the whole experiment to get concordant results, then calculate the mean mass burnt for each alcohol.

Part 4. Variables

VariableIdentification
IndependentThe alcohol (ethanol, propanol, butanol, pentanol)
DependentMass of alcohol burnt for the fixed temperature rise
ControlVolume / mass of water; starting temperature; distance from wick to calorimeter; same calorimeter; same target temperature rise; draught conditions

Part 5. Trend and Combustion Equations

The longer the carbon chain, the more energy released per gram, so the mass needed for a fixed temperature rise decreases:

ethanol > propanol > butanol > pentanol (mass burnt, for same Î”T)

Balanced combustion equations (complete combustion):

  • Ethanol: C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O
  • Propanol: 2C3H7OH + 9O2 → 6CO2 + 8H2O
  • Butanol: C4H9OH + 6O2 → 4CO2 + 5H2O
  • Pentanol: 2C5H11OH + 15O2 → 10CO2 + 12H2O

Part 6. Sources of Error

  1. Heat loss to surroundings, the dominant error; up to 50–80% of energy is lost to air, calorimeter walls and the clamp. Reduce with a lid, a draught shield, and a polished metal calorimeter.
  2. Incomplete combustion, yellow / sooty flame means some carbon is unreacted, so less energy is released than expected.
  3. Evaporation from the wick after extinguishing, cap the burner the instant the temperature reaches the target.
  4. Calorimeter absorbs heat, the metal walls warm up as well as the water, so the water gets less of the energy than expected.

Because of these losses, experimental values are always lower than data-book values, often by 50–80%. The pattern along the series is reliable but the absolute energies are not.

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. Balanced equation for combustion of ethanol.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O [1 mark]

Check: left = 2C, 6H, 7O; right = 2C, 6H, (4+3)=7O. Balanced.

A is incorrect. With only 2O2, the right-hand side has 7 O atoms (4 + 3) but the left only has 5 (1 + 4), not balanced.

C is incorrect. CO is the incomplete combustion product. Complete combustion gives CO2.

D is incorrect. Doubling the ethanol and using 5O2 doesn't balance either: left = 13 O, right = (8+6) = 14 O.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Balancing alcohol combustion: write the products first (n CO2 from n carbons; (n+1)/2 H2O from (2n+1) H atoms if the alcohol is CnH2n+1OH). Then count the O atoms on the right and balance O2 on the left, remembering the O atom in the −OH group.

For ethanol: 2CO2 has 4 O; 3H2O has 3 O; total 7 O on the right. Left side has 1 O from −OH, so need 6 more from O2, i.e. 3 O2.

Question 2. Pentanol mass per °C.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

A. 0.02 g [1 mark]

Working: 0.46 ÷ 20 = 0.023, which to 2 decimal places is 0.02.

B is incorrect. 0.23 = 0.46 ÷ 2 (used 2 instead of 20).

C is incorrect. 0.46 is the original mass, not divided by anything.

D is incorrect. 9.20 = 0.46 × 20 (multiplied instead of divided).

Examiner Tips and Tricks

If x g produces a temperature rise of Î”T, then x ÷ Î”T g produces a 1 °C rise, assuming the response is proportional. Always divide by the temperature rise.

Watch the decimal-place instruction: "to 2 decimal places" means 0.023 rounds to 0.02 (not 0.023). The third decimal place is rounded off.

Question 3. Why experimental < data-book.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. Most of the energy is lost to the surroundings, not transferred into the water [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Alcohols in spirit burners are not diluted with water.

B is incorrect. The thermometer reads correctly; the issue is that less heat is reaching the water in the first place.

D is incorrect. Data-book values are theoretical maxima from calorimetry, they are not estimates that happen to be too high.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The school version of this experiment typically transfers only 20–40% of the combustion energy to the water. Heat is lost to: air (convection from the rising hot gases), calorimeter walls (conduction; warming the metal beaker rather than the water), and the clamp / surroundings (radiation). Lids and draught shields reduce all three.

Question 4. Independent variable.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

A. The alcohol used [1 mark]

B is incorrect. The mass of water is a control variable (kept constant).

C is incorrect. The mass of alcohol burnt is the dependent variable (what you measure).

D is incorrect. The distance from the wick to the calorimeter is a control variable.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The independent variable is the thing you deliberately change to investigate its effect. Here you change the alcohol, that's the I in "I change".

Question 5. Improvement that reduces heat loss most.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. Adding a lid to the calorimeter and a draught shield around the apparatus [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Starting temperature does not affect heat loss; it just shifts the initial reading.

C is incorrect. More alcohol does not change heat loss; it just lets the experiment run longer.

D is incorrect. A glass beaker loses more heat by conduction than polished metal, so this would make things worse, though glass beakers are sometimes used for visibility.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Two-part improvements always beat one-part improvements: a lid stops convection losses (hot air rising out); a draught shield stops convection losses (cold air blowing in). Used together they typically cut heat loss in half.

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Describe the experiment in detail. [6 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

This is a 6-mark levelled-response question. Mark across the response rather than per point.

Chemical ideas expected:

  • Measure 100 cm3 of water into the calorimeter (using a measuring cylinder)
  • Place the calorimeter above the spirit burner (clamped at a fixed distance)
  • Record the initial temperature of the water
  • Weigh the spirit burner with cap (containing the alcohol) and record the initial mass
  • Light the wick
  • Stir the water continuously / use a stirrer
  • Stop the heating (cap the burner) when the temperature has risen by 20 °C (not "reached 20 °C")
  • Reweigh the burner with cap and record the final mass
  • Calculate mass burnt = initial mass − final mass
  • Repeat with each alcohol (ethanol, propanol, butanol, pentanol)
  • Control variables: same volume of water; same distance from wick to calorimeter; same target temperature rise; same calorimeter

Level descriptors:

  • Level 3 (5–6 marks): A well-developed answer covering apparatus, measurements before and after, the temperature-rise stopping point, the mass-loss calculation, and at least one control variable, in a logical order with chemical terms used correctly.
  • Level 2 (3–4 marks): A relevant answer with most of the method steps, possibly missing one of: control variables, the explicit "temperature rise" wording, or the mass-loss subtraction.
  • Level 1 (1–2 marks): A basic answer with some method steps in the right order but key details (apparatus names, what is measured, the temperature-rise wording) missing or wrong.
  • Level 0: No relevant chemistry.

Common errors that cap the mark:

  • "Stop heating when the temperature reaches 20 °C" instead of "rises by 20 °C" caps at Level 2 (per the 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report)
  • Confusing the mass of the burner with the mass of alcohol (e.g. "the mass of alcohol was 122.51 g") caps at Level 2
Examiner Tips and Tricks

This question was lifted directly from 1CH0/2F November 2021 Q7(b)(ii) format. The 1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report on the same practical highlighted the two top errors: "many candidates answered by stating 'stop heating the water once it had reached 30 °C', as opposed to a temperature increase of 30 °C. Many candidates confused the mass of the burner with the mass of ethanol."

Top-band template (memorise this structure):

  1. Measure 100 cm3 water into calorimeter
  2. Record initial water temperature
  3. Weigh burner (with cap) and record initial mass
  4. Light wick, stir continuously
  5. Cap burner when temperature has risen by 20 °C
  6. Reweigh burner with cap, calculate mass burnt = initial − final
  7. Repeat with each alcohol, keeping the volume of water and the wick distance the same
Question (b). Mass of butanol burnt. [2 marks]

Model Answer

To calculate the mass of butanol burnt:

  • Write down the formula:

    mass burnt = initial mass − final mass

  • Substitute the values from Table 1:

    mass burnt = 152.62 − 152.23

    mass burnt = 0.39 [1 mark]

  • State the answer with the correct unit, to 2 decimal places:

    mass of butanol burnt = 0.39 g [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for the correct subtraction (152.62 − 152.23 = 0.39).

1 mark for the final answer with correct unit g, to 2 decimal places.

"0.39" with no unit is not accepted for the second mark.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Always: mass of fuel burnt = initial mass of burner − final mass of burner. The whole burner is weighed each time, so the difference gives you the mass of alcohol that has evaporated and burnt.

Question (c). Mass of pentanol per °C. [2 marks]

Model Answer

To calculate the mass of pentanol needed per °C rise:

  • Write down the formula:

    mass per °C = total mass ÷ total temperature rise

  • Substitute the values:

    mass per °C = 0.36 ÷ 20

    mass per °C = 0.018 [1 mark]

  • Round to 2 decimal places and state with the correct unit:

    mass per °C = 0.02 g [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for the calculation (0.36 ÷ 20 = 0.018).

1 mark for the final answer 0.02 g (to 2 decimal places) with unit.

"0.018" is accepted for the calculation mark, but the question asks for 2 decimal places, only 0.02 scores the second mark.

An answer of 0.02 without working or without unit scores only 1 mark.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Watch the rounding instruction: "to 2 decimal places" cuts off everything from the third decimal place onwards. 0.018 rounds to 0.02 because the third decimal (8) rounds the second decimal up from 1 to 2.

This style of calculation (mass per degree) appears almost every sitting on this Core Practical. The trick is the unit: g/°C or just g.

Question (d). Trend and explanation in terms of bonds. [3 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The trend and explanation:

  • The mass of alcohol burnt decreases as the number of carbon atoms in the alcohol increases (use specific data: e.g. ethanol = 0.49 g, pentanol = 0.36 g) [1 mark]
  • Longer-chain alcohols have more bonds (more C–C and C–H bonds) per molecule [1 mark]
  • So more energy is released per molecule (or per gram) when they burn, meaning less mass is needed for the same temperature rise [1 mark]

"It decreases" without a data reference is acceptable for the first mark.

"More energy because longer chain" alone scores 1 of the final 2 marks, the answer must link the longer chain to more bonds and then to more energy released.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The bond-energy reasoning: making bonds releases energy. Combustion forms many new C=O and O–H bonds in CO2 and H2O. Longer-chain alcohols have more carbons and more hydrogens, so combustion forms more product bonds per molecule, releasing more energy per molecule, and per gram of fuel.

For full marks, link the chain: more carbons → more bonds (to break and to form) → more energy released per gram.

Question (e). Two reasons for low experimental value + one improvement. [3 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

Any two reasons (1 mark each):

  • Heat lost to the surroundings (air, beaker walls, clamp, calorimeter sides)
  • Incomplete combustion of the alcohol (yellow / sooty flame means some energy stays in unreacted carbon)
  • Some alcohol evaporates from the wick without burning
  • Some of the heat warms the calorimeter (metal walls) rather than the water

Plus one improvement (1 mark):

  • Add a lid to the calorimeter
  • Use a draught shield around the apparatus
  • Use a polished metal calorimeter (reduces radiation losses)
  • Insulate the sides of the calorimeter
  • Reduce the distance from wick to calorimeter base
  • Use a bomb calorimeter (commercial method, isolates the system from the surroundings entirely)

"Inaccurate equipment" or "human error" without specifics scores 0.

"Improve the procedure" without naming a specific apparatus change scores 0 for the improvement mark.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The top scoring three-part answer:

  1. Heat loss to surroundings (the dominant effect)
  2. Incomplete combustion (yellow flame, soot)
  3. Lid on calorimeter + draught shield, both reduce convection losses

These three points are the canonical answers and appear in nearly every sitting of this practical.
