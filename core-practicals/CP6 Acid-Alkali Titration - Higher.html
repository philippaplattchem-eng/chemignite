CP6 Acid-Alkali Titration

Higher Tier

Tier: Examined on Foundation and Higher tier

Qualification: Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, not assessed in Combined Science (1SC0)

Paper: Paper 1

Specification: 5.9C. Core Practical: Carry out an accurate acid-alkali titration, using burette, pipette and a suitable indicator.

Total marks: 20

This Core Practical is the accurate titration of an acid against an alkali, measured precisely enough that the titre can be used to calculate an unknown concentration. Higher-tier questions test apparatus terminology, reading the burette to 0.05 cm3, identifying concordant results within 0.10 cm3, and using the mean titre in a follow-on concentration calculation. The June 2019 1CH0/1F examiner report flagged that the most common errors are confusing burette with pipette and giving an incomplete method (describing only one of the rough or accurate stages).

Key Revision Notes

Part 1. Apparatus and the Reasons Behind Each Choice

  1. Burette, holds the acid; calibrated to deliver to the nearest 0.05 cm3. Acid is in the burette by convention because it can be added in tiny, controlled drops as the end-point is approached.
  2. Pipette (25.0 cm3) with pipette filler, transfers an exact, fixed volume of alkali into the conical flask. Uncertainty typically ±0.06 cm3, which is much smaller than a measuring cylinder.
  3. Conical flask, shape allows continuous swirling without splashing. A beaker would splash and lose alkali.
  4. White tile, placed under the flask so that the colour change is sharp and easy to see against a uniform background.
  5. Funnel, used only while filling the burette. Removed before titrating so that drips don't add uncounted acid to the burette during the run.
  6. The burette is rinsed with the acid (not water) before filling, so that residual water does not dilute the acid, otherwise the titre would be too high.
  7. The pipette is rinsed with the alkali before drawing the measured volume, for the same reason.
  8. The burette tip is filled with acid before starting (by briefly opening the tap) to remove any air bubble, an air bubble would otherwise be displaced into the flask during the titration and the recorded titre would be too high.

Part 2. The Two-Stage Method

  1. Rough titration: add acid quickly while swirling, until the indicator just changes. Record the rough titre. This gives an approximate end-point.
  2. Accurate titration: with a fresh 25.0 cm3 portion of alkali, add acid quickly to within 1–2 cm3 of the rough titre, then add dropwise while swirling, until a single drop produces the colour change. Record the accurate titre.
  3. Repeat the accurate titration until two titres are concordant (within 0.10 cm3 of each other).
  4. The mean titre is the average of the concordant accurate titres only, the rough run and any anomalies are discarded.

Part 3. Choosing the Indicator

Part 4. Mean Titre to Concentration

  1. Once you have the mean titre, the calculation of unknown concentration uses three steps: moles of known → moles of unknown via the balanced equation → concentration of unknown.
  2. Formula: concentration (mol/dm3) = moles ÷ volume (in dm3). Always convert cm3 to dm3 by dividing by 1000.

Mark Scheme and Guidance

Section A. Multiple Choice

Question 1. Pipette vs measuring cylinder.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. A pipette has a smaller uncertainty / is more precise [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Cost is not the reason, pipettes are often more expensive than measuring cylinders.

C is incorrect. The pipette is harder to hold steady than a measuring cylinder; that is not the reason.

D is incorrect. Both can be made in 25 cm3 sizes, volume is not the deciding factor.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

A 25 cm3 bulb pipette has uncertainty around ±0.06 cm3; a 25 cm3 measuring cylinder has graduations 10 times wider (uncertainty around ±0.5 cm3). For accurate titration calculations, this difference matters: an error of 0.5 cm3 in the alkali volume propagates into a 2% error in the final concentration.

Question 2. Why rinse burette with acid?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. To stop any residual water from diluting the acid, which would make the titre too high [1 mark]

A is incorrect. A burette is washed with water first; the acid rinse is to displace this water, not to dissolve solids.

C is incorrect. Burettes do not crack from rinsing with water or acid.

D is incorrect. Tap wetting is not the reason, the issue is the bulk acid being diluted.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

If the burette is wet with water and you fill it with acid, the water dilutes the acid in the burette. A diluted acid means more volume of acid is needed to neutralise the same amount of alkali (the titre is too high) which would make the calculated acid concentration too low.

Always link the rinse to the effect on the recorded titre, not just "to avoid contamination".

Question 3. Which titres for the mean?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

C. The two concordant titres only (26.30 and 26.35 cm3) [1 mark]

A is incorrect. The rough titre is always discarded, it is a quick first estimate, not an accurate reading.

B is incorrect. 26.55 cm3 differs by 0.20 cm3 from 26.35, which is greater than the 0.10 cm3 concordant limit, so it should be discarded.

D is incorrect. The rough titre alone is the least reliable value of the four.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

"Concordant" = within 0.10 cm3. Scan the accurate titres only, ignore the rough run, then pick out the values that match each other most closely. Only use those in the mean.

If only one titre is left after discarding anomalies, repeat the experiment, one value is not enough to calculate a meaningful mean.

Question 4. Why not use universal indicator?
Mark Scheme and Guidance

B. It gives a gradual range of colours, with no single sharp end-point [1 mark]

A is incorrect. Universal indicator does change colour at neutral (green) but the change to or from neutral is part of a continuous range, not a sharp transition.

C is incorrect. Universal indicator works across the full pH range, including in acid.

D is incorrect. Universal indicator is cheap and widely available; cost is not the reason.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Universal indicator passes through red → orange → yellow → green → blue → purple as pH changes. In a titration you need to know exactly when neutralisation has happened, and a gradual rainbow makes that moment impossible to identify precisely. Phenolphthalein (pink → colourless) gives a single, unmistakeable change.

Question 5. Concentration of HCl from titration.
Mark Scheme and Guidance

D. 0.1250 mol/dm3 [1 mark]

Working: moles of NaOH = 0.100 × (25.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.00250 mol. From the 1:1 stoichiometry, moles of HCl = 0.00250 mol. Concentration of HCl = 0.00250 ÷ (20.0 ÷ 1000) = 0.125 mol/dm3.

A is incorrect. 0.0625 would come from dividing by 2 by mistake.

B is incorrect. 0.0800 would come from using the wrong ratio (e.g. 25/20 instead of 20/25 the wrong way).

C is incorrect. 0.1000 would assume the acid concentration is the same as the alkali concentration, which ignores the different volumes.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Quick check: a smaller volume of acid is needed than alkali (20 vs 25 cm3), so the acid must be more concentrated. The answer should therefore be larger than 0.100, only D fits.

The ratio shortcut: cHCl ÷ cNaOH = VNaOH ÷ VHCl = 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25. So cHCl = 1.25 × 0.100 = 0.125 mol/dm3.

Section B. Structured Question

Question (a). Why rinse the pipette with sodium hydroxide. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The pipette is rinsed with sodium hydroxide because:

  • Any residual water in the pipette would dilute the sodium hydroxide / lower the concentration of the alkali in the flask [1 mark]
  • This would change the volume of acid needed to neutralise it / give an inaccurate / incorrect titre [1 mark]

"To clean the pipette" or "to prevent contamination" alone is not accepted. The marker is looking for the dilution effect and its consequence for the titre.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The full chain: residual water → dilutes the alkali → fewer moles of NaOH in the flask → less acid needed → titre too low. Mention at least two steps for full marks.

Notice that the rinse logic is the same as for the burette, both must be rinsed with the solution they will hold, not water.

Question (b). Why fill the burette tip first. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The burette tip is filled first because:

  • To remove any air bubble (from the tap / tip of the burette) [1 mark]
  • If the air bubble was still there at the start of the titration, it would be displaced into the flask during the run, and the recorded volume of acid (the titre) would be too high [1 mark]

"To make the burette ready" is not accepted. The answer must mention the air bubble specifically.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Air bubbles trapped below the tap take up space that would otherwise contain acid. During the titration, the bubble is displaced through the tap into the flask, but this volume of "acid" was never really there, so the burette reading drops without the matching amount of acid having actually been delivered. The recorded titre is artificially high, and so the calculated acid concentration would be artificially low.

Question (c)(i). Which titres are concordant. [1 mark]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The concordant titres are:

  • Titrations 1, 2 and 3: 21.45, 21.55 and 21.65 cm3. The closest pair is 21.55 and 21.65 cm3. [1 mark]

Strict reading: each adjacent pair differs by 0.10 cm3 (21.55 and 21.65; 21.45 and 21.55), so all three values are within the concordant range pairwise.

Accept also "Titrations 2 and 3 (21.55 and 21.65 cm3)" if students apply the strictest pair-matching interpretation.

"All four" is not accepted. The rough titre (22.40) is always discarded.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The strict "within 0.10 cm3" rule applies pairwise. When in doubt, take the closest matching pair as your concordant set. Always discard the rough run.

Question (c)(ii). Mean titre. [2 marks]

Model Answer

Using the three concordant accurate titres (Titrations 1, 2 and 3):

  • Add the three titres:

    21.45 + 21.55 + 21.65 = 64.65

  • Divide by 3:

    mean titre = 64.65 ÷ 3

    mean titre = 21.55 [1 mark]

  • State the answer with the correct unit, to 2 decimal places:

    mean titre = 21.55 cm3 [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for correct addition and division (64.65 ÷ 3).

1 mark for the final answer 21.55 cm3 with correct unit and to 2 decimal places.

If only Titrations 2 and 3 are used (the closest pair), accept (21.55 + 21.65) ÷ 2 = 21.60 cm3.

"21.55" without unit is not accepted for the second mark.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

Burette readings and titres must always be given to 2 decimal places (the second decimal is either 0 or 5). Answers like 21.6 or 21.55... (with trailing dots) lose marks.

Question (d). Calculate the concentration of HCl. [4 marks]

Model Answer

To calculate the concentration of the hydrochloric acid:

  • Step 1, convert the volume of NaOH (the known solution) to dm3 and find the moles of NaOH:

    volume of NaOH = 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm3

    moles of NaOH = concentration × volume = 0.100 × 0.0250

    moles of NaOH = 0.00250 mol [1 mark]

  • Step 2, use the 1:1 stoichiometry of the equation to find moles of HCl:

    HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O (1:1 mole ratio)

    moles of HCl = 0.00250 mol [1 mark]

  • Step 3, convert the mean titre (volume of HCl) to dm3:

    volume of HCl = 21.55 ÷ 1000 = 0.02155 dm3

  • Step 4, calculate the concentration of HCl:

    concentration = moles ÷ volume = 0.00250 ÷ 0.02155

    concentration = 0.11600… [1 mark]

  • Step 5, state the final answer with the correct unit, to 3 significant figures:

    concentration = 0.116 mol/dm3 [1 mark]

Mark Scheme and Guidance

1 mark for the correct moles of NaOH calculation (0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol).

1 mark for correctly stating that moles of HCl = moles of NaOH from the 1:1 ratio.

1 mark for the concentration calculation (moles ÷ volume in dm3).

1 mark for the final answer 0.116 mol/dm3 (accept 0.12 to 2 s.f.) with correct unit.

Allow ECF (error carried forward) from a wrong mean titre in part (c)(ii), provided the working is shown.

An answer of 0.116 without working scores 4/4. An answer of 0.116 with no unit scores 3/4.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The standard three-step titration calculation, every time:

  1. Moles of known = concentration × volume (converted to dm3)
  2. Moles of unknown from the balanced equation (here, 1:1)
  3. Concentration of unknown = moles ÷ volume (in dm3)

The two cm3 → dm3 conversions are the most commonly forgotten steps. Always divide cm3 by 1000 to get dm3.

Question (e). Effect of leaving the funnel in the burette. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The effect on the calculated concentration is:

  • Drips from the funnel would add extra acid to the burette during the titration / the recorded volume of acid used would be smaller than the true volume actually delivered (the titre would be too low) [1 mark]
  • So the calculated concentration of the acid would be too high (because a smaller volume appears to neutralise the same amount of alkali) [1 mark]

"It would affect the result" or "results would be inaccurate" is not accepted alone, the direction (too high) and the reasoning must be given.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The trick is to track the direction of error all the way through to the calculated concentration:

Funnel drips → extra acid enters the burette uncounted → recorded titre is smaller than the true delivered volume → using a smaller volume in the calculation (concentration = moles ÷ volume) gives a larger calculated concentration.

Question (f). Why phenolphthalein but not universal indicator. [2 marks]
Mark Scheme and Guidance

The explanation should include:

  • Phenolphthalein gives a sharp / single colour change at the end-point (pink to colourless) [1 mark]
  • Universal indicator gives a gradual range of colours, so it is impossible to identify a single, precise end-point [1 mark]

"Phenolphthalein is better" alone is not accepted.

"Universal indicator is not accurate" alone is not accepted. The answer must mention the gradual colour change.

Examiner Tips and Tricks

The key word is sharp. Titration end-point detection depends on one decisive colour change, phenolphthalein switches from pink to colourless with a single drop of acid. Universal indicator's red → orange → yellow → green → blue → purple sweep is great for showing pH approximately, but useless for pinning down the neutral end-point to 0.05 cm3.
