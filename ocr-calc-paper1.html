0   Mathematical Skills — Significant Figures & Uncertainties  (M1.1 & M1.3)

Significant Figures (M1.1)

General ruleReport the final answer to the same number of sig figs as the least precise piece of data.
Addition & subtractionMatch decimal places (not sig figs) — e.g. 25.50 − 8.30 = 17.20 °C (2 d.p.)
Intermediate stepsDo not round intermediate values — keep full precision in the calculator until the final step.
pH & logarithmsThe whole number part of a logarithm is not a significant figure. Only the decimal part counts. So pH = 2.45 = 2 sig figs; pH = 2.450 = 3 sig figs.
pH rule in practiceIf data is given to 3 sig figs, give pH to 2 decimal places (e.g. 1.70, 3.04). OCR mark schemes accept 2 d.p. as default.

Uncertainties (M1.3)

Absolute uncertaintyHalf the resolution of the instrument. Burette: ±0.05 cm³ per reading.
% uncertainty (single measurement)$\%\ \text{uncertainty} = \dfrac{\text{absolute uncertainty}}{\text{measurement}} \times 100\%$
Adding/subtracting measurementsAdd the absolute uncertainties. E.g. titre = final − initial: uncertainty = 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.10 cm³
% uncertainty in a titre$\%\ \text{uncertainty} = \dfrac{2 \times 0.05}{\text{titre}} \times 100\%$
Temperature differenceThermometer resolution 0.5 °C: uncertainty in ΔT = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0 °C

Common Examiner Errors

• Rounding intermediate values — always round only at the final step.
• Confusing sig figs with decimal places (e.g. giving 2.48 instead of 2.5 when 2 sig figs are required).
• Forgetting that significant zeroes must be kept (e.g. 5.0 × 10⁵, not 5 × 10⁵).
• Using only one burette reading's uncertainty instead of adding both (initial and final).

Worked Example — % Uncertainty in a Titration

Given: Titre = 22.55 cm³; burette absolute uncertainty = ±0.05 cm³ per reading.
Absolute uncertainty in titre: 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.10 cm³ (two readings — initial and final)
% uncertainty: $\dfrac{0.10}{22.55} \times 100 = \mathbf{0.44\%}$

1   The Mole & Amount of Substance  (2.2.1)

Key Formulae

Amount from mass$n = \dfrac{m}{M_r}$
Amount from particles$n = \dfrac{N}{N_A}$   ($N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}$ mol$^{-1}$)
Amount from gas volume (r.t.p.)$n = \dfrac{V}{24.0}$   ($V$ in dm$^3$)
Concentration$c = \dfrac{n}{V}$   ($V$ in dm$^3$);   $n = cV$
Volume unit conversions$1\ \text{dm}^3 = 1000\ \text{cm}^3$;   $1\ \text{cm}^3 = 10^{-3}\ \text{dm}^3$;   $1\ \text{m}^3 = 1000\ \text{dm}^3$

Common Examiner Errors

• Forgetting to convert cm$^3$ to dm$^3$ (divide by 1000) before using $n = cV$.
• Using $M_r$ instead of the correct molar mass (e.g. using $M_r(\text{Cl})=35.5$ instead of $M_r(\text{Cl}_2)=71$).
• Rounding intermediate values — keep full precision until the final answer.

Worked Example

A compound contains only C, H and O. A sample of 0.720 g contains $1.44 \times 10^{22}$ atoms of carbon. The relative molecular mass is 60.0. Identify the molecular formula of the compound.

Step 1 — Moles of C: $n(\text{C}) = \dfrac{1.44\times10^{22}}{6.02\times10^{23}} = 0.02393$ mol
Step 2 — Moles of compound: $n(\text{compound}) = \dfrac{0.720}{60.0} = 0.01200$ mol
Step 3 — C atoms per molecule: $\dfrac{0.02392}{0.01200} = 1.99 \approx 2$ carbon atoms per molecule
Step 4 — Identify formula: mass of 2C per mole $= 24.0$ g mol$^{-1}$; remaining $= 60.0 - 24.0 = 36.0$ g mol$^{-1}$. Try 4H + 2O: $4(1.0)+2(16.0) = 36.0$ ✓ → molecular formula $\mathbf{C_2H_4O_2}$ (e.g. ethanoic acid, methyl methanoate).

2   Empirical & Molecular Formulae; Hydrated Salts  (2.2.2)

Key Formulae & Procedure

Empirical formula method% mass → ÷ $A_r$ → divide by smallest → whole number ratio
Molecular formula$\text{Molecular formula} = (\text{Empirical formula}) \times n$, where $n = M_r / M_r(\text{EF})$
Water of crystallisation$\text{Salt} \cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}$; find $x$ from mole ratio of anhydrous salt : water lost on heating

Common Examiner Errors

• Incomplete dehydration (heating not to constant mass) → $x$ value too low.
• Thermal decomposition of the anhydrous salt → $x$ value too high.
• Rounding to whole number ratios too early (e.g. 1.5 → must double to get 3 : 2).

Worked Example 1 — Empirical Formula

A compound contains 40.0% C, 6.67% H, 53.3% O by mass.

Step 1 — Divide by $A_r$: C: $40.0/12.0=3.33$; H: $6.67/1.0=6.67$; O: $53.3/16.0=3.33$
Step 2 — Divide by smallest (3.33): C: 1; H: 2; O: 1
Answer — Empirical formula: $\mathbf{CH_2O}$. If $M_r=180$, molecular formula: $n=180/30=6$ → $\mathbf{C_6H_{12}O_6}$.

Worked Example 2 — Hydrated Salt

4.91 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate ($\text{CuSO}_4\cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}$) was heated to constant mass, giving 3.14 g of anhydrous salt.

Step 1 — Mass of water lost $= 4.91 - 3.14 = 1.77$ g
Step 2 — $n(\text{CuSO}_4)=3.14/159.6=0.01966$ mol; $n(\text{H}_2\text{O})=1.77/18.0=0.09833$ mol
Step 3 — Ratio: $0.09833/0.01966=5.0$ → $x=\mathbf{5}$; formula $= \mathbf{CuSO_4\cdot5H_2O}$

3   Reaction Calculations — mass, volume, concentration, limiting reagent  (2.2.3)

Three-Step Method

Step 1Convert given quantity to moles
Step 2Use molar ratio from balanced equation
Step 3Convert moles to required quantity
Limiting reagentCalculate moles of each reagent; the one that gives the smaller moles of product is the limiting reagent
Gas at r.t.p.$V\ (\text{dm}^3) = n \times 24.0$
Concentration$n = c \times V$   (V in dm$^3$);   $c = n/V$

Worked Example — Limiting Reagent

4.00 g of calcium reacts with 100 cm$^3$ of 1.50 mol dm$^{-3}$ HCl. Calculate the volume of H$_2$ produced at r.t.p.$\\$ $\text{Ca(s) + 2HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2\text{(aq) + H}_2\text{(g)}$

Step 1 — $n(\text{Ca})=4.00/40.1=0.0997$ mol; $n(\text{HCl})=1.50\times0.100=0.150$ mol
Step 2 — HCl needed to react all Ca $=2\times0.0997=0.199$ mol, but only 0.150 mol available → HCl is the limiting reagent
Step 3 — $n(\text{H}_2)=0.150/2=0.0750$ mol; $V=0.0750\times24.0=\mathbf{1.80\ \text{dm}^3}$

4   The Ideal Gas Equation  (2.2.4)

Key Formulae

Ideal gas equation$pV = nRT$
Gas constant$R = 8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
Units required$p$ in Pa; $V$ in m$^3$; $T$ in K ($T/\text{K} = \theta/°C + 273$)
Unit conversions$1\ \text{kPa} = 1000\ \text{Pa}$; $1\ \text{dm}^3 = 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$; $1\ \text{cm}^3 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3$
Finding $M_r$ of volatile liquid$M_r = \dfrac{mRT}{pV}$

Common Examiner Errors

• Using cm$^3$ or dm$^3$ instead of m$^3$ for volume — always convert to m$^3$.
• Leaving temperature in °C — must convert to Kelvin.
• Using kPa instead of Pa.

Worked Example

0.153 g of a volatile liquid is vaporised at 97 °C and 100 kPa, occupying a volume of 65.0 cm$^3$. Calculate the $M_r$ of the liquid.

Step 1 — Convert units: $p=100\times10^3=1.00\times10^5$ Pa; $V=65.0\times10^{-6}=6.50\times10^{-5}$ m$^3$; $T=97+273=370$ K
Step 2 — $n = pV/RT = (1.00\times10^5\times6.50\times10^{-5})/(8.314\times370) = 6.50/(3076.2) = 2.113\times10^{-3}$ mol
Step 3 — $M_r = m/n = 0.153/2.113\times10^{-3} = \mathbf{72.4}$

Practice Questions

Q2. A flask of volume 500 cm$^3$ contains 1.20 g of a gas at 27 °C and 150 kPa. Calculate the $M_r$ of the gas. [3]
Q3. Calculate the pressure, in kPa, exerted by 2.00 mol of gas in a 50.0 dm$^3$ container at 500 K. [2]
Mark Scheme
A1. $V=nRT/p=(0.500\times8.314\times298)/(101\times10^3)=1.239\times10^{-2}$ m$^3$ ✓ $=\mathbf{12.4\ \text{dm}^3}$ ✓
A2. $n=pV/RT=(150\times10^3\times500\times10^{-6})/(8.314\times300)=75/(2494.2)=0.03007$ mol ✓✓; $M_r=1.20/0.03007=\mathbf{39.9}$ ✓
A3. $p=nRT/V=(2.00\times8.314\times500)/(50.0\times10^{-3})=8314/0.0500=\mathbf{166\ \text{kPa}}$ ✓✓
5   Percentage Yield & Atom Economy  (2.2.5)

Key Formulae

Percentage yield$\%\ \text{yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual moles (or mass) of product}}{\text{theoretical moles (or mass) of product}} \times 100$
Atom economy$\%\ \text{atom economy} = \dfrac{M_r\ \text{of desired product}}{\sum M_r\ \text{of all products}} \times 100$
Addition reactionsAlways have 100% atom economy (only one product)

Common Examiner Errors

• Using $\sum M_r$ of reactants (not products) as the denominator in atom economy.
• Forgetting to multiply $M_r$ by stoichiometric coefficients when calculating $\sum M_r$ products.

Worked Example

Ethanol can be made by fermenting glucose: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow \text{2C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH + 2CO}_2$
If 36.8 g of ethanol is produced from 90.0 g of glucose, calculate (a) the % yield and (b) the % atom economy.

(a) % yield — Theoretical: $n(\text{glucose})=90.0/180.2=0.4994$ mol; $n(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH})_\text{theoretical}=2\times0.4994=0.9989$ mol; mass $=0.9989\times46.1=46.1$ g. $\%\ \text{yield}=36.8/46.1\times100=\mathbf{79.8\%}$
(b) % atom economy — $\sum M_r(\text{products})=2\times46.1+2\times44.0=92.2+88.0=180.2$. $\%\text{AE}=(2\times46.1)/180.2\times100=92.2/180.2\times100=\mathbf{51.2\%}$

Practice Questions

Q2. 12.5 g of aspirin ($M_r=180$) is produced from 15.0 g of salicylic acid ($M_r=138$). Calculate the % yield. [3]
Q3. Chlorine reacts with ethene by addition: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_2\text{ClCH}_2\text{Cl}$. State the % atom economy and explain why. [2]
Mark Scheme
A1. Only one product, so $\%\text{AE} = \mathbf{100\%}$ (addition reaction) ✓
A2. Theoretical: $n(\text{sal})=15.0/138=0.1087$ mol; theoretical $m(\text{aspirin})=0.1087\times180=19.57$ g ✓; $\%\text{yield}=12.5/19.57\times100=\mathbf{63.9\%}$ ✓✓
A3. $\mathbf{100\%}$ ✓; because it is an addition reaction — the only product is 1,2-dichloroethane, so all atoms are incorporated into the desired product ✓
6   Acid–Base Titrations  (2.3.2)

Key Formulae & Rules

Moles from titration$n = c \times V$   (V in dm$^3$)
Concordant titresReadings within $\pm 0.10$ cm$^3$ of each other (use mean of concordant values only)
Burette precisionRead to $\pm 0.05$ cm$^3$; titre uncertainty $=\pm 0.10$ cm$^3$
Scale-upIf 25.0 cm$^3$ aliquot from 250 cm$^3$ → multiply moles in aliquot $\times 10$ for whole sample

Common Examiner Errors

• Including a rough titre in the mean — only use concordant runs.
• Forgetting to convert cm$^3$ to dm$^3$ (divide by 1000) before calculating moles.
• Not scaling up from the aliquot to the whole solution.

Worked Example

25.0 cm$^3$ aliquots of an impure Na$_2$CO$_3$ solution were titrated against 0.100 mol dm$^{-3}$ HCl. Three concordant titres of 23.60, 23.55, 23.65 cm$^3$ were obtained. The solution was made from 2.00 g of impure Na$_2$CO$_3$ dissolved in 250 cm$^3$. Calculate the purity of the sample.
$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + \text{2HCl} \rightarrow \text{2NaCl + H}_2\text{O + CO}_2$

Step 1 — Mean titre $= (23.60+23.55+23.65)/3 = 23.60$ cm$^3$
Step 2 — $n(\text{HCl})=0.100\times(23.60/1000)=2.360\times10^{-3}$ mol
Step 3 — $n(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3)=2.360\times10^{-3}/2=1.180\times10^{-3}$ mol in 25.0 cm$^3$
Step 4 — Scale up: $\times 10 = 1.180\times10^{-2}$ mol in 250 cm$^3$; $m=1.180\times10^{-2}\times106.0=1.251$ g
Step 5 — Purity $=1.251/2.00\times100=\mathbf{62.6\%}$

A2. $n(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4)=0.100\times(20.00/1000)=2.00\times10^{-3}$ mol ✓; $n(\text{KOH})=2\times2.00\times10^{-3}=4.00\times10^{-3}$ mol in 25.0 cm$^3$ ✓; $\times10$: $4.00\times10^{-2}$ mol total; $m=4.00\times10^{-2}\times56.1=2.244$ g ✓; purity $=2.244/2.81\times100=\mathbf{79.9\%}$ ✓
7   Calorimetry  (3.4.2)

Key Formulae

Heat transferred$q = mc\Delta T$
Specific heat capacity of water$c = 4.18\ \text{J g}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
$m$ in calorimetryMass of the solution (not just the water); assume density $= 1.00\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$
Enthalpy change per mole$\Delta H = -q/n$   (sign: exothermic $\Rightarrow \Delta H < 0$)

Common Examiner Errors

• Using the mass of water alone instead of the total mass of solution.
• Using the mass of solid solute as $m$ in $q=mc\Delta T$.
• Incorrect sign: if temperature rises, reaction is exothermic, $\Delta H$ must be negative.
• Not converting $q$ from J to kJ before quoting $\Delta H$.

Worked Example

50.0 cm$^3$ of 1.00 mol dm$^{-3}$ HCl is mixed with 50.0 cm$^3$ of 1.00 mol dm$^{-3}$ NaOH. Temperature rises by 6.80 °C. Calculate the enthalpy of neutralisation.

Step 1 — $m(\text{solution})=(50.0+50.0)\times1.00=100.0\ \text{g}$
Step 2 — $q=mc\Delta T=100.0\times4.18\times6.80=2842\ \text{J}=2.842\ \text{kJ}$
Step 3 — $n(\text{H}_2\text{O formed})=1.00\times(50.0/1000)=0.0500$ mol
Step 4 — $\Delta H=-2.842/0.0500=\mathbf{-56.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}}$

Practice Questions

Q3. Explain why the experimental value for enthalpy of combustion is always less exothermic than the data book value. [2]
Mark Scheme
A1. $m\approx200\ \text{g}$ (assume mass of solution $\approx$ mass of HCl solution; Mg dissolves, small correction) ✓; $q=200\times4.18\times19.2=16051\ \text{J}=16.05\ \text{kJ}$ ✓; $n(\text{Mg})=2.40/24.3=0.09877$ mol ✓; $\Delta H=-16.05/0.09877=\mathbf{-163\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}}$ ✓
A2. $q=200\times4.18\times35.0=29260\ \text{J}=29.26\ \text{kJ}$ ✓; $n(\text{ethanol})=0.920/46.1=0.01995$ mol ✓; $\Delta H_c=-29.26/0.01995=\mathbf{-1467\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}}$ ✓✓
A3. Heat is lost to the surroundings / to the container ✓; combustion may be incomplete (not all ethanol burns fully) ✓
8   Bond Enthalpies  (3.4.3)

Key Formulae

$\Delta_r H$ from bond enthalpies$\Delta_r H = \sum E(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum E(\text{bonds made})$
Definition of bond enthalpyEnergy to break 1 mol of a given bond in gaseous molecules, averaged over many compounds

Common Examiner Errors

• Reversing the formula (bonds made − bonds broken) — gives wrong sign.
• Forgetting to multiply individual bond enthalpies by the number of bonds of that type.
• Using bond enthalpies for non-gaseous species — the method is only valid for gaseous reactants and products.

Worked Example — Methane Combustion

$\text{CH}_4\text{(g) + 2O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\text{(g) + 2H}_2\text{O(g)}$

9   Hess's Law  (3.4.4)

Key Formulae

Formation cycle$\Delta_r H = \sum \Delta_f H(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta_f H(\text{reactants})$
Combustion cycle$\Delta_r H = \sum \Delta_c H(\text{reactants}) - \sum \Delta_c H(\text{products})$
Note on elements$\Delta_f H = 0$ for elements in their standard state
StoichiometryMultiply $\Delta H$ values by stoichiometric coefficients; reverse a reaction → change sign of $\Delta H$

Common Examiner Errors

• Mixing up the combustion cycle formula — remember: reactants minus products (opposite to formation).
• Not multiplying $\Delta_c H$ or $\Delta_f H$ by stoichiometric coefficients.
• Sign errors when reversing arrows in the Hess cycle.

12   The Arrhenius Equation  (5.1.7)

Key Formulae

Key Formulae

16   Born–Haber Cycles  (5.4.3)

Key Definitions & Cycle Steps

Lattice enthalpy of formation ($\Delta_{LE}H$)Enthalpy change when 1 mol of ionic solid forms from its gaseous ions under standard conditions; always exothermic (negative)
Factors affecting $|\Delta_{LE}H|$Ionic charge (dominant) — higher charge → more exothermic; ionic radius — smaller → more exothermic
Cycle (Hess's Law)$\Delta_f H = \Delta_{at}H(\text{metal}) + \Delta_{at}H(\text{non-metal}) + \text{IE} + \text{EA} + \Delta_{LE}H$
Finding $\Delta_{LE}H$Rearrange: $\Delta_{LE}H = \Delta_f H - [\Delta_{at}H + \text{IE} + \Delta_{at}H(\frac{1}{2}\text{X}_2) + \text{EA}]$
MgCl$_2$ — key pointMust use IE$_1$ + IE$_2$ for Mg; must double $\Delta_{at}H(\text{Cl})$ and EA(Cl)

• Forgetting to double (or triple) enthalpy terms for compounds with more than one halide ion (e.g. MgCl$_2$ needs ×2 for Cl steps).
• Describing lattice enthalpy as "higher/lower" — say "more/less exothermic" or "more/less negative".
• Getting the sign of EA wrong: EA for O (first) is exothermic; EA for O$^{2-}$ (second) is endothermic.

18   Entropy  (5.4.6)

Key Formulae

Entropy change$\Delta S = \sum S^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum S^\circ(\text{reactants})$
UnitsJ K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$
Order of entropy$S^\circ(\text{gas}) \gg S^\circ(\text{liquid}) > S^\circ(\text{solid}) > 0$
Key rule$S^\circ$ of elements and compounds is always positive; $\Delta S$ can be negative
Predicting sign of $\Delta S$Increase in gas moles → positive $\Delta S$; decrease → negative $\Delta S$

Common Examiner Errors

• Confusing $S^\circ$ (always positive) with $\Delta S$ (can be negative).
• Forgetting to convert $\Delta S$ from J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$ to kJ K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$ before using in $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$.

Worked Example

$\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s)} \rightarrow \text{CaO(s) + CO}_2\text{(g)}$
$S^\circ$: CaCO$_3$ = 92.9; CaO = 39.7; CO$_2$ = 213.7 J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$

$\Delta S = [39.7+213.7]-[92.9]=253.4-92.9=\mathbf{+160.5\ \text{J K}^{-1}\ \text{mol}^{-1}}$
Positive $\Delta S$ is expected: 1 mol of gas produced from a solid.

19   Gibbs Free Energy  (5.4.8)

Key Formulae

Gibbs equation$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$
Units$\Delta H$ in kJ mol$^{-1}$; $\Delta S$ must be converted: ÷1000 to get kJ K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$; $T$ in K
FeasibilityReaction is feasible (thermodynamically spontaneous) when $\Delta G < 0$
Minimum temperature$T_\text{min} = \Delta H / \Delta S$   (when $\Delta H > 0$ and $\Delta S > 0$)
Link to equilibrium$\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$

Sign Combinations

$\Delta H < 0$, $\Delta S > 0$Always feasible (all $T$)
$\Delta H > 0$, $\Delta S < 0$Never feasible
$\Delta H < 0$, $\Delta S < 0$Feasible at low $T$
$\Delta H > 0$, $\Delta S > 0$Feasible at high $T$ (above $T_\text{min}$)

Common Examiner Errors

• Not converting $\Delta S$ from J to kJ before substituting into $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$.
• Stating a reaction is "feasible" when $\Delta G > 0$ — it must be negative.
• Forgetting kinetic limitations: a negative $\Delta G$ does not guarantee a fast reaction.

Worked Example

For CaCO$_3$(s) → CaO(s) + CO$_2$(g): $\Delta H=+178$ kJ mol$^{-1}$; $\Delta S=+160.5$ J K$^{-1}$ mol$^{-1}$.
(a) Calculate $\Delta G$ at 298 K. (b) Estimate the minimum temperature for feasibility.

(a) $\Delta G=+178-(298\times0.1605)=178-47.8=\mathbf{+130\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}}$ — not feasible at 298 K
(b) $T_\text{min}=\Delta H/\Delta S=178\times10^3/160.5=\mathbf{1109\ \text{K}}$ (≈ 836 °C)

20   Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis  (5.5.1)

Key Formulae

Charge$Q = It$   ($Q$ in C, $I$ in A, $t$ in s)
Moles of electrons$n(\text{e}^-) = \dfrac{Q}{F}$   ($F = 96\,500$ C mol$^{-1}$)
Moles of elementUse the half-equation: e.g. Cu$^{2+}$ + 2e$^-$ → Cu means $n(\text{Cu}) = \frac{1}{2}n(\text{e}^-)$
Mass deposited$m = n \times M_r$

Common Examiner Errors

• Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds before calculating $Q = It$.
• Not dividing by the number of electrons in the half-equation (e.g. 2 for Cu$^{2+}$, 3 for Al$^{3+}$).
• Using $F = 96\,500$ C mol$^{-1}$ — check the data sheet value if given differently.

Worked Example — Mass of Copper Deposited

A current of 0.500 A is passed through a solution of CuSO$_4$ for 20.0 minutes. Calculate the mass of copper deposited at the cathode. ($M_r(\text{Cu})=63.5$)

Step 1 — Charge: $Q = It = 0.500 \times (20.0 \times 60) = 0.500 \times 1200 = 600$ C
Step 2 — Moles of e⁻: $n(\text{e}^-) = \dfrac{600}{96\,500} = 6.218\times10^{-3}$ mol
Step 3 — Half-equation: Cu$^{2+}$ + 2e$^-$ → Cu; $n(\text{Cu}) = \dfrac{6.218\times10^{-3}}{2} = 3.109\times10^{-3}$ mol
Step 4 — Mass: $m = 3.109\times10^{-3} \times 63.5 = \mathbf{0.197\ \text{g}}$

22   Electrode Potential Calculations  (5.5.4)

Key Formulae

Cell EMF$E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ(\text{more positive/right hand electrode}) - E^\circ(\text{less positive/left hand electrode})$
FeasibilityReaction is feasible if $E^\circ_\text{cell} > 0$
Important$E^\circ$ values are intensive — do not multiply by stoichiometric coefficients
Standard conditions298 K, 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm$^{-3}$ ion concentrations

Common Examiner Errors

• Multiplying $E^\circ$ values by stoichiometric coefficients — they do not change.
• Predicting feasibility without acknowledging kinetic limitations (e.g. high $E_a$ may prevent reaction).
• Using "higher/lower" instead of "more positive/less negative" when comparing $E^\circ$ values.

Worked Example — Daniell Cell

$\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}$: $E^\circ=+0.34$ V; $\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}$: $E^\circ=-0.76$ V.
Calculate $E^\circ_\text{cell}$ and write the overall cell reaction.

$E^\circ_\text{cell}$ $= +0.34-(-0.76)=+0.34+0.76=\mathbf{+1.10\ \text{V}}$
Overall: Zn is oxidised (less positive); Cu$^{2+}$ is reduced: $\text{Zn(s)+Cu}^{2+}\text{(aq)}\rightarrow\text{Zn}^{2+}\text{(aq)+Cu(s)}$
