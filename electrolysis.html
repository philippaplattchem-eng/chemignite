🔋 3.22–3.24 What is electrolysis? F + H ▼

Electrolysis means using electricity to break apart an ionic compound. The compound has to be melted or dissolved in water first — so the ions can move freely.

The basic setup

The PANIC mnemonic — never mix them up again!

The biggest cause of lost marks in electrolysis is getting the cathode and anode the wrong way round. Use PANIC:

⚠️ Watch out! The June 2019 examiner report said electrode confusion was the single biggest source of lost marks in electrolysis questions. Write PANIC at the top of any electrolysis answer!

Key vocabulary

WordWhat it means
ElectrolysisUsing electricity to break down (decompose) an ionic compound
ElectrolyteThe ionic substance being broken down — must be molten or dissolved in water
ElectrodeA conductor (usually graphite or metal) that electricity enters or leaves through
CathodeThe negative (−) electrode — cations move here and gain electrons
AnodeThe positive (+) electrode — anions move here and lose electrons
CationA positively charged ion (e.g. Na+, Cu2+) — moves toward the cathode

Inert electrodeAn electrode (usually graphite/carbon) that does not react with the products

Why must the compound be molten or dissolved?

In a solid ionic compound, the ions are locked in a lattice — they can't move. Electricity is carried by moving charges, so we need free-moving ions. We can free them by:

  • Melting the compound — the lattice breaks down and ions become mobile
  • Dissolving in water — water molecules pull the ions apart, freeing them to move
💡 Tip: "Electrolyte" is the liquid between the electrodes, not the electrodes themselves. Students sometimes call the electrodes the electrolyte — they're not!
✅ Quick check

Which electrode do negative ions move towards — cathode or anode?

The anode — it is positive (+), so it attracts negative ions. Remember PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.
🔥 3.25–3.26 Electrolysis of molten compounds F + H ▼

Melt an ionic compound, pass electricity through it, and you always get the metal at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode. Simple rule — no competition!

The classic example: molten lead bromide (PbBr2)

Lead bromide is an ionic compound made of Pb2+ and Br− ions. When you melt it, those ions become free to move. Connect a power supply and electrolysis happens.

Figure 2 — Electrolysis of molten lead bromide. Lead metal forms at the cathode; orange-brown bromine gas forms at the anode.

What happens at each electrode?

Cathode (−)Pb2+Lead metal (grey liquid)Pb2+ ions gain 2 electrons each → become neutral Pb atoms
Anode (+)Br−Bromine gas (Br2)Br− ions lose 1 electron each → pairs join to form Br2 molecules

The general rule — easy!

For any molten binary ionic compound (one metal + one non-metal):

  • 🔵 Cathode (−): always the metal element
  • 🟠 Anode (+): always the non-metal element
CompoundFormulaCathode productAnode product
Lead bromidePbBr2Lead (Pb)Bromine (Br2)
Copper chlorideCuCl2Copper (Cu)Chlorine (Cl2)

💡 Tip: Notice Cl2, Br2, O2 — the non-metal gases form as diatomic molecules. Always write the subscript 2. Writing just "Cl" or "O" will lose you a mark!
✅ Quick check

Molten copper(II) chloride is electrolysed using inert electrodes. What is produced at each electrode?

Cathode: Copper (Cu) — Cu2+ ions are attracted to the negative electrode, gain 2 electrons each, and become copper metal.
Anode: Chlorine gas (Cl2) — Cl− ions are attracted to the positive electrode, each loses 1 electron, and pairs join to form Cl2.
💧 3.25 Electrolysis of aqueous solutions F + H ▼

When an ionic compound is dissolved in water, you have extra ions from the water itself — H+ and OH−. You need to decide which ions get discharged at each electrode using a simple set of rules.

Why aqueous solutions are trickier

Water itself partly splits up:  H2O ⇌ H+ + OH−

So any aqueous solution always has at least four competing ions:

  • From the dissolved salt: a metal cation (e.g. Na+, Cu2+) and an anion (e.g. Cl−, SO42−)
  • From the water: H+ ions and OH− ions

⚠️ Common mistake: Students often say "oxygen comes from SO42−" or "sulfate is discharged at the anode." This is wrong! Sulfate ions are never discharged. The oxygen actually comes from OH− ions (from water).
✅ Quick check

Aqueous sodium chloride is electrolysed using inert electrodes. State the product at the anode and explain why.

Chlorine gas (Cl2). Chloride ions (Cl−) are halide ions, so they are preferentially discharged at the anode over OH− ions. Each Cl− loses one electron and pairs join to form Cl2 molecules.
⚗️ 3.27–3.29 Half-equations and redox Higher only ▼
H Higher tier only. Foundation students can skip this section — but it's great extension if you want a challenge!

Half-equations show exactly which electrons move where at each electrode. At the cathode, ions gain electrons (reduction). At the anode, ions lose electrons (oxidation). Remember: OIL RIG!

OIL RIG — your electron mnemonic

OIL Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons)
RIG Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)
  • 🔵 Cathode (−): ions gain electrons → REDUCTION
  • 🟠 Anode (+): ions lose electrons → OXIDATION

The 4-step method for writing any half-equation

  1. Write the ion on the left and the product on the right, separated by →
  2. Add electrons (e−) to balance the charges — they go on the left for cathode, right for anode
  3. Balance atoms if needed (especially H2, O2 and H2O)
  4. Check: same number of atoms and same total charge on each side

All the half-equations you need

ElectrodeIonHalf-equationType
CathodeCu2+Cu2+ + 2e− → CuReduction
CathodeH+2H+ + 2e− → H2Reduction
CathodeNa+Na+ + e− → NaReduction
CathodePb2+Pb2+ + 2e− → PbReduction
CathodeAl3+Al3+ + 3e− → AlReduction
AnodeCl−2Cl− → Cl2 + 2e−Oxidation
AnodeBr−2Br− → Br2 + 2e−Oxidation
AnodeOH−4OH− → O2 + 2H2O + 4e−Oxidation
⚠️ The 5 most common exam errors (from examiners):
  1. Putting electrons on the wrong side (cathode needs e− on the left; anode needs e− on the right)
  2. Writing Cl instead of Cl2 — forgetting atoms are diatomic
  3. Leaving out the minus sign — writing "e" instead of "e−"
  4. Unbalanced charges — always check both sides add up to the same number
  5. Writing × instead of → in the equation
✅ Quick check

Write the half-equation for the discharge of bromide ions at the anode.

2Br− → Br2 + 2e−
Charge check: Left = 2×(−1) = −2   |   Right = 0 + 2×(−1) = −2  ✓
Atom check: 2 Br each side ✓
🧪 3.30–3.31 Core Practical — electrolysis of copper sulfate F + H ▼

You need to know what happens when copper sulfate solution is electrolysed with (a) inert carbon electrodes, and (b) copper electrodes. The results are completely different — and both come up in exams!

Figure 5 — Left: inert carbon electrodes — solution loses Cu2+ and fades. Right: copper electrodes — anode dissolves and solution stays blue.

Key differences

▬ Inert (carbon/graphite) electrodes

  • Cathode: Cu2+ deposits as copper (pink coating)
  • Anode: OH− discharged → O2 gas forms
  • Blue colour fades — Cu2+ ions are used up
  • Cathode gains mass; anode unchanged

🟨 Copper electrodes

  • Cathode: Cu deposits (same as above)
  • Anode: copper dissolves → makes Cu2+ ions
  • Blue colour stays the same throughout
  • Cathode gains mass; anode loses mass equally
  • Used industrially for copper purification!

Method for the exam

  1. Set up: CuSO4 solution, two electrodes, power supply, ammeter, variable resistor
  2. Dry and weigh both electrodes on a balance before starting
  3. Set a fixed current (e.g. 0.5 A) and run for a fixed time (e.g. 20 minutes)
  4. Remove electrodes carefully, rinse with distilled water, dry gently, then re-weigh
  5. Calculate mass change: cathode should gain mass; anode loses mass if using copper
⚠️ Examiner tip: Always dry the electrodes before weighing — wet electrodes give inaccurate results. Examiners ask about sources of error in this practical, and a wet electrode is the most common answer.
💡 Real-world link: The copper electrode version is exactly how industrial copper purification works. Impure copper is the anode; it dissolves away. Pure copper deposits on the cathode. Impurities sink to the bottom as "anode sludge" and are collected (they can contain valuable metals like gold and silver!).
✅ Quick check

In the copper sulfate practical using inert electrodes, why does the blue colour of the solution fade over time?

The blue colour is due to Cu2+ ions in solution. As the experiment runs, Cu2+ ions are continually discharged at the cathode, depositing as copper metal. The concentration of Cu2+ in solution decreases, so the blue colour fades. With inert anodes, no new Cu2+ is added to replace what is lost.
🏭 3.22–3.31 Industrial uses of electrolysis F + H ▼

Electrolysis isn't just a lab experiment — it's used on a massive industrial scale. You need to know three key applications: electroplating, the chlor-alkali process, and aluminium extraction.

1. Electroplating ✏️

Electroplating coats an object with a thin layer of metal using electrolysis. It's used for jewellery (gold-plated items), cutlery (silver-plated), car bumpers (chrome-plated) and circuit boards.

Figure 6 — Electroplating with gold. The object is the cathode; the plating metal is the anode; the electrolyte is a solution of gold ions.

📌 The three things to know for electroplating:
  • Cathode (−): the object you want to plate
  • Anode (+): the metal you want to plate it with (it gradually dissolves)
  • Electrolyte: a solution containing ions of the plating metal

2. The chlor-alkali process 💧

Electrolysis of brine (concentrated sodium chloride solution) produces three very useful industrial chemicals from one process:

ProductWhere it formsIndustrial uses
Chlorine (Cl2)Anode (+)Disinfecting drinking water and swimming pools; making PVC plastic; making bleach
Hydrogen (H2)Cathode (−)Fuel cells; making margarine (food industry); Haber process (making ammonia)
Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)Remains in solutionMaking soap and detergents; paper manufacture; oven cleaner; ceramics
💡 Why does NaOH form? The Na+ ions and OH− ions that aren't discharged at the electrodes stay in solution. Once Cl2 and H2 are removed, what's left is sodium hydroxide solution. Three useful products from electrolysing one simple salt!

3. Extraction of aluminium 🎮

Aluminium is too reactive to be extracted by carbon reduction — it sits above carbon in the reactivity series. Instead, we use electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide (Al2O3).

  • The ore (bauxite) is purified to get Al2O3 (alumina)
  • Al2O3 has a very high melting point (~2072°C), so it is dissolved in molten cryolite to lower the working temperature to ~950°C — saving huge amounts of energy
  • Cathode (−):  Al3+ + 3e− → Al   (liquid aluminium sinks to the bottom and is tapped off)
  • Anode (+):  2O2− → O2 + 4e−   (oxygen forms, then reacts with hot graphite anodes)
  • The carbon anodes burn away and must be replaced regularly — this adds to production costs
⚠️ Common mistake: Students say the anode "erodes by electrolysis." It doesn't — the oxygen produced at the anode reacts with the hot graphite (carbon) to form CO2 gas, burning the electrode away. This is a chemical reaction, not electrolysis.
✅ Quick check

In the electroplating of a copper bracelet with silver: (a) which is the cathode? (b) what is the electrolyte?

(a) The copper bracelet is the cathode — it is connected to the negative (−) terminal. Silver ions will be discharged here and deposit onto the bracelet.
(b) A solution of a silver salt (e.g. silver nitrate, AgNO3) — it must contain Ag+ ions so they can be discharged at the cathode.
🎯 Exam Exam technique — electrolysis F + H ▼

The examiner's most common complaints

  1. Getting cathode and anode backwards. Use PANIC at the top of every answer — don't rely on memory alone.
  2. Saying "sulfate is discharged" at the anode. Sulfate (SO42−) is never discharged. Oxygen comes from OH− ions.
  3. Writing O instead of O2 (and Cl instead of Cl2, H instead of H2, Br instead of Br2). Non-metal gases are diatomic!
  4. Confusing molten and aqueous rules. The 4-ion competition only applies to aqueous solutions — not molten compounds.
  5. [H] Half-equation errors: electrons on the wrong side, unbalanced atoms, "e" written without the minus superscript.
