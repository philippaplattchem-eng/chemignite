  • write the formulae of common ionic compounds from memory
  • balance symbol equations and ionic equations with state symbols
  • identify common ions and gases from their tests
  • recognise basic organic functional groups (alkane, alkene, alcohol, carboxylic acid)
How to work through it: aim for roughly 30–45 minutes per section. Do every practice question with a pen — do not just read them. The answers are at the back; only look once you have tried. If you get something wrong, write a one-line note about why.

1.6 Calculating relative atomic mass (Ar)

The relative atomic mass of an element is the weighted mean mass of its atoms, taking isotopic abundance into account, compared to &frac{1}{12} the mass of a 12C atom. This is why most Ar values on the periodic table are not whole numbers — elements are mixtures of isotopes.

Ar = Σ(isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100

You will use this constantly in Module 2 (mass spectrometry, isotopes) and again when interpreting mass spectra of organic compounds in Year 13. The abundance data usually comes from a mass spectrum — the peak height (or relative intensity) gives the % abundance and the m/z value gives the isotope mass.

Worked example: chlorine. A mass spectrum shows two peaks: 35Cl at 75.0% abundance and 37Cl at 25.0% abundance.

Ar = (75.0 × 35 + 25.0 × 37) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 35.5

OCR examiner-flagged error (H432/01 June 2023): students confuse % abundance with relative abundance. If you are given relative abundances (e.g. peaks of height 3 and 1), the divisor is the sum of the abundances (here 4), not 100. Always check what the question gives you.

Practice 1F: Calculating Ar

  1. Bromine has two isotopes: 79Br (50.5% abundance) and 81Br (49.5% abundance). Calculate the Ar of bromine to 3 significant figures.
  2. Copper has two isotopes: 63Cu (69.2%) and 65Cu (30.8%). Calculate the Ar of copper to 1 decimal place.
  3. Boron exists as 10B (19.9%) and 11B (80.1%). Calculate the Ar of boron.
  4. The mass spectrum of magnesium shows three peaks: m/z 24 (79.0%), m/z 25 (10.0%) and m/z 26 (11.0%). Calculate the Ar of magnesium.

  7. The Ar of chlorine is 35.5. Chlorine has two isotopes, 35Cl and 37Cl. Calculate the percentage abundance of each isotope.

2.2 Building the formula: the crossover rule

Ionic compounds are always neutral — the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. The quickest method is the crossover rule (OCR's official terminology): the magnitude of one ion's charge becomes the subscript of the other. Simplify the ratio if both subscripts share a factor; drop subscripts of 1.

Worked example 1: aluminium oxide: Al3+ and O2−. Crossover: Al2O3.

Worked example 2: with compound ions: calcium nitrate: Ca2+ and NO3− → Ca(NO3)2. Use brackets around the polyatomic ion when there are two or more.

Worked example 3: simplification trap: barium sulfate: Ba2+ and SO42−. Crossover gives Ba2(SO4)2, but both subscripts share a factor of 2, simplify to BaSO4. Always check your final formula is in its lowest ratio.

OCR examiner-flagged errors (H032/02 June 2022): writing element symbols in the wrong case ("br" instead of Br, "na" instead of Na), and writing "SrOH" instead of Sr(OH)2 — Sr2+ needs two OH− to balance the charge. Always: one capital then one lowercase per symbol; bracket polyatomic ions when there is more than one.

3. Balancing equations

A balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom on both sides. You can only change the big numbers in front (the stoichiometric coefficients). Never change a formula to balance an equation.

3.1 A reliable method

  1. Write the correct formulae for all reactants and products.
  2. List the elements present and count atoms on both sides.
  3. Balance metals first, then non-metals (other than H and O), then H, then O.
  4. Check.

3.4 Ionic equations

An ionic equation shows only the ions that actually take part — the spectator ions are cancelled out. Charges as well as atoms must balance.

Worked example: AgNO3 (aq) + NaCl (aq) → AgCl (s) + NaNO3 (aq)

Spectator ions are Na+ and NO3−. The ionic equation is: Ag+ (aq) + Cl− (aq) → AgCl (s).

4. Chemical tests

You need to know the test, the observation, and (often) the ionic equation for the reaction taking place. OCR examines this in both Module 2 (PAG 4) and Module 3 (Group 17), and routinely sets 6-mark "logic puzzle" questions where you must identify an unknown from a sequence of test results.

The strict OCR anion test order: carbonate → sulfate → halide, on three separate portions of the unknown. Get the order wrong and you trigger false positives (carbonate would precipitate with both Ba2+ and Ag+). This sequencing logic is itself a regular exam mark.

4.1 Tests for cations: flame tests (OCR PAG 4)

Dip a clean nichrome (or platinum) wire into concentrated HCl, then into the solid, then into a roaring (blue) Bunsen flame.

IonFlame colour

Na+yellow / orange
K+lilac (view through cobalt-blue glass to remove any Na+ interference)
Ca2+brick red / orange-red
Sr2+red (slightly crimson)
Ba2+apple green
Cu2+blue-green

4.2 Test for ammonium ions, NH4+

Warm the unknown with sodium hydroxide solution. Hold a piece of damp red litmus paper in the mouth of the test tube (do not let it touch the solution). If ammonia is given off, the litmus turns blue.

Ionic equation: NH4+ (aq) + OH− (aq) → NH3 (g) + H2O (l)

The litmus must be damp — ammonia gas needs water to dissolve into and ionise. The mixture must be warmed to drive NH3 out as a gas.

4.3 Tests for cations with sodium hydroxide solution (GCSE foundation)

You will have met these at GCSE. They are not on the OCR A Level spec for cations, but the chemistry underpins the chemistry of Group 2 and transition metals later in Year 12.

4.4 Tests for anions

(i) Carbonate, CO32−: do this first

Add dilute acid (HCl or HNO3) to the unknown. If a carbonate is present, you will see effervescence — confirm the gas is CO2 by bubbling it through limewater, which turns milky.

Ionic equation: CO32− (aq) + 2H+ (aq) → H2O (l) + CO2 (g)

Limewater confirmation: Ca(OH)2 (aq) + CO2 (g) → CaCO3 (s) + H2O (l)

Effervescence alone is not enough — the limewater step is what makes the test specific.

(ii) Sulfate, SO42−: do this second

On a fresh portion, add a few drops of acidified barium chloride (acidified with dilute HCl or HNO3). A white precipitate of BaSO4 confirms sulfate.

Ionic equation: Ba2+ (aq) + SO42− (aq) → BaSO4 (s)

"Acidified" is a routine mark — the acid destroys any carbonate, which would otherwise give a white BaCO3 false positive.

(iii) Halide, Cl− / Br− / I−: do this last

On a fresh portion, add dilute nitric acid (not HCl — it would add Cl− and give a false positive), then silver nitrate solution. The colour of the precipitate identifies the halide. To confirm, add first dilute then concentrated ammonia — this is OCR spec point 3.3.3(g).

HalidePrecipitateColourDilute NH3Concentrated NH3
Cl−AgClwhitedissolvesdissolves

I−AgIyellowinsolubleinsoluble

Ionic equation (X = Cl, Br, I): Ag+ (aq) + X− (aq) → AgX (s)

The trend reflects decreasing solubility of the silver halide down Group 17 — AgI's lattice is too stable for even concentrated NH3 to dissolve it.

5. Basic organic chemistry

You need to recognise the main GCSE functional groups and be able to draw and name short members of each family. At A Level you will build on this with mechanisms, isomerism and many more functional groups.

5.1 Alkanes

General formula: CnH2n+2. Single bonds only; saturated.

nNameFormula
1methaneCH4
2ethaneC2H6
3propaneC3H8
4butaneC4H10
5pentaneC5H12
6hexaneC6H14

5.2 Alkenes

General formula: CnH2n. Contains a C=C double bond; unsaturated. Decolourises orange bromine water (orange → colourless) — this is the test for a C=C bond.

Names: ethene (C2H4), propene (C3H6), but-1-ene (C4H8), pent-1-ene (C5H10), hex-1-ene (C6H12).

From C4 onwards a locant is required because the C=C can sit in different positions: but-1-ene has C=C between C1 and C2; but-2-ene has C=C between C2 and C3. OCR requires the locant in every IUPAC name where ambiguity is possible.

5.3 Alcohols

Functional group: –OH. Names end in -ol. Methanol (CH3OH), ethanol (C2H5OH), propan-1-ol (CH3CH2CH2OH), butan-1-ol (CH3CH2CH2CH2OH).

As with alkenes, locants are required from C3 onwards because the –OH could sit on different carbons (propan-1-ol vs propan-2-ol). Methanol and ethanol need no locant because only one isomer is possible.

Alcohols burn in oxygen, react with sodium to release hydrogen, and can be oxidised by acidified potassium dichromate(VI) to carbonyls or carboxylic acids (Year 12 work).

5.4 Carboxylic acids

Functional group: –COOH. Names end in -oic acid. Methanoic acid (HCOOH), ethanoic acid (CH3COOH), propanoic acid (CH3CH2COOH), butanoic acid (CH3CH2CH2COOH).

They are weak acids — they show all the typical acid reactions (with metals, carbonates, alkalis). No locant is needed because the –COOH carbon is C1 by convention.

Drawing rules (OCR examiner-flagged, H032/02 June 2024): C has 4 bonds, O has 2 bonds, H has 1 bond. When drawing –OH, the O must be bonded to the C — never draw "OH–C" with the H pointing inwards. This loses the connectivity mark every time.
Why this matters: roughly half of OCR A Level Chemistry is organic. Year 12 starts with alkanes and alkenes, then haloalkanes and alcohols. Coming in fluent on the families above, with correct locants, will save you huge amounts of time.

4B: Whole-sample ID

  1. Sodium sulfate, Na2SO4 (yellow flame → Na+; white precipitate with BaCl2/HCl → SO42−)
  2. Iron(II) chloride, FeCl2 (pale green solution + green precipitate with NaOH → Fe2+; white precipitate that dissolves in dilute NH3 → AgCl → Cl−)
  3. Calcium carbonate, CaCO3 (gas turning limewater milky → CO32−; brick-red flame → Ca2+). Equation: CaCO3 (s) + 2HNO3 (aq) → Ca(NO3)2 (aq) + H2O (l) + CO2 (g)

4C: OCR halide + ammonia step

  1. Bromide, Br−. Ionic equation: Ag+ (aq) + Br− (aq) → AgBr (s).
  2. Iodide, I−. (AgI is so insoluble that even concentrated ammonia cannot dissolve it.)
  3. Hydrochloric acid would introduce Cl− ions, which would react with Ag+ to form a white AgCl precipitate even if no halide were present — giving a false positive every time. Dilute nitric acid destroys any carbonate without adding any halide.
  4. Carbonate reacts with both Ba2+ and Ag+ to give white precipitates (BaCO3, Ag2CO3), so it would give a false positive in both the sulfate and halide tests. Removing carbonate first, then sulfate, ensures each test only responds to the ion it is designed for.
  5. A white precipitate could also be BaCO3 (from any carbonate present). Acidifying with dilute HCl or HNO3 first destroys any carbonate, so a white precipitate that forms after acidification can only be BaSO4.
