Ions

Spec 1.22 — Recall that an ion is an atom or group of atoms with a positive or negative charge.

Spec 1.23 — Calculate the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in simple ions given the atomic number and mass number.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
IonAn atom, or a group of atoms, that has a positive or negative electrical charge because the number of electrons is no longer equal to the number of protons
CationA positively charged ion — an atom or group of atoms that has lost one or more electrons
AnionA negatively charged ion — an atom or group of atoms that has gained one or more electrons
Monatomic ionAn ion made of a single atom, e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻, Mg²⁺, O²⁻
Polyatomic ionAn ion made of two or more atoms covalently bonded together that together carry a single charge, e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, OH⁻, NH₄⁺
Atomic number (Z)The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom (or ion) — this never changes during the formation of an ion
Mass number (A)The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus — this never changes during the formation of an ion
ChargeThe net electrical imbalance on a particle; equal to (protons − electrons)

What an ion actually is

In a neutral atom the number of protons in the nucleus is exactly equal to the number of electrons in the surrounding shells. The positive charge on the protons cancels the negative charge on the electrons, so the atom as a whole has no overall charge.

An ion forms whenever this balance is broken. If an atom loses one or more electrons, the protons in its nucleus outnumber the remaining electrons and the particle becomes positively charged. If an atom gains one or more electrons, the electrons outnumber the protons and the particle becomes negatively charged. The size of the charge tells you exactly how many electrons have been transferred:

  • A charge of 1+ means one electron lost
  • A charge of 2+ means two electrons lost
  • A charge of 1− means one electron gained
  • A charge of 2− means two electrons gained
  • A charge of 3+ or 3− means three electrons transferred, and so on

The nucleus is never involved in this process. Protons and neutrons stay exactly where they were — only the electrons change. This is why the atomic number and the mass number of an ion are the same as those of the parent atom, even though the electron count is different.

Why the charge sign tells you the direction of electron transfer

A positive sign on an ion looks counter-intuitive at first because electrons are negative — students often expect that "gaining negatives" should make something positive. The opposite is true. Protons in the nucleus are fixed; the only particles that move are the electrons. Losing a negatively charged electron leaves the atom with one more proton than electron overall, so the net charge is positive. Gaining a negatively charged electron gives the atom one extra negative particle, so the net charge is negative.

A simple way to track this is:

`` charge = (number of protons) − (number of electrons) ``

If protons > electrons → positive ion (cation) If electrons > protons → negative ion (anion) If protons = electrons → neutral atom

Monatomic vs polyatomic ions

Most ions you meet at GCSE come in two flavours: those made of a single atom (monatomic) and those made of several atoms held together by covalent bonds but carrying an overall charge (polyatomic).

For polyatomic ions like sulfate (SO₄²⁻), the 2− charge belongs to the whole group rather than any one atom — you do not need to track which atom holds the electrons. For counting subatomic particles at GCSE, exam questions almost always restrict themselves to monatomic ions, which is the case the spec point 1.23 calculation focuses on.

Counting protons, neutrons and electrons in a simple ion

The rules for spec point 1.23 follow directly from what stays the same and what changes when an ion forms:

QuantityHow to find itWhat changes when an ion forms?
ProtonsEqual to the atomic number (Z)Does not change — the nucleus is untouched
NeutronsMass number − atomic number (A − Z)Does not change — the nucleus is untouched
ElectronsAtomic number − charge (signed)Does change — electrons are lost (cation) or gained (anion)

The "electrons" rule is the one that catches people out. Reading it as a single signed subtraction makes it foolproof:

`` electrons = (atomic number) − (charge, with sign) ``

So for Mg²⁺: electrons = 12 − (+2) = 10. For O²⁻: electrons = 8 − (−2) = 8 + 2 = 10. For Al³⁺: electrons = 13 − (+3) = 10.

Notice that all three of those ions have 10 electrons — they have each reached the electronic configuration of neon (2.8), which is why these ions form in the first place. Linking the electron count back to a noble-gas configuration is a quick way to sanity-check your answer.

Worked example — counting particles in an oxide ion (O²⁻)

A student is told that an oxide ion is represented as ¹⁶O²⁻. Find the number of protons, neutrons and electrons.

*Step 1 — Identify the inputs:*

  • Atomic number, Z = 8 (oxygen is the 8th element)
  • Mass number, A = 16 (the superscript on the left)
  • Charge = 2− (so two electrons have been gained)

*Step 2 — Protons:*

Protons = Z = 8. The nucleus is unchanged.

*Step 3 — Neutrons:*

Neutrons = A − Z = 16 − 8 = 8.

*Step 4 — Electrons:*

Electrons = Z − charge = 8 − (−2) = 8 + 2 = 10.

Check: the oxide ion has gained two electrons (from 8 to 10), giving it a 2.8 configuration — the same as neon. Charge sanity check: protons − electrons = 8 − 10 = −2, which matches the 2− label.

Worked example — counting particles in a heavy 2− anion (Higher-tier-style)

An ion of an element X is represented as ¹²⁵X²⁻. The ion has 54 electrons. Calculate the number of protons and the number of neutrons.

*Step 1 — Use the electron count and charge to find the number of protons:*

electrons = Z − charge, so Z = electrons + charge = 54 + (−2) = 52 protons.

Alternatively, think of it directly: the ion has gained 2 electrons to reach 54, so the parent atom (and the ion) has 54 − 2 = 52 protons.

*Step 2 — Use the mass number to find the number of neutrons:*

Neutrons = A − Z = 125 − 52 = 73 neutrons.

Why this question trips candidates up: the obvious-looking move is to read "54 electrons" and call that the number of protons. But the symbol shows a 2− charge — the ion has gained electrons, so the proton count is less than the electron count, not equal to it.

Why the nucleus never changes when an ion forms

Forming an ion is a chemical change — it only involves the outermost (valence) electrons. The strong nuclear force holding protons and neutrons together inside the nucleus is many millions of times stronger than the electrostatic forces involved in chemistry, so chemical reactions cannot add or remove protons or neutrons. Changing the nucleus is the domain of nuclear physics (radioactive decay, fission, fusion), not chemistry.

The practical consequence for spec point 1.23 is the rule of thumb:

  • Same atomic number → same element
  • Same mass number → same isotope
  • Different electron count → different ion of that isotope of that element

Examiner Report Insights

1CH0/1H — 2021 November Examiner Report (on Q9(a), the ¹²⁵X²⁻ ion calculation): "Approximately a third of responses scored both marks here, with many of the remaining answers scoring one mark. The most common mistake was not to realise that the information given was about an ion rather than an atom, and so candidates completed the calculation assuming that the element contained 54 protons. A few candidates realised that they had been given information about an ion, but then added two protons to the electron number rather than subtracting it."

Two distinct errors are flagged in this report:

  1. Ignoring the charge altogether. Candidates read "54 electrons" and treated the particle as a neutral atom, so they used Z = 54. The fix is to notice the charge symbol every single time you see one — and to ask yourself "has this gained or lost electrons?" before doing any arithmetic.
  2. Adjusting in the wrong direction. Candidates spotted the 2− charge but added 2 rather than subtracting 2 from the electron count to reach the proton number. The reliable cure is the signed equation: electrons = Z − charge, with the sign of the charge carried in. For a 2− ion: Z = electrons + (−2) = electrons − 2.

If you can write down "electrons = Z − charge (with sign)" before touching the calculator, both errors disappear.

Mathematical Skill 3b — Changing the subject of an equation

Spec point 1.23 is tagged with maths skill 3b (change the subject of an equation). Two formulae arise from this spec point, and you need to be comfortable rearranging both:

*1. The mass-number equation:*

`` Mass number (A) = Protons (Z) + Neutrons (N) ``

Rearranged to find neutrons (the most common version in exams):

`` Neutrons = Mass number − Atomic number N = A − Z ``

*2. The electron-count equation:*

`` electrons = atomic number − charge (with sign) ``

Rearranged to find protons (when the question gives you electrons and charge instead of Z directly):

`` protons = electrons + charge (with sign) Z = electrons + charge ``

Carrying the sign of the charge through the equation is critical — it is what stops the "added instead of subtracted" mistake flagged in the 2021 examiner report.

*General procedure for any "subatomic particles in an ion" calculation:*

  1. Read off the atomic number Z (lower-left or position in the periodic table)
  2. Read off the mass number A (upper-left)
  3. Read off the charge (upper-right, including its sign)
  4. Protons = Z (no adjustment, ever)
  5. Neutrons = A − Z
  6. Electrons = Z − charge (signed)
  7. Sanity check: protons − electrons must equal the charge you read in step 3
Key Takeaways
  1. An ion is an atom or group of atoms with a positive or negative charge, caused by an imbalance between the numbers of protons and electrons.
  2. Forming an ion changes only the electrons — protons and neutrons in the nucleus stay exactly the same as in the parent atom.
  3. A cation (positive ion) has lost one or more electrons; an anion (negative ion) has gained one or more electrons.
  4. The size of the charge equals the number of electrons transferred: a 2+ ion has lost 2 electrons; a 3− ion has gained 3 electrons.
  5. Protons = atomic number (Z) — this is true for both atoms and ions.
  6. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number (A − Z) — also true for both atoms and ions.
  7. Electrons = atomic number − charge (with sign) — this is the only rule where the charge matters. For Mg²⁺ that gives 12 − (+2) = 10 electrons; for O²⁻ that gives 8 − (−2) = 10 electrons.
  8. Many common ions (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, O²⁻, F⁻) reach the electronic configuration of a noble gas — a useful sanity check on your electron count.
  9. Polyatomic ions (e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺, OH⁻) carry the charge across the whole group; treat them as a unit when writing formulae.
  10. The most common exam mistake (flagged in the 1CH0/1H November 2021 Examiner Report) is treating an ion as if it were a neutral atom — always check for the charge symbol before counting protons.
  11. The second most common mistake is adjusting electrons in the wrong direction; writing "electrons = Z − charge (signed)" before you start avoids this.
  12. Exam questions on this spec point are usually short (1–3 marks) and appear early in Paper 1; the typical task is a calculate question asking for protons, neutrons or electrons given the mass number, atomic number and charge.
1.3.1.2 Formation of Ions F+H ▼

Formation of Ions

Spec 1.24: Explain the formation of ions in ionic compounds from their atoms, limited to compounds of elements in groups 1, 2, 6 and 7. Foundation + Higher · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
IonA charged particle formed when an atom (or group of atoms) loses or gains one or more electrons
CationA positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses one or more electrons
AnionA negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains one or more electrons
Electron transferThe movement of one or more electrons from a metal atom to a non-metal atom during the formation of an ionic compound
Noble gas configurationA full outer shell of electrons, the same arrangement as the nearest noble gas (2, 2.8 or 2.8.8) — the stable state that ions achieve
Ionic compoundA compound made of oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction

Why atoms form ions in the first place

Atoms are most stable when they have a full outer shell of electrons, the same arrangement as a noble gas in group 0. Most atoms do not start out with a full outer shell, so they react to achieve one. For elements in groups 1, 2, 6 and 7, the easiest way to reach a noble gas configuration is by transferring electrons between a metal atom and a non-metal atom.

  • Metals in groups 1 and 2 have only 1 or 2 electrons in their outer shell. It takes less energy to lose those few outer electrons than to gain six or seven new ones. Losing electrons leaves the next shell down — which is already full — as the new outer shell.
  • Non-metals in groups 6 and 7 have 6 or 7 electrons in their outer shell. They only need to gain 2 or 1 electrons to fill the outer shell, which is much easier than losing six or seven.

When a metal and a non-metal react, the metal hands its outer electrons to the non-metal. Both atoms end up with full outer shells, and both atoms are now charged because the number of protons (positive) no longer equals the number of electrons (negative).

What determines the charge on an ion

The charge on a simple ion is set by the group number in the periodic table. The rule comes directly from how many electrons need to move to reach a full outer shell.

GroupElectrons in outer shellElectrons movedCharge on ionExample
Group 11Loses 1 electron1+Na → Na⁺
Group 22Loses 2 electrons2+Mg → Mg²⁺
Group 66Gains 2 electrons2−O → O²⁻
Group 77Gains 1 electron1−Cl → Cl⁻

Notice the pattern: the size of the charge equals the number of electrons transferred, and the sign (+ or −) tells you whether the atom lost (+) or gained (−) electrons.

Worked example 1 — Sodium chloride (NaCl)

Sodium is in group 1, chlorine is in group 7.

  1. Sodium atom has electronic configuration 2,8,1. To get a full outer shell, it loses the single outer electron.
  2. Chlorine atom has electronic configuration 2,8,7. It needs one more electron to fill the outer shell.
  3. Sodium transfers one electron to chlorine.
  4. Sodium now has 11 protons but only 10 electrons → charge of 1+. It is written Na⁺, with the new configuration 2,8.
  5. Chlorine now has 17 protons and 18 electrons → charge of 1−. It is written Cl⁻, with the new configuration 2,8,8.
  6. The ratio of Na⁺ to Cl⁻ is 1:1, so the formula is NaCl.

You can show this with a dot-and-cross diagram: brackets around each ion, the charge written outside the top right of each bracket, and the chlorine ion showing its eight outer-shell electrons (one of them as a dot from the original sodium electron, and the rest as crosses — or vice versa).

Worked example 2 — Magnesium oxide (MgO)

Magnesium is in group 2, oxygen is in group 6.

  1. Magnesium atom has configuration 2,8,2. It loses both outer electrons.
  2. Oxygen atom has configuration 2,6. It needs to gain two electrons.
  3. Magnesium transfers two electrons to oxygen — a single one-to-one transfer is enough here because magnesium has exactly two electrons to give and oxygen needs exactly two.
  4. Magnesium becomes Mg²⁺ (12 protons, 10 electrons), configuration 2,8.
  5. Oxygen becomes O²⁻ (8 protons, 10 electrons), configuration 2,8.
  6. The ratio of Mg²⁺ to O²⁻ is 1:1, so the formula is MgO.

Note how both ions in MgO end up with the configuration 2,8 — the same as the noble gas neon. That is exactly why this reaction is so favourable.

Worked example 3 — Magnesium chloride (MgCl₂)

Magnesium is in group 2, chlorine is in group 7. Here the numbers do not match in a 1:1 ratio, so you have to balance the electron transfer.

  1. Magnesium has two electrons to lose.
  2. Each chlorine atom can only accept one electron.
  3. To use up both of magnesium's outer electrons, you need two chlorine atoms.
  4. One Mg atom transfers one electron to each of two Cl atoms.
  5. You end up with one Mg²⁺ ion and two Cl⁻ ions.
  6. The ratio of Mg²⁺ : Cl⁻ is 1:2, so the formula is MgCl₂.

This is where the maths skill "use ratios" becomes important — the overall compound must be electrically neutral, so the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge. One 2+ ion balances two 1− ions.

Worked example 4 — Sodium oxide (Na₂O)

The reverse case: sodium is in group 1, oxygen is in group 6.

  1. Each sodium atom can only lose one electron.
  2. Oxygen needs to gain two electrons.
  3. You need two sodium atoms to supply the two electrons that one oxygen atom needs.
  4. You end up with two Na⁺ ions and one O²⁻ ion.
  5. The ratio of Na⁺ : O²⁻ is 2:1, so the formula is Na₂O.

Balancing ions in a formula — the maths skill

The general rule is straightforward and applies to every ionic compound formed from groups 1, 2, 6 and 7:

`` Total positive charge = Total negative charge ``

Procedure:

  1. Identify the group of each element and write the charge it will form (group 1 → 1+, group 2 → 2+, group 6 → 2−, group 7 → 1−).
  2. Find the smallest whole-number ratio of cations to anions that makes the total positive charge equal to the total negative charge.
  3. Write that ratio as subscripts in the formula (no subscript if the number is 1). The metal goes first.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Confusing ionic bonding with covalent bonding. Ions form by transfer of electrons, not by sharing. A dot-and-cross diagram for sodium chloride must show the electron moving from sodium to chlorine, and the resulting ions drawn in brackets with their charges — not a shared pair between the two atoms.
  • Just restating the electronic configurations without saying what is transferred. Writing "Na is 2,8,1 and Cl is 2,8,7" by itself is not an explanation — you must say Na loses one electron to Cl and state the charges formed.
  • Forgetting to show the brackets and charges on a dot-and-cross diagram. Examiners want to see [Na]⁺ and [Cl]⁻ with the 1+ and 1− charges clearly placed at the top right of each bracket.
  • Mixing up which atom loses and which gains. Metals lose, non-metals gain — always. If you have written "sodium gains an electron", you have made a fundamental error.
  • Writing the wrong charge size. A group 2 metal loses two electrons, so the charge is 2+, not 1+. A group 6 non-metal gains two electrons, so the charge is 2−, not 1−.

How Formation of Ions appears in exams

This spec point is foundational to ionic bonding and frequently appears inside longer-tariff questions, particularly those that name a specific group 1 + group 7 or group 2 + group 6 compound. A typical question asks how a metal atom and a non-metal atom form ions — to get full credit your answer needs:

  1. Which atom loses electrons and which atom gains electrons
  2. How many electrons are transferred
  3. The resulting charge on each ion (with sign and size)

It also features in 6-mark extended-response questions where the formation of ions is combined with the structure of the ionic lattice and the nature of the ionic bond itself.

Paper 1 (1CH0/1F + 1CH0/1H) — typical appearances:

  • Short "explain" questions on a named compound (2–3 marks): electron transfer between specific atoms
  • 6-mark extended-response questions combining ion formation, ionic bonding and lattice structure

  • 1CH0/2F — 2019 June, Q9(d) (6 marks): "Explain how sodium and chlorine atoms form the ions in sodium chloride and how the ions are arranged in the solid sodium chloride. You may wish to use diagrams in your answer."

Key Takeaways
  1. Atoms form ions to achieve a full outer shell of electrons — the same stable arrangement as the nearest noble gas.
  2. Metals in groups 1 and 2 lose their outer electrons to form positive ions (cations); non-metals in groups 6 and 7 gain electrons to form negative ions (anions).
  3. The size of the charge on the ion equals the number of electrons transferred: group 1 → 1+, group 2 → 2+, group 6 → 2−, group 7 → 1−.
  4. In an ionic compound the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge — this fixes the ratio of cations to anions in the formula.
  5. NaCl forms from a 1:1 transfer of one electron from Na (2,8,1) to Cl (2,8,7), giving Na⁺ (2,8) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8).
  6. MgO forms from a 1:1 transfer of two electrons from Mg to O, giving Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, both with the configuration 2,8.
  7. MgCl₂ needs two Cl atoms per Mg atom because each Cl only accepts one electron and Mg has two to give away; Na₂O needs two Na atoms per O for the same reason in reverse.
  8. To get marks on an "explain how ions form" question, you must say which atom loses electrons, which gains, how many, and what charge each ion ends up with — never just restate the starting electron configurations.
  9. Ionic bonding is not the same as covalent bonding — atoms transfer electrons, they do not share them; dot-and-cross diagrams should show separate ions in brackets with charges outside, not a shared pair.
  10. The mathematical skill behind every formula in this spec point is simple ratio balancing: find the smallest whole numbers of cations and anions whose charges cancel out.
1.3.2.1 Ionic Bonds F+H ▼

Ionic Bonds

Spec 1.21 — Explain how ionic bonds are formed by the transfer of electrons between atoms to produce cations and anions, including the use of dot and cross diagrams. Foundation + Higher tier, Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Ionic bondThe strong electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions
CationA positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses one or more electrons
AnionA negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains one or more electrons
Electron transferThe movement of one or more electrons from a metal atom to a non-metal atom during ionic bond formation
Dot and cross diagramA diagram that uses dots and crosses to represent electrons from different atoms, used to show how the outer-shell electrons rearrange during bonding
Noble gas configurationA full outer electron shell, the stable arrangement that ions achieve after the transfer of electrons

How an ionic bond forms

Ionic bonds form between a metal and a non-metal. Metal atoms have only a few electrons in their outer shell, and these electrons are relatively easy to lose. Non-metal atoms have almost-full outer shells and only need to gain a small number of electrons to fill them. The electron(s) leave the metal atom and are gained by the non-metal atom — this is a complete transfer of electrons, not sharing.

Once the transfer has happened, the metal atom is left with fewer electrons than protons, giving it an overall positive charge — it has become a cation. The non-metal atom now has more electrons than protons, giving it an overall negative charge — it has become an anion. Both ions now have full outer shells (the electron configuration of the nearest noble gas), which is energetically stable.

The positively charged cation and the negatively charged anion then attract each other. The strong electrostatic force of attraction between these opposite charges is what we call the ionic bond. The electrostatic attraction is not just between one cation and one anion — it acts in all directions at once, which is why ionic compounds extend into giant 3D lattices rather than forming discrete molecules (see 1.3.2.2 Ionic Lattices).

Why electron transfer happens — the driving force

A neutral metal atom is not in a particularly stable arrangement; its outer-shell electrons are loosely held and far from the nucleus. Losing them leaves a smaller, fully-filled inner shell as the new outermost layer — this is the noble gas configuration of the previous period (e.g. sodium loses one electron to look like neon).

A neutral non-metal atom of group 6 or 7 is one or two electrons short of a full outer shell. Adding those electrons fills the shell and reaches the noble gas configuration of its own period (e.g. chlorine gains one electron to look like argon).

The combination of a stable cation, a stable anion and a powerful electrostatic attraction between them releases energy overall — which is why the bond forms.

Worked example — sodium chloride (NaCl)

Sodium is in Group 1 with electronic configuration 2,8,1. Chlorine is in Group 7 with electronic configuration 2,8,7.

Step 1 — sodium loses its single outer electron: Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻

Step 2 — chlorine gains that electron into its outer shell: Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8)

Step 3 — the Na⁺ cation and Cl⁻ anion attract each other to form the ionic bond. The compound is sodium chloride, NaCl. The formula reflects the 1:1 ratio of charges (+1 and −1 balance).

The dot and cross diagram shows the sodium atom with no dots in its outer shell (it has lost its single outer electron) and the chlorine atom with eight electrons in its outer shell — seven of its original crosses plus the one dot that came from sodium. Each ion is drawn in square brackets with the charge written in the top-right corner, like this:

[Na]⁺ [Cl with 7 crosses and 1 dot]⁻

The brackets and charge labels are essential — they show that a transfer has occurred and that each particle is now a charged ion, not a neutral atom.

Worked example — magnesium oxide (MgO)

Magnesium is in Group 2 (2,8,2) and oxygen is in Group 6 (2,6). Two electrons must move for both atoms to reach a full outer shell:

Mg (2,8,2) → Mg²⁺ (2,8) + 2e⁻ O (2,6) + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ (2,8)

The charges (+2 and −2) balance in a 1:1 ratio, so the formula is MgO. Because the charges on each ion are twice those in NaCl, the electrostatic attraction in magnesium oxide is stronger — which is why MgO has a much higher melting point than NaCl.

Worked example — calcium fluoride (CaF₂)

When the charge magnitudes do not match, the ratio of ions changes. Calcium is in Group 2 (2,8,8,2) and fluorine is in Group 7 (2,7). Calcium loses two electrons, but each fluorine atom can only accept one — so two fluorine atoms are needed per calcium atom:

Ca (2,8,8,2) → Ca²⁺ (2,8,8) + 2e⁻ 2 F (2,7) + 2 e⁻ → 2 F⁻ (2,8)

The overall formula is CaF₂. The dot and cross diagram should show two fluoride ions, each with its own brackets and a −1 charge, alongside one Ca²⁺ ion. A common mistake is to show one fluoride ion with a −2 charge — fluorine can only ever take one extra electron, so each F must be drawn separately as F⁻.

Dot and cross diagram conventions

When you draw a dot and cross diagram for ionic bonding, follow these rules:

  • Show only the outer shell electrons of each atom — inner shells are not required.
  • Use dots for one element and crosses for the other so it is clear which electron has come from which atom.
  • After transfer, the metal ion has an empty outer shell in the diagram (because all its outer electrons have moved across). For ions of groups 1 and 2 in periods 2 or 3, you typically draw just the symbol in brackets — no outer dots.
  • The non-metal ion has a full outer shell of eight (or two, in the case of H⁻), made up of its own original crosses plus the dot(s) it has received from the metal.
  • Each ion is drawn separately in square brackets, with the charge written in the top-right outside the brackets, e.g. [Na]⁺, [Cl]⁻, [Mg]²⁺, [O]²⁻.
  • If the formula requires more than one ion of a given type (as in CaF₂ or Na₂O), draw each ion separately with its own brackets and charge — do not just write a subscript.

Comparing ionic bonds with covalent bonds

Many exam questions ask you to contrast ionic and covalent bonding. The differences come directly from the bond mechanism.

FeatureIonic bondCovalent bond
Bonded particlesMetal + non-metalNon-metal + non-metal
What happens to electronsTransferred from one atom to the otherShared between two atoms
Particles formedOppositely charged ions (cation + anion)Neutral molecules (or giant covalent structures)

Notice that both bond types are electrostatic in origin — the difference is in what is being attracted to what.

Mathematical Skills

The mathematical skill associated with this spec point is Skill 5b — Visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms, including 2D representations of 3D objects (Source: GCSE_Chemistry_Spec.pdf). In practice, this means being able to draw and interpret dot and cross diagrams that represent the 3D outer electron shells of ions on a flat page. The procedure for a dot and cross diagram of an ionic compound is:

  1. Identify the metal and the non-metal, and write the electronic configuration of each.
  2. Decide how many electrons must transfer from the metal to the non-metal for both to reach a full outer shell.
  3. Draw the metal ion (empty outer shell for most simple cases) in square brackets with the correct positive charge.
  4. Draw the non-metal ion with a full outer shell — using its own crosses plus dot(s) received from the metal — in square brackets with the correct negative charge.
  5. If the formula requires more than one of either ion, draw each ion separately with its own brackets and charge.
  6. Check: do the total positive and total negative charges balance to zero?

  2. Ionic bonds form between a metal (which loses electrons) and a non-metal (which gains electrons).
  3. A cation is a positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons; an anion is a negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains one or more electrons.

1.3.2.2 Ionic Lattices F+H ▼

Ionic Lattices

Syllabus reference 1.27: Explain the structure of an ionic compound as a lattice structure: (a) consisting of a regular arrangement of ions, (b) held together by strong electrostatic forces (ionic bonds) between oppositely-charged ions. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 1 of both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Ionic latticeA regular, repeating 3D arrangement of oppositely-charged ions held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction
Electrostatic forceThe force of attraction that acts between particles with opposite electrical charges (and the force of repulsion between particles with the same charge)
Ionic bondThe strong electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely-charged ions in an ionic compound
Giant structureA structure made of a very large number of particles arranged in a repeating pattern that extends in all directions
CationA positively charged ion (e.g. Na⁺, Mg²⁺)
AnionA negatively charged ion (e.g. Cl⁻, O²⁻)

What an ionic lattice actually looks like

When metal atoms react with non-metal atoms, electrons are transferred from the metal to the non-metal. This produces positive metal ions (cations) and negative non-metal ions (anions). Once they are formed, these oppositely-charged ions do not stay as isolated pairs. Instead, every positive ion attracts every nearby negative ion, and every negative ion attracts every nearby positive ion. The ions pull themselves into a regular, repeating pattern that extends in three dimensions — this is the ionic lattice.

The most important features you need to be able to describe are:

  • The ions are arranged in a regular, repeating, 3D pattern (a giant structure)
  • Positive and negative ions alternate in the pattern — every positive ion is surrounded by negative ions, and every negative ion is surrounded by positive ions
  • The ions are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely-charged ions
  • These electrostatic forces act in all directions throughout the lattice (not just between one pair of ions)
  • The number of positive ions and negative ions present is in the ratio shown by the chemical formula (for example, NaCl has a 1:1 ratio of Na⁺ to Cl⁻; MgCl₂ has a 1:2 ratio of Mg²⁺ to Cl⁻)

The classic example is sodium chloride, NaCl. In a sodium chloride lattice, each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by six Cl⁻ ions, and each Cl⁻ ion is surrounded by six Na⁺ ions. The structure stretches outwards in all directions to form a cube-shaped giant lattice. A single grain of table salt contains an enormous number of ions arranged this way.

Why the ions stay in the lattice

It is worth thinking about why the lattice is so stable. An isolated Na⁺ ion and an isolated Cl⁻ ion will attract each other — that is one ionic bond. But in a lattice, every ion is attracted to every oppositely-charged neighbour. A single Na⁺ ion in NaCl is electrostatically attracted to six surrounding Cl⁻ ions at the same time, and weakly repelled by Na⁺ ions slightly further away. When you add up all of these attractions and subtract the repulsions across the whole structure, the overall energy released is very large. That is what makes the lattice so much more stable than separated ions, and why ionic compounds always exist as giant structures rather than as small molecules.

This is the underlying reason for two trends you will meet again and again at GCSE:

  • Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points because a huge amount of energy is needed to break all of the strong electrostatic attractions in the lattice
  • The melting and boiling points are higher when the ions have higher charges (for example, MgO with Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ has a higher melting point than NaCl with Na⁺ and Cl⁻), because the electrostatic forces between 2+ and 2− ions are stronger than between 1+ and 1− ions of similar size

Lattice vs single ionic bond — clearing up a common confusion

Students often picture an ionic compound as a single pair of ions joined by a single bond — for example, one Na⁺ glued to one Cl⁻. This is wrong, and examiners specifically watch for it.

Single ionic bond (a pair of ions)Ionic lattice (the actual compound)
One positive ion bonded to one negative ionA huge number of positive and negative ions
Just one electrostatic attractionElectrostatic attractions in all directions
Not how ionic compounds exist in the solid stateThe correct picture of solid ionic compounds
Sometimes shown by a dot-and-cross diagram for teaching purposesShown as a 3D lattice diagram

A dot-and-cross diagram for NaCl shows the electron transfer from sodium to chlorine — it is useful for explaining where the ions come from, but it does not represent the structure of solid sodium chloride. The real solid is the lattice.

Drawing and interpreting lattice diagrams

The specification flags Mathematical Skill 5b for this spec point — visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms (Source: GCSE_Chemistry_Spec.pdf). In practice, this means you should be able to:

  • Recognise a 3D lattice diagram and identify which ions are positive and which are negative (positive ions are usually labelled with a + sign or shown as smaller spheres; chloride ions are usually shown as the larger spheres in NaCl)
  • Draw a simple 2D representation showing alternating + and − ions in a regular grid pattern
  • State the ratio of cations to anions from the lattice (this matches the chemical formula)

A simple way to draw NaCl in 2D is to sketch a square grid where the corners alternate between Na⁺ and Cl⁻. Label at least one of each ion and indicate that the pattern continues in all directions.

Worked example — describing the structure of an ionic compound

A common 2-mark question asks you to describe the structure of an ionic compound such as sodium chloride. To pick up both marks, you need to mention the arrangement of the ions and the forces that hold them together.

Question: Describe the structure of solid sodium chloride.

A 2-mark answer would be:

> Sodium chloride consists of a giant regular 3D lattice of alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions (1). The ions are held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely-charged ions (1).

Notice that "strong forces" on its own is not enough — the marking point is "strong electrostatic forces between oppositely-charged ions". Examiners want the cause of the attraction made explicit.

For a 6-mark extended-response question, you would also need to explain how the ions were formed (electron transfer from the metal to the non-metal), the charges on each ion, the role of the noble gas configuration, and why the lattice is so stable.

Higher tier extension — linking the lattice to properties

The same lattice idea is used at Higher tier to explain quantitative differences in properties between ionic compounds. For example, you can be asked to explain why magnesium oxide (MgO) has a much higher melting point than sodium chloride (NaCl).

A Higher-tier explanation should include:

  1. Both compounds have giant ionic lattices held together by strong electrostatic forces
  2. Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions have higher charges than Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions
  3. The higher charges produce stronger electrostatic forces of attraction between the oppositely-charged ions
  4. More energy is therefore needed to break the lattice, so MgO has a higher melting point

This same line of reasoning is used for boiling points, hardness, and (later in the course) the energy changes involved in dissolving and reacting ionic compounds.

1.3.3.1 Naming Ionic Compounds F+H ▼

Naming Ionic Compounds

Spec point 1.25: Explain the use of the endings –ide and –ate in the names of compounds. Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Naming ionic compounds is one of the most testable transferable skills on Paper 1: nearly every question that involves a reaction, a precipitate, an electrolysis product, or a salt requires you to read or write a correct compound name. The Edexcel spec point is narrow — you must be able to explain the use of the endings –ide and –ate — but the rule is applied across the whole qualification, from balancing equations and identifying ions to writing word equations for neutralisation and group-trend reactions.

The core idea is short: an ionic compound's name is built from the name of its positive ion (the cation) followed by the name of its negative ion (the anion), and the anion ending tells you what is in it. A name ending in –ide signals that the negative ion contains only one element (a monatomic anion, or in a few cases a simple non-metal–hydrogen pair such as hydroxide). A name ending in –ate signals that the negative ion contains oxygen as well as another element (a polyatomic oxoanion).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Ionic compoundA compound made from a positive ion (usually a metal or ammonium) and a negative ion, held together by electrostatic attraction
CationA positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses electrons; written first in an ionic compound's name
AnionA negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains electrons or from a polyatomic group; written second in an ionic compound's name
–ide endingAn ending used for anions that contain only one element (monatomic), e.g. chloride (Cl⁻), oxide (O²⁻), sulfide (S²⁻); hydroxide (OH⁻) is the conventional exception
–ate endingAn ending used for polyatomic anions that contain oxygen plus another element, e.g. sulfate (SO₄²⁻), nitrate (NO₃⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻)
Monatomic ionAn ion made from a single atom that has lost or gained electrons, e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻
Polyatomic ionAn ion made from a group of atoms covalently bonded together but carrying an overall charge, e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, OH⁻, NH₄⁺

How an ionic-compound name is built

Every binary ionic compound name follows the same two-step pattern:

  1. The cation keeps its element name. Sodium stays sodium. Magnesium stays magnesium. The metal does not change.
  2. The anion's element name is shortened and the ending –ide is added.

This gives the standard set of binary compounds:

There is one widely accepted "irregular" –ide name to memorise: the OH⁻ ion is called hydroxide. It contains two elements (hydrogen and oxygen), but because it does not contain extra oxygen beyond the O–H pair, convention keeps it inside the –ide family rather than calling it an –ate. Sodium hydroxide is therefore NaOH, calcium hydroxide is Ca(OH)₂.

Why the ending changes when the anion contains oxygen

When the negative ion is a polyatomic group built around a central non-metal atom with extra oxygen atoms attached, the compound is named with the ending –ate. This is the rule the spec asks you to explain. The –ate ending is shorthand for "this anion contains oxygen as well as the element it is named after."

Central non-metalPolyatomic anionFormulaCompound example
Sulfur (with oxygen)sulfateSO₄²⁻copper sulfate (CuSO₄)

Compare the two pairs:

  • Sodium sulfide (Na₂S) contains S²⁻ — sulfur on its own. No oxygen. So the ending is –ide.
  • Sodium sulfate (Na₂SO₄) contains SO₄²⁻ — sulfur surrounded by four oxygens. So the ending is –ate.
  • Calcium carbide (CaC₂) would contain only carbon as its anion (not on Edexcel GCSE, but the logic still applies).
  • Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) contains CO₃²⁻ — carbon with three oxygens — so the ending is –ate.

Spotting –ate in a compound's name is a fast diagnostic: there is oxygen hidden inside the anion, and you will need to remember the formula of that oxoanion to balance the compound or write the equation. Most students who lose marks on equations involving sulfates, nitrates and carbonates do so because they wrote SO₃ for sulfate, NO₂ for nitrate, or forgot the brackets when there are two of them, e.g. Ca(NO₃)₂ rather than CaNO₃₂.

The ammonium ion: a positive polyatomic ion

Most positive ions are metal cations (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, etc.) and they keep the simple element name. The major exception you will meet is the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺. It behaves like a cation in salts, so when it appears first you get compounds such as:

  • Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl)
  • Ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄)
  • Ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃)

Notice that the –ide and –ate rules still apply to the second word: chloride (no oxygen), sulfate (oxygen in SO₄²⁻), nitrate (oxygen in NO₃⁻). The ammonium part does not change the anion rule.

Transition metals: variable charge means a Roman numeral

Some metals — particularly the transition metals — can form more than one stable positive ion. Iron, for example, exists as Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺; copper as Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺. To make the name unambiguous, a Roman numeral in brackets is added to show the charge on the metal:

  • Iron(II) chloride — FeCl₂ — the iron ion is Fe²⁺
  • Iron(III) chloride — FeCl₃ — the iron ion is Fe³⁺
  • Copper(II) sulfate — CuSO₄ — the copper ion is Cu²⁺

The Roman numeral always refers to the metal's charge, not the number of metal atoms in the formula. The –ide / –ate ending on the anion is unaffected.

Worked example 1: building a name from a formula

Name the compound MgBr₂.

  1. Identify the cation: Mg²⁺ — magnesium. Name does not change.
  2. Identify the anion: Br⁻ — bromide (single element, so –ide).
  3. Combine: magnesium bromide.

Name the compound K₂CO₃.

  1. Identify the cation: K⁺ — potassium.
  2. Identify the anion: CO₃²⁻ — this is the carbonate group (carbon plus oxygen), so the ending is –ate.
  3. Combine: potassium carbonate.

Name the compound (NH₄)₂SO₄.

  1. Identify the cation: NH₄⁺ — ammonium.
  2. Identify the anion: SO₄²⁻ — sulfate (sulfur plus oxygen, ending –ate).
  3. Combine: ammonium sulfate.

Worked example 2: building a formula from a name

Write the formula of aluminium oxide.

  1. The first word — aluminium — gives the cation: Al³⁺.
  2. The second word ends in –ide and the element is oxygen, so the anion is O²⁻.
  3. Balance the charges so the total positive charge equals the total negative charge: two Al³⁺ give +6, three O²⁻ give –6. Formula: Al₂O₃.

Write the formula of calcium nitrate.

  1. Cation: Ca²⁺.
  2. The second word ends in –ate and is named after nitrogen, so the anion is the polyatomic nitrate ion: NO₃⁻.
  3. Charge balance: one Ca²⁺ (+2) needs two NO₃⁻ (–2 total). Because nitrate is a polyatomic group, when you need two of them you use brackets: Ca(NO₃)₂.

Quick decision flow for naming

If you are given a formula and asked for a name:

  1. Read off the cation — keep the metal name (add a Roman numeral if it is a transition metal with variable charge).
  2. Look at the anion. Is it a single non-metal element (Cl, Br, I, O, S, N, H)? If yes, use the –ide ending.
  3. Is the anion a group of atoms containing oxygen plus another non-metal? If yes, use the standard –ate name (sulfate, nitrate, carbonate, phosphate).
  4. Is the anion OH⁻? Then it is hydroxide (kept inside the –ide family by convention).

Common student errors

The following errors lose marks on naming and equation-writing questions:

  • Calling sulfate "sulfide" (or vice versa). Sulfate contains oxygen (SO₄²⁻); sulfide does not (S²⁻). Listen for the "a" sound in "–ate."
  • Writing the formula of nitrate as NO₂⁻ or N₃⁻ — it is NO₃⁻.
  • Forgetting brackets when an –ate anion is repeated, e.g. writing CaNO₃₂ instead of Ca(NO₃)₂.
  • Naming compounds with the ions in the wrong order. The metal/cation always comes first: "sodium chloride," not "chloride sodium."
  • Forgetting the Roman numeral on transition-metal compounds. "Iron chloride" alone is ambiguous; you need iron(II) or iron(III) chloride.
  • Mis-naming ammonium compounds. Ammonium (NH₄⁺) is the cation, so it is written first, e.g. ammonium sulfate, not "sulfate ammonium."

1.3.3.2 Deducing Formulae F+H ▼

Deducing Formulae

Spec 1.26: Deduce the formulae of ionic compounds (including oxides, hydroxides, halides, nitrates, carbonates and sulfates) given the formulae of the constituent ions. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 1, for both Separate Chemistry and Combined Science.

Ionic compounds are electrically neutral. That single rule drives the whole of this spec point: when you combine a positive ion (cation) with a negative ion (anion), the number of each ion you write in the formula must make the total positive charge equal to the total negative charge. Once you can apply that rule consistently, you can write the formula of any ionic compound from group 1/2/3 cations with the named anion families (oxides, hydroxides, halides, nitrates, carbonates, sulfates).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Ionic compoundA compound formed from positive and negative ions held together by electrostatic forces of attraction
CationA positively charged ion (formed when an atom loses electrons)
AnionA negatively charged ion (formed when an atom gains electrons)
Monatomic ionAn ion formed from a single atom (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻)
Polyatomic ionAn ion made up of more than one atom that carries an overall charge (e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, OH⁻, CO₃²⁻)
Formula unitThe simplest whole-number ratio of ions in an ionic compound (e.g. NaCl, MgCl₂)

The ion charges you must know

Edexcel expects you to recall the typical ion charges of the main groups and the formulae of the six named polyatomic anions. The cations and anions you will combine in spec 1.26 are:

Cations (positive ions)Anions (negative ions)
Group 1 metals — 1+ (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺)Group 7 halides — 1− (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻)
Group 2 metals — 2+ (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺)Group 6 oxides — 2− (O²⁻)
Group 3 metals — 3+ (Al³⁺)Hydroxide — 1− (OH⁻)
Hydrogen — 1+ (H⁺)Nitrate — 1− (NO₃⁻)
Ammonium — 1+ (NH₄⁺)Carbonate — 2− (CO₃²⁻)
Common transition metal ions given to you (e.g. Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Ag⁺)Sulfate — 2− (SO₄²⁻)

Transition metal charges are not predictable from group number, so Edexcel will always tell you the formula of the transition metal ion in the question (e.g. "copper(II) ions, Cu²⁺"). The Roman numeral in the name tells you the charge.

The charge-balancing rule (the only rule you need)

The total positive charge in the formula must equal the total negative charge. To apply it:

  1. Write the formula of the cation and the anion side by side, with their charges.
  2. Find the smallest whole-number ratio of cation : anion that makes the charges cancel.
  3. Write that ratio as subscripts in the formula. A subscript of 1 is left off.
  4. For polyatomic ions taken more than once, put the whole ion in brackets before the subscript — e.g. Mg(OH)₂, Al(NO₃)₃, (NH₄)₂SO₄.

A quick shortcut that works for most pairs is the "swap-and-drop" method: swap the size of the cation charge to the anion subscript, and the size of the anion charge to the cation subscript, then cancel down to the simplest ratio. For example, Al³⁺ + O²⁻ becomes Al₂O₃ (the 3 from Al³⁺ goes onto O, the 2 from O²⁻ goes onto Al). It works because cross-multiplying the charges guarantees that the totals balance: 2 × (+3) = +6 and 3 × (−2) = −6.

Worked examples

*Example 1 — Sodium chloride (1+ with 1−)*

Cation Na⁺, anion Cl⁻. One of each balances: +1 and −1 cancel. Formula: NaCl.

*Example 2 — Magnesium chloride (2+ with 1−)*

Cation Mg²⁺, anion Cl⁻. You need two Cl⁻ to balance one Mg²⁺: +2 and 2 × (−1) = −2. Formula: MgCl₂.

*Example 3 — Aluminium oxide (3+ with 2−)*

Cation Al³⁺, anion O²⁻. Swap-and-drop: Al₂O₃. Check: 2 × (+3) = +6, 3 × (−2) = −6. Formula: Al₂O₃.

*Example 4 — Potassium sulfate (1+ with 2−)*

Cation K⁺, anion SO₄²⁻. You need two K⁺ to balance one SO₄²⁻: 2 × (+1) = +2, and (−2) from the sulfate. Formula: K₂SO₄. The most common student error here is to write KSO₄ — see Examiner Report Insights below.

*Example 5 — Calcium hydroxide (2+ with 1−)*

Cation Ca²⁺, anion OH⁻. You need two OH⁻ for one Ca²⁺. Because OH⁻ is polyatomic, put it in brackets: Ca(OH)₂. Writing CaOH₂ would mean one O and two H, which is wrong.

*Example 6 — Ammonium sulfate (1+ with 2−)*

Cation NH₄⁺, anion SO₄²⁻. You need two NH₄⁺ for one SO₄²⁻. Both are polyatomic, but only the one taken more than once goes in brackets: (NH₄)₂SO₄.

*Example 7 — Iron(III) carbonate (3+ with 2−)*

Cation Fe³⁺, anion CO₃²⁻. Swap-and-drop: 2 Fe³⁺ and 3 CO₃²⁻. Bracket the polyatomic carbonate because it is taken three times: Fe₂(CO₃)₃. Check: 2 × (+3) = +6, 3 × (−2) = −6.

*Example 8 — Aluminium nitrate (3+ with 1−)*

Cation Al³⁺, anion NO₃⁻. You need three NO₃⁻ for one Al³⁺: Al(NO₃)₃.

When to use brackets

Brackets are essential for polyatomic ions that appear more than once in the formula. Without brackets, the subscript would only apply to the last atom, which changes the formula. Compare:

CompoundCorrect formulaWhat it would mean without brackets
Calcium hydroxideCa(OH)₂CaOH₂ — one O, two H (wrong)
Magnesium nitrateMg(NO₃)₂MgNO₃₂ — meaningless / one N, 32 O
Aluminium hydroxideAl(OH)₃AlOH₃ — one O, three H (wrong)
Ammonium carbonate(NH₄)₂CO₃NH₄₂CO₃ — meaningless

A polyatomic ion appearing only once does not need brackets (NaOH, NaNO₃, K₂SO₄ are all fine without brackets around the polyatomic part).

Why the formula does not look like a 1 : 1 swap of names

Students often try to write the formula by mirroring the order of the name. "Magnesium chloride" feels like it should be MgCl. But the name only lists which two ions are present — it does not tell you the ratio. The ratio is set by the charges. You will get the formula wrong every time if you skip the charge-balance step.

For the same reason, "potassium sulfate" is not KSO₄ even though the name contains only one "potassium" and one "sulfate". Sulfate is a 2− ion, potassium is 1+, so two potassiums are needed: K₂SO₄.

Mathematical Skills

Spec 1.26 maps to maths skill 1c (use ratios, fractions and percentages). The maths is the ratio step in the charge balance:

`` (cation charge) × (number of cations) = (anion charge) × (number of anions) ``

Procedure:

  1. Write the magnitude of the cation charge (ignore the sign).
  2. Write the magnitude of the anion charge (ignore the sign).
  3. Cross over: the cation charge becomes the anion subscript, and vice versa.
  4. Simplify the ratio to its smallest whole numbers (e.g. Mg²⁺ with O²⁻ gives 2 : 2, which simplifies to 1 : 1 → MgO, not Mg₂O₂).

Step 4 matters: ionic formulae are always written as the simplest whole-number ratio of ions, because the lattice extends indefinitely and "Mg₂O₂" would imply a discrete molecule, which an ionic compound is not.

Common student errors

  • Writing KSO₄ instead of K₂SO₄ — failing to balance a 1+ cation against a 2− anion.
  • Writing CaOH₂ instead of Ca(OH)₂ — forgetting brackets around a polyatomic ion taken twice.
  • Writing Mg₂O₂ instead of MgO — not simplifying to the smallest ratio.
  • Putting the subscript inside the bracket (e.g. (NO₃)₂Mg) — formulae are written cation first, then anion, with subscripts outside the bracket.
  • Mixing up the charge sign with the subscript number — the swap-and-drop only uses the magnitude of the charge, never the sign.
  • Forgetting that transition metals can have more than one charge — always read the Roman numeral (Fe(II) vs Fe(III)).
  • Treating ammonium NH₄⁺ as a metal — it is polyatomic and goes in brackets when taken more than once: (NH₄)₂SO₄.

Covalent Bonding

Spec points 1.28–1.30: Explain how a covalent bond is formed when a pair of electrons is shared between two atoms; recall that covalent bonding results in the formation of molecules; recall the typical size (order of magnitude) of atoms and small molecules. Foundation + Higher tier, Paper 1, both 1CH0 and 1SC0.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Covalent bondA shared pair of electrons between two atoms
MoleculeA particle made of two or more atoms joined by covalent bonds
Diatomic moleculeA molecule made of two atoms only (e.g. H₂, Cl₂, O₂, N₂)
Bonding pairThe pair of electrons that is shared between two atoms in a covalent bond
Lone pairA pair of outer-shell electrons on an atom that is not involved in bonding
Single bondA covalent bond made of one shared pair of electrons (drawn as a single line)
Double bondA covalent bond made of two shared pairs of electrons (drawn as a double line)
Triple bondA covalent bond made of three shared pairs of electrons (drawn as a triple line)
Nanometre (nm)A unit of length equal to 1 × 10⁻⁹ m, used to express atomic and molecular sizes

How a covalent bond is formed

A covalent bond forms between two non-metal atoms. Each atom contributes one electron from its outer shell, and the two electrons together form a shared pair that sits in the region between the two nuclei.

The key idea is that both nuclei attract the same pair of electrons at the same time. The positive nucleus of atom A attracts the shared pair, and the positive nucleus of atom B attracts that same shared pair. This mutual electrostatic attraction between the two nuclei and the shared pair of electrons is what holds the atoms together — that is the covalent bond.

*Why atoms share electrons in the first place*

Atoms are most stable when their outer (valence) shell is full. A full outer shell is 2 electrons for hydrogen and helium, and 8 electrons for most other small non-metals (the so-called "noble gas configuration"). Non-metal atoms typically have between 4 and 7 electrons in their outer shell, so they are several electrons short of a full shell. They cannot lose that many electrons easily (it would take far too much energy), so instead they share electrons with another non-metal. By overlapping outer shells and sharing pairs of electrons, each atom counts the shared pair as part of its own outer shell — and both atoms achieve a full outer shell at the same time.

*Hydrogen (H₂) — a single covalent bond*

A hydrogen atom has 1 electron in its outer shell and needs 2 for a full shell. When two hydrogen atoms come together, each donates its single electron into the bonding region. The two electrons form one shared pair. Both hydrogen atoms now "see" 2 electrons around them, so both have a full outer shell. The molecule is written H₂ and the bond is drawn as H–H.

*Chlorine (Cl₂) — a single covalent bond with lone pairs*

Each chlorine atom has 7 electrons in its outer shell and needs 8. Each Cl donates one electron to a shared pair, giving one Cl–Cl single bond. Around each chlorine, the bonding pair plus three lone pairs gives 8 electrons in total — a full outer shell. The molecule is written Cl₂ and the bond is Cl–Cl.

*Hydrogen chloride (HCl) — a single covalent bond between different atoms*

A covalent bond does not have to be between identical atoms. Hydrogen needs 1 more electron, chlorine needs 1 more electron, so they share one pair. H contributes 1 electron, Cl contributes 1 electron, and the shared pair sits between them. H now has 2 outer electrons (full), and Cl now has 8 outer electrons (full).

*Oxygen (O₂) — a double covalent bond*

Each oxygen atom has 6 outer electrons and needs 8. Sharing a single pair would leave each atom with only 7 — still short. Instead, each oxygen contributes 2 electrons to the bonding region, giving two shared pairs (a double bond, O=O). Each oxygen now has 4 bonding electrons + 4 lone-pair electrons = 8 outer electrons.

*Nitrogen (N₂) — a triple covalent bond*

Each nitrogen has 5 outer electrons and needs 8. Each nitrogen contributes 3 electrons to the bonding region, giving three shared pairs (a triple bond, N≡N). Each nitrogen ends up with 6 bonding electrons + 2 lone-pair electrons = 8 outer.

Covalent bonding produces molecules

A molecule is the discrete particle formed when atoms join through covalent bonds. The number of atoms in the molecule is fixed: H₂ always has 2 atoms, H₂O always has 3, CH₄ always has 5, NH₃ always has 4. The chemical formula tells you exactly which atoms and how many of each are present in one molecule.

Molecules are electrically neutral — there is no transfer of electrons, so there are no ions and no overall charge. The atoms in a molecule are held together very strongly (covalent bonds are strong), but the forces between separate molecules (intermolecular forces) are typically weak — that is why most small covalent substances have low melting and boiling points and are gases or liquids at room temperature.

This is the fundamental contrast with ionic bonding: ionic bonding produces a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions, not discrete molecules. Covalent bonding produces small, separate, neutral molecules (or, in some cases, giant covalent structures like diamond — but the bonding mechanism is still the shared pair).

Typical size of atoms and small molecules

Atoms and small molecules are extremely small. You are expected to know the order of magnitude (the rough size) rather than precise values.

ParticleTypical size
Atom (diameter)~1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (about 0.1 nm)
Small molecule (e.g. H₂, H₂O, CO₂)A few × 10⁻¹⁰ m (about 0.1–0.5 nm)
Nanoparticle (for context)1 × 10⁻⁹ m to 1 × 10⁻⁷ m (1–100 nm)

To put 10⁻¹⁰ m into perspective: one metre divided by 10 billion. If you stretched out one hydrogen atom to the size of a marble, the marble — at the same scale — would be roughly the size of the Earth.

*Worked example — estimating molecular size*

Estimate how many water molecules, placed end-to-end, would stretch across the diameter of a human hair (~0.1 mm).

  1. Diameter of a water molecule ≈ 3 × 10⁻¹⁰ m
  2. Diameter of a hair ≈ 0.1 mm = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m
  3. Number of molecules = (1 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (3 × 10⁻¹⁰) ≈ 3.3 × 10⁵

So roughly 300,000 water molecules side-by-side would span the width of one human hair. The point of this calculation is not to memorise the answer — it is to feel the scale: atoms and small molecules are about 10⁻¹⁰ m, far smaller than anything you can see.

Spec maths skill 1d asks you to relate size and scale of atoms to objects in the physical world. Be ready to estimate, to convert between m and nm (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m = 10 × 10⁻¹⁰ m), and to recognise that nanoparticles are about 10–1000 times bigger than individual atoms.

1.4.2.1 Dot & Cross Diagrams for Molecules F+H ▼

Dot & Cross Diagrams for Molecules

Spec 1.31: Explain the formation of simple molecular, covalent substances, using dot and cross diagrams, including hydrogen, hydrogen chloride, water, methane, oxygen and carbon dioxide. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 1, for both 1CH0 and 1SC0.

A dot-and-cross diagram is a way of showing how two non-metal atoms hold themselves together by sharing pairs of outer-shell electrons. The electrons from one atom are drawn as dots, the electrons from the other atom are drawn as crosses, and the shared pair sits in the overlap between the atoms — that overlap is the covalent bond. Once you know how many electrons each atom brings to the bond and how many it needs to fill its outer shell, every diagram on the spec list (H₂, HCl, H₂O, CH₄, O₂, CO₂) can be built using the same procedure.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Covalent bondA shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms
Dot-and-cross diagramA diagram showing how the outer-shell electrons of two atoms are shared (or transferred), using dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other
Lone pairA pair of outer-shell electrons on an atom that is not involved in bonding
Bonding pairA pair of outer-shell electrons that is shared between two atoms — i.e. one covalent bond
Double bondTwo shared pairs of electrons between the same two atoms — counts as a single covalent bond but with four shared electrons
Outer shellThe highest-energy occupied shell; the only shell shown in a GCSE dot-and-cross diagram of a molecule

Why dot-and-cross diagrams use only outer-shell electrons

Atoms become more stable when their outer shell is full (2 electrons for hydrogen, 8 electrons for almost every other small atom you meet at GCSE). The inner shells are already full and are not involved in bonding, so the diagrams drop them. Drawing inner shells doesn't earn extra credit and is one of the easiest ways to clutter the diagram, miscount, and lose a mark.

You are also told this explicitly in the question stem — Edexcel papers consistently ask candidates to "show outer shell electrons only". Treat that wording as a hard rule, not a hint.

How to build any dot-and-cross diagram (procedure)

  1. Write down the number of outer-shell electrons for each atom (Group number = outer-shell electrons for Groups 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7; Group 0/8 = 8, except He = 2).
  2. Work out how many more electrons each atom needs to reach a full outer shell (2 for H, 8 for everything else at GCSE).
  3. Match the shortfalls so each atom gets to a full shell — every shared pair counts as one electron for each atom in the pair.
  4. Draw the atoms (often as their element symbols), overlap their outer shells, and place the shared pair(s) in the overlap. Lone (non-bonding) pairs go on the outside of the overlap.
  5. Use dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other. Pair up electrons on each atom (lone pairs as two dots together, two crosses together, or one dot + one cross in the bonding region).
  6. Count again at the end: each H should have 2 electrons in total around it, every other atom should have 8 in total around it.

The six required molecules — worked through

The spec lists six specific molecules. You should be able to draw any of these from memory and adapt the method to any other simple covalent molecule you are given in the exam (e.g. NH₃, F₂, Cl₂, H₂S).

*a) Hydrogen, H₂*

Each H has 1 outer-shell electron and needs 2. They share their single electrons to make one bonding pair.

  • 1 bonding pair (shared between the two H atoms)
  • 0 lone pairs
  • Each H now "sees" 2 electrons → full outer shell
  • Total outer-shell electrons shown: 2

Drawn as: H × • H with the dot and cross in the overlap.

*b) Hydrogen chloride, HCl*

H has 1 outer-shell electron and needs 2; Cl has 7 outer-shell electrons (Group 7) and needs 8. They share one pair.

  • 1 bonding pair (between H and Cl)
  • 3 lone pairs on Cl (the remaining 6 electrons on Cl that are not in the bond)
  • H sees 2 electrons; Cl sees 8 electrons (2 shared + 6 lone)
  • Total outer-shell electrons shown: 8 (1 from H + 7 from Cl)

*c) Water, H₂O*

O has 6 outer-shell electrons (Group 6) and needs 8; each H has 1 and needs 2. Oxygen needs two more electrons, so it forms two single bonds — one with each H.

  • 2 bonding pairs (each between O and one H)
  • 2 lone pairs on O (the 4 remaining electrons on O sit as two pairs)
  • Each H sees 2 electrons; O sees 8 electrons (4 shared + 4 lone)
  • Total outer-shell electrons shown: 8 (1 + 1 + 6)

The two lone pairs are often the trip-up here (see Examiner Report Insights below).

*d) Methane, CH₄*

C has 4 outer-shell electrons (Group 4) and needs 8; each H has 1 and needs 2. Carbon needs four more electrons, so it forms four single bonds — one to each of four H atoms.

  • 4 bonding pairs (each between C and one H)
  • 0 lone pairs (carbon's outer shell is completely used up in bonding)
  • Each H sees 2 electrons; C sees 8 electrons (all from the 4 shared pairs)
  • Total outer-shell electrons shown: 8 (4 from C + 4×1 from H)

*e) Oxygen, O₂*

Each O has 6 outer-shell electrons and needs 8. A single bond would only give each oxygen 7 — not enough. They share two pairs of electrons instead, making a double bond.

  • 2 bonding pairs in one bond (i.e. one double bond)
  • 2 lone pairs on each O (4 electrons each, sitting as two pairs)
  • Each O sees 8 electrons (4 shared + 4 lone)
  • Total outer-shell electrons shown: 12 (6 from each O)

The double bond is usually drawn as two pairs of dots-and-crosses stacked in the overlap region, or as O=O with the four shared electrons clearly placed.

*f) Carbon dioxide, CO₂*

C has 4 outer-shell electrons and needs 8; each O has 6 and needs 8. Carbon needs 4 more electrons; each oxygen needs 2 more. Carbon forms a double bond to each oxygen — two shared pairs to each side.

  • 4 bonding pairs total (a double bond on each side of the central C)
  • 2 lone pairs on each O (so 4 lone pairs across the whole molecule); 0 lone pairs on C
  • C sees 8 electrons (all bonding); each O sees 8 electrons (4 shared + 4 lone)
  • Total outer-shell electrons shown: 16 (4 from C + 6 + 6)

Drawn linearly: O = C = O with two shared pairs in each overlap and two lone pairs sitting on each O on the far side.

Why a dot-and-cross is not the same as an ionic diagram

This is the single most common cross-up. Ionic bonding transfers electrons — one atom loses, one atom gains — and the diagram is drawn with brackets around each ion and a charge shown outside the brackets (e.g. [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻ with the chloride shown with 8 electrons, all from the original Cl plus the donated electron).

Mathematical / drawing skill

Spec 1.31 is tagged to maths skill 5b — Visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms, including 2D representations of 3D objects. In practice this means:

  • Place the central atom (the one that forms more bonds) in the middle of the page.
  • Position the outer atoms symmetrically around it (2 H atoms either side of O in water, 4 H atoms around C in methane, etc.).
  • Position lone pairs opposite the bonding pairs where possible — this gives a clean, easy-to-mark diagram and reduces the chance of miscounting.

The molecule's shape doesn't have to be perfectly to scale at GCSE — what matters is that the right atoms are connected and the right numbers of bonding pairs and lone pairs are drawn.

1.5.1.1 Classifying Substances F+H ▼

Classifying Substances

Spec 1.32: Explain why elements and compounds can be classified as ionic, simple molecular (covalent), giant covalent or metallic, and how the structure and bonding of these types of substances results in different physical properties (relative melting and boiling points, relative solubility in water, electrical conductivity as solid and in solution). Foundation + Higher tier, Separate + Combined.

Classification of substances is the single biggest "anchor" topic in Paper 1. You will almost always be given a table of physical properties (melting point, boiling point, electrical conductivity as a solid, molten and in solution, sometimes solubility) and asked to work backwards from the data to deduce the structure and bonding of each substance. Doing this well means knowing the four substance types cold, knowing the properties they each produce, and — most importantly — knowing why each property follows from the structure.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Ionic compoundA compound formed when a metal transfers electrons to a non-metal, producing oppositely charged ions held together in a giant lattice by strong electrostatic forces
Simple molecular substanceA substance made of small molecules where atoms within the molecule are joined by strong covalent bonds, but separate molecules are held together only by weak intermolecular forces
Giant covalent substanceA substance with a huge 3D network of atoms all joined by strong covalent bonds (e.g. diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide)
Metallic substanceA lattice of positive metal ions arranged regularly, with delocalised electrons that move freely throughout the structure
Electrostatic attractionThe force of attraction between particles with opposite charges
Delocalised electronAn electron that is not attached to any particular atom and is free to move through the structure
Intermolecular forceA weak force of attraction between separate molecules (much weaker than a covalent bond)
LatticeA regular, repeating 3D arrangement of particles (ions, atoms or molecules)

The four classes of substance

a) Ionic compounds are always formed between a metal and a non-metal — for example sodium chloride (NaCl), magnesium oxide (MgO) or calcium fluoride (CaF₂). Electrons are transferred from the metal to the non-metal, producing positive metal ions and negative non-metal ions. These oppositely charged ions are then arranged in a giant ionic lattice held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction in all directions.

b) Simple molecular substances are made of small molecules — examples include water (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), oxygen (O₂), methane (CH₄) and hydrogen chloride (HCl). The atoms within each molecule are joined by strong covalent bonds (shared pairs of electrons), but the molecules themselves are held to each other only by weak intermolecular forces.

c) Giant covalent substances are a small but important group — the main examples at GCSE are diamond, graphite, graphene and silicon dioxide (SiO₂). Every atom is joined to its neighbours by strong covalent bonds, and the bonding extends throughout the whole structure to form a giant 3D network (or, in graphite and graphene, giant 2D layers).

d) Metallic substances include all metallic elements and alloys — for example iron, copper, sodium, aluminium and brass. A metal consists of a regular lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a "sea" of delocalised electrons that came from the outer shell of each atom.

Comparing physical properties

The properties listed in 1.32 — melting and boiling point, solubility in water, and electrical conductivity — all follow directly from the structure and bonding above. The summary table below is the single most useful learning object for this spec point.

Notice that only metallic substances conduct as solids (with the single exception of graphite/graphene, which contain delocalised electrons in their layers). And only ionic substances conduct when molten or in solution but not as solids — that distinctive pattern is the single most reliable fingerprint of an ionic compound in a data table.

Why each property follows from the structure

Why melting and boiling points are high or low. When a substance melts or boils, the forces between its particles have to be overcome. In an ionic compound, the strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions act in every direction across the whole lattice — a lot of energy is needed to break those forces, so melting and boiling points are high. In a giant covalent substance, every atom is held in place by strong covalent bonds that extend through the whole structure, so melting points are even higher (diamond sublimes above 3500 °C). Metals also have strong electrostatic attractions, this time between the positive ions and the sea of delocalised electrons, so they too usually have high melting points.

Simple molecular substances are very different. The covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong, but those are not the bonds that break on melting or boiling. What breaks are the weak intermolecular forces between separate molecules. These take very little energy to overcome, so simple molecular substances have low melting and boiling points and many are gases or liquids at room temperature.

Why ionic compounds conduct only when molten or in solution. Electrical conductivity needs charged particles that can move. In a solid ionic compound the ions are locked in the lattice — they vibrate but cannot move from place to place, so there are no charge carriers and the solid is an insulator. Once the ionic compound is melted or dissolved in water, the lattice breaks up and the ions become free to move and carry charge. That is why testing conductivity in the molten and aqueous states is such a powerful classification tool — only ionic compounds switch from non-conducting to conducting in this way.

Why metals conduct as solids. The delocalised electrons in a metal lattice are free to move throughout the whole structure, even when the metal is solid. When a potential difference is applied they drift through the metal and carry charge. Because the electrons are not held to particular atoms, metals are excellent conductors of both electricity and heat.

Why giant covalent substances are insulators (mostly). In diamond and silicon dioxide every outer electron is involved in a covalent bond, so there are no free electrons and no free ions — these substances do not conduct in any state. Graphite is the famous exception: each carbon atom is bonded to only three others, leaving one electron per atom delocalised in the layers. Those delocalised electrons make graphite (and graphene) conduct electricity.

Why simple molecular substances do not conduct. They contain no ions and no delocalised electrons in any state. Molten or dissolved simple molecular substances are still electrically neutral molecules, so they cannot carry charge.

Why some substances dissolve in water and others do not. Water is a polar solvent. It can pull apart an ionic lattice by attracting the positive ions to the slightly negative oxygen atoms of water and the negative ions to the slightly positive hydrogens — that is why many ionic compounds are soluble. Most simple molecular substances are not pulled apart by water and are insoluble (although some, such as sugar and hydrogen chloride, dissolve because they can interact with water). Giant covalent and metallic substances are not pulled apart by water at all, so they are insoluble.

Working through a data table — the exam method

When you are given a properties table and asked to classify substances, work through the same checklist every time. The order matters — you can usually rule out two classes immediately and choose between the remaining two using the melting point.

  1. Does it conduct as a solid? Yes → metallic (or graphite). No → not metallic. Move on.
  2. Does it conduct when molten or in solution but not as a solid? Yes → ionic. This is the most distinctive single fingerprint.
  3. Is the melting point very low (below ~300 °C)? Yes → simple molecular.
  4. Is the melting point extremely high (above ~1000 °C) and the substance an insulator in all states? Yes → giant covalent.

A worked example: a substance has a melting point of 801 °C, does not conduct as a solid, and conducts when dissolved in water. Step 1: not metallic. Step 2: conducts when dissolved but not as a solid — that points clearly to ionic. Step 3 confirms it: 801 °C is high, consistent with a giant ionic lattice. (For reference, 801 °C is the real melting point of sodium chloride.)

A second example: a substance melts at –78 °C, does not conduct in any state and is insoluble in water. The very low melting point rules out ionic, giant covalent and metallic immediately. The substance is simple molecular — this is in fact solid carbon dioxide.

A third example: a substance melts at 3550 °C, does not conduct as a solid and does not dissolve in water. The huge melting point rules out simple molecular. The lack of conductivity rules out metallic. Insoluble in water rules out ionic. The only remaining class is giant covalent — this is diamond.

1.5.2.1 Comparing Ionic & Covalent Compounds F+H ▼

Comparing Ionic & Covalent Compounds

Spec 1.33: Explain the properties of ionic compounds — high melting and boiling points in terms of forces between ions, and conductivity as solid, molten and aqueous.

Spec 1.34: Explain the properties of typical covalent, simple molecular compounds — low melting and boiling points in terms of forces between molecules (intermolecular forces), and poor conduction of electricity.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Ionic compoundA compound made of positively and negatively charged ions arranged in a giant 3D lattice held together by strong electrostatic attractions
Simple molecular compoundA compound made of small, discrete molecules in which atoms are joined by covalent bonds, with weak intermolecular forces between molecules
Electrostatic attractionThe force of attraction between particles of opposite charge — the force holding ionic lattices together
Intermolecular forceA weak force of attraction acting between separate molecules (not within them)
Covalent bondA shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms — found inside simple molecules
Free to moveDescription of a charged particle (ion or electron) that is not held in a fixed position and can therefore carry an electric current

*Two completely different particle pictures*

Before you can explain why ionic and simple molecular compounds behave so differently, you need to picture them correctly. An ionic compound such as sodium chloride is not a collection of molecules — it is a giant 3D arrangement of alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, and every ion is attracted to every neighbouring oppositely charged ion. A simple molecular compound such as carbon dioxide is the opposite: it is a collection of separate CO₂ molecules. The C=O bonds are strong, but the molecules themselves only interact via weak intermolecular forces.

Melting and Boiling Points

*Why ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points*

To melt an ionic compound you have to break apart the lattice — the ions have to escape from their fixed positions in the regular 3D arrangement. That means overcoming the strong electrostatic attractions between every positive ion and every neighbouring negative ion. Because each ion is held in place by many surrounding ions of opposite charge, and because those attractions are strong, a very large amount of energy is needed. That energy is supplied as heat, which is why the melting and boiling points are high. The same logic explains boiling: more energy still is needed to completely separate the ions in the molten liquid into the gas phase.

A good Edexcel-level answer says all three things:

  1. The ions are arranged in a giant ionic lattice
  2. There are strong electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged ions
  3. A lot of energy is needed to overcome these forces

If you leave out the word "strong", or you say "to break the bonds" without saying which forces, you risk losing the mark.

*Why simple molecular compounds have low melting and boiling points*

When you melt or boil a simple molecular substance, you do not break the covalent bonds inside the molecules. You only have to overcome the weak intermolecular forces between separate molecules. Because these forces are weak, only a small amount of energy is needed, so the melting and boiling points are low — many simple molecular substances are gases or volatile liquids at room temperature (CO₂, H₂O, Cl₂, NH₃, CH₄).

This is the single most-tested discrimination in this topic. Saying that "the weak covalent bonds break" is the classic error, because the covalent bonds inside the molecule are strong — and they are not the ones being broken. The question is always about the intermolecular forces.

*Why bigger molecules generally have higher boiling points*

Although every simple molecular compound has relatively low boiling points compared to ionic compounds, there is still a trend within the simple molecular family. Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, so they need more energy to separate. This is why CH₄ is a gas at room temperature but heavier hydrocarbons such as petrol-range alkanes are liquids, and waxes are solids. You do not need this for the basic spec point, but it is useful background for why melting points within "simple molecular" are not all the same.

Conductivity

Electrical conductivity needs charged particles that are free to move. If the charge carriers are locked in place, no current can flow. This single principle explains every conductivity prediction at GCSE.

*Conductivity of ionic compounds — the three states*

StateConducts?Why
SolidNoIons are held in fixed positions in the lattice — they cannot move to carry charge
Molten (liquid)YesThe lattice has broken apart; ions are now free to move and carry charge
Aqueous solutionYes (if soluble)Ions are separated by water molecules and are free to move through the solution

When an ionic compound melts or dissolves, the lattice breaks apart and the ions become mobile. Each ion carries a charge, so an electric current can flow when a voltage is applied — positive ions move one way, negative ions move the other. In the solid state, the ions are still charged but cannot move, so no current flows.

A really common Foundation-tier wording error is to say that the electrons carry the charge in molten or aqueous ionic compounds. This is wrong — ionic compounds do not have delocalised electrons (that's metals). The charge carriers in molten or dissolved ionic compounds are ions.

*Conductivity of simple molecular compounds*

Simple molecular compounds do not conduct electricity in any state. The molecules themselves are uncharged — there are no ions and no delocalised electrons. Even though some molecular compounds dissolve in water, the solutions usually do not conduct (sugar solution, for example).

The exception you may meet is when a covalent molecular substance reacts with water to form ions — for example, hydrogen chloride gas (HCl) dissolves and produces H⁺ (aq) and Cl⁻ (aq), so hydrochloric acid conducts. But that is because ions have been formed in the reaction, not because the original HCl molecules carry charge.

Key Takeaways
  1. Ionic compounds are giant 3D lattices of oppositely charged ions held together by strong electrostatic attractions — they are not made of molecules.
  2. Simple molecular compounds are made of small, discrete molecules held together by weak intermolecular forces — the covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong but are not what melts or boils.
  3. Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points because a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions across the lattice.
  4. Simple molecular compounds have low melting and boiling points because only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules need to be overcome.
  5. Ionic compounds do not conduct as solids because the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice.
  6. Ionic compounds do conduct when molten or dissolved because the lattice has broken apart and the ions are now free to move and carry charge.
  7. Simple molecular compounds do not conduct electricity in any state because there are no ions and no delocalised electrons — the molecules themselves are uncharged.
  8. Saying "the weak covalent bonds break" when CO₂ boils is the single most common Higher-tier error — it is the intermolecular forces that are overcome.
  9. The charge carriers in molten or dissolved ionic compounds are ions, not electrons — saying "free electrons" is wrong for ionic compounds.
  10. The phrase "free to move" is essential when describing conductivity — saying ions are "free" alone is not enough.
  11. To deduce the bonding from a property table: high MP + conducts only when molten/dissolved → ionic; low MP + does not conduct in any state → simple molecular.

Diamond & Graphite

Spec 1.35: Recall that graphite and diamond are different forms of carbon and that they are examples of giant covalent substances.

Spec 1.36: Describe the structures of graphite and diamond.

Spec 1.37: Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why graphite is used to make electrodes and as a lubricant, whereas diamond is used in cutting tools.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AllotropeA different physical form of the same element, in which the atoms are arranged differently. Diamond and graphite are two allotropes of carbon
Giant covalent substanceA substance made of a huge 3D (or layered) network of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds throughout the whole structure
Covalent bondA shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms
Delocalised electronAn electron that is not fixed in one place or between two specific atoms — it is free to move through the structure and can carry charge
Layer (in graphite)A flat 2D sheet of carbon atoms arranged in hexagons; many of these sheets stacked on top of one another make up bulk graphite
Intermolecular forceA weak force of attraction between separate particles or layers (between the layers in graphite — not within a single layer)

*Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon — and yet they could not be more different*

Diamond is one of the hardest substances known. It is transparent, an electrical insulator, and used on the cutting edges of drills and saws. Graphite is opaque grey-black, slippery enough to use as a lubricant, and conducts electricity well enough to be used as electrodes in electrolysis. Both substances are made of nothing but carbon atoms. The only thing that is different between them is the arrangement of those atoms — that is, the structure. This is the central idea behind every answer on this spec point: identical building blocks can produce wildly different properties when arranged differently. The take-home is that structure determines properties, and at GCSE you must always link the two explicitly.

The Structures

*The structure of diamond*

In diamond, every carbon atom forms four covalent bonds with four other carbon atoms. Those four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron (109.5° between them), and that same pattern repeats in three dimensions across the whole crystal. The result is a giant 3D rigid network of strong C–C covalent bonds with no weak points anywhere in the structure. There are no separate molecules — a diamond crystal is essentially one enormous covalently bonded "molecule" containing many millions of carbon atoms. Critically, every one of carbon's four outer electrons is used in a bond, so there are no free electrons and no other charge carriers anywhere in diamond.

*The structure of graphite*

In graphite, every carbon atom forms only three covalent bonds with three other carbon atoms. Those three bonds lie in a flat plane, 120° apart, producing a flat layer made up of interlocking hexagonal rings (like chicken-wire). Stacks of these flat layers are then placed on top of one another to form bulk graphite. Within each layer the bonding is very strong. Between layers there are only weak intermolecular forces — no covalent bonds joining one layer to the next.

The crucial extra detail is what happens to carbon's fourth outer electron. Since each carbon only uses three electrons in covalent bonds within a layer, the fourth electron is not held between two atoms. Instead, it is delocalised — free to move through the whole layer. Graphite therefore contains a "sea" of delocalised electrons running throughout each layer, similar (but not identical) to the delocalised electrons in metals.

Why Diamond Has the Properties It Does

*Why diamond is very hard*

Every carbon atom in diamond is locked into the network by four strong covalent bonds pointing in different directions. To scratch, dent or chip diamond you would have to break a huge number of these strong C–C covalent bonds. Because no other substance can do this easily, diamond is one of the hardest naturally occurring materials and is used to put cutting edges on saws, drills and grinding wheels.

*Why diamond has a very high melting point*

Melting diamond means breaking apart the giant covalent network — you must overcome all of the strong C–C covalent bonds at the same time across the structure. Breaking strong covalent bonds requires a huge amount of energy, so the melting point of diamond is extremely high (over 3500 °C).

*Why diamond does not conduct electricity*

For something to conduct electricity it needs charged particles that are free to move. In diamond there are no ions, and crucially no delocalised electrons — every outer electron on every carbon is locked into a covalent bond. With nothing free to move and carry charge, diamond does not conduct. This is the property that most clearly tells diamond and graphite apart in exam data tables.

*Why diamond is used in cutting tools*

The cutting tools use rests on diamond being hard enough to scratch through other materials without being damaged itself. The exam answer must link the use to the structure: a giant 3D network of strong covalent bonds means lots of energy is needed to break any bonds, so diamond resists scratching and wear.

Why Graphite Has the Properties It Does

*Why graphite is soft and slippery (used as a lubricant)*

Within each layer of graphite the atoms are held very tightly by strong covalent bonds. But the layers themselves are only attracted to each other by weak intermolecular forces. When you rub graphite (or apply a small sideways force), the layers can slide over each other without breaking any covalent bonds. This is why pencil graphite leaves a mark on paper (layers flake off), and why powdered graphite works as a dry lubricant — the layers slide freely. The exam mark scheme typically wants three ideas: layers, weak forces between layers, layers slide.

*Why graphite has a very high melting point*

This is the property students most often get wrong. Despite being soft, graphite has a very high melting point. To melt graphite you would have to break the strong covalent bonds within the layers — not just slide layers past each other. Breaking those bonds requires a large amount of energy, just like in diamond, so the melting point is very high.

*Why graphite conducts electricity*

This is the killer property. Each carbon atom uses three of its outer electrons in covalent bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom. These delocalised electrons can move freely through the layer, so when a voltage is applied they carry an electrical current. Graphite is therefore an electrical conductor — unusual for a non-metal — and is used to make electrodes in electrolysis cells.

*Why graphite is used to make electrodes*

Electrodes must conduct electricity and survive the conditions inside an electrolysis cell (often hot, often reactive). Graphite ticks both boxes: its delocalised electrons let it conduct, and its very high melting point (strong covalent bonds within layers) means it does not melt or break apart even at high temperatures. Graphite is also cheap and easy to shape compared with metals.

*Why graphite is used as a lubricant*

Layers slide easily over one another because the forces between layers are weak. This means graphite reduces friction between two surfaces without needing oil. The link in the exam answer must be: weak intermolecular forces between layers → layers slide → low friction → lubrication.

The Single Most-Tested Discrimination

The question Edexcel keeps asking is: both diamond and graphite are made of carbon, so why do their properties differ? A complete answer needs all three of these ideas:

  1. The arrangement of carbon atoms is different (3D network vs flat layers).
  2. The bonding is therefore different — each C makes four bonds in diamond but only three in graphite.
  3. This difference creates different structural features with property consequences: graphite has weak forces between layers (so layers slide → soft, lubricant) and a delocalised electron per carbon (so it conducts), whereas diamond does not have either of these features.

Worked Example — Explaining Property Differences (Higher 6-mark style)

*Question style:** *"Diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon, but diamond is hard and does not conduct electricity, whereas graphite is soft and conducts electricity. Explain these differences in terms of structure and bonding."

*Procedure for a level-3 (5–6 mark) answer:*

  1. Both are giant covalent structures of carbon atoms held by strong covalent bonds — establish the shared starting point.
  2. In diamond, each carbon forms four covalent bonds in a rigid 3D network. To deform diamond you would have to break many strong covalent bonds, so it is hard.
  3. In diamond, all four outer electrons on each carbon are used in bonding, so there are no free (delocalised) electrons. No free charged particles → does not conduct.
  4. In graphite, each carbon forms only three covalent bonds, producing flat layers of hexagons. The layers are held to each other by weak intermolecular forces — layers can slide over each other, making graphite soft and slippery.
  5. In graphite, each carbon's fourth outer electron is delocalised and free to move along the layer. These delocalised electrons can carry a current → graphite conducts.
  6. Conclude by linking the explanation back to the property differences: different bonding numbers and different structural arrangements produce the different hardness and conductivity.

A level-3 response must reference: covalent bonds, the number of bonds per carbon (4 vs 3), layers and sliding for graphite, weak intermolecular forces between layers, and delocalised electrons. Missing any of these usually drops the answer to level 2 or below.

Fullerenes & Graphene

Spec 1.38: Explain the properties of fullerenes including C60 and graphene in terms of their structures and bonding.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Fullerenes and graphene are two more allotropes of carbon — different structural forms of the same element. Like diamond and graphite, they are built from carbon atoms joined by covalent bonds, but the way those atoms are arranged is different, and that arrangement is what gives each one its distinct set of properties. Once you can picture each structure, every property follows from the bonding.

Key Definitions

The Structures — What Each One Looks Like

*Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀)*

C₆₀ is a single molecule of exactly 60 carbon atoms arranged in a hollow ball. The surface of the ball is made up of 20 hexagons and 12 pentagons, fitted together in the same pattern as the panels on a traditional football. Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds to its three neighbours within the ball — so each carbon uses three of its four outer electrons in bonding pairs, leaving one electron per carbon delocalised over the surface of the molecule. The whole molecule is a single, discrete unit roughly 1 nanometre across, and the inside of the ball is hollow.

Because C₆₀ exists as separate, individual molecules (it is not a giant structure), it behaves more like a simple molecular substance in some respects: the C₆₀ molecules in solid buckminsterfullerene are held to each other by weak intermolecular forces. Inside each molecule, the C–C covalent bonds are strong, but between molecules the forces are weak — exactly the same picture as in CO₂ or I₂.

*Carbon nanotubes*

Carbon nanotubes are another type of fullerene, where the carbon atoms are arranged in a hexagonal pattern and rolled up into a long hollow cylinder. As with C₆₀, each carbon is covalently bonded to three other carbons, leaving one delocalised electron per atom available to carry charge along the tube. Nanotubes can be many micrometres long but only a few nanometres wide — they have a very high length-to-diameter ratio.

*Graphene*

Graphene is a single layer of graphite — one atom thick. Imagine peeling one sheet off a stack of graphite: that sheet is graphene. Inside the sheet, every carbon atom is covalently bonded to three other carbon atoms in a flat hexagonal arrangement, exactly like the rings inside a graphite layer. Because each carbon has four outer electrons and only three of them are used in bonding, one electron per carbon is delocalised over the whole sheet. That delocalised "sea" of electrons sitting above and below the plane of the sheet is what gives graphene its conductivity.

Unlike graphite, graphene is not a stack of layers — it is just one layer. There are no intermolecular forces between layers to worry about, because there is only ever one layer.

Properties Explained from Structure and Bonding

The spec asks you to explain the properties of fullerenes and graphene "in terms of their structures and bonding". That means every property answer needs the same recipe: name the structural feature, name the bonding feature, then link to the property.

*Why graphene is an excellent conductor of electricity*

Each carbon atom in graphene forms only three covalent bonds, leaving the fourth outer electron delocalised. These delocalised electrons are spread out across the whole hexagonal sheet and are free to move. When a voltage is applied across the sheet, the delocalised electrons flow through the structure, carrying charge. This is the same explanation you use for graphite conductivity and for metallic conductivity — the principle is identical: delocalised electrons that are free to move.

The Edexcel-level answer to "Explain why graphene will be a good conductor of an electric current" needs three points:

  1. Each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds (not four)
  2. This leaves one delocalised electron per carbon atom
  3. The delocalised electrons are free to move through the sheet and carry charge

*Why graphene is very strong and light*

Inside the sheet, every carbon atom is held in place by three strong covalent bonds to its neighbours. To deform or break the sheet you would have to break a lot of those strong covalent bonds, which takes a huge amount of energy. The strength-to-weight ratio is exceptional because each carbon atom is light, the sheet is only one atom thick, and the bonds inside the sheet are very strong. This is the property that makes graphene attractive for composite materials and electronics — it is reported to be many times stronger than steel by weight.

*Why graphene has a very high melting point*

The covalent bonds between carbon atoms inside the sheet are strong. To melt graphene you would have to break large numbers of those strong covalent bonds, which needs a great deal of energy. So like graphite and diamond, graphene has a very high melting point.

*Why C₆₀ (buckminsterfullerene) has a low melting point and is soft*

This is the trap. Although the C–C covalent bonds inside each C₆₀ molecule are strong, when you melt buckminsterfullerene you are not breaking those bonds — you are only separating C₆₀ molecules from one another. The forces between C₆₀ molecules are weak intermolecular forces, and only a small amount of energy is needed to overcome them. So solid buckminsterfullerene is soft and has a relatively low melting point compared to giant covalent structures like diamond or graphite.

This is the same trap as the CO₂ boiling-point question for simple molecular compounds: saying "the weak covalent bonds break" is wrong, because the covalent bonds are strong — what's weak is the intermolecular forces between molecules.

*Why C₆₀ can be useful for drug delivery and as a lubricant*

C₆₀ molecules are roughly spherical and hollow. Small drug molecules can be trapped inside the cage, and the C₆₀ "carrier" can then transport them around the body. Because C₆₀ molecules are roughly ball-shaped and only weakly attracted to each other, they can roll over one another, which is why they have lubricant properties.

*Why carbon nanotubes are strong along their length and conduct electricity*

Inside a nanotube every carbon is held in place by three strong covalent bonds — the same as in graphene — so the tube is very strong along its length. The remaining delocalised electron per carbon is free to move along the tube, so nanotubes conduct electricity along their length. This combination of high tensile strength and conductivity is why nanotubes are used in composites, electronics and as catalyst supports.

  12. A complete "Explain why graphene conducts" answer needs three points: three covalent bonds per carbon → one delocalised electron per carbon → electrons free to move and carry charge.
1.5.5.1 Polymers F+H ▼

Polymers

Spec 1.39: Describe, using poly(ethene) as the example, that simple polymers consist of large molecules containing chains of carbon atoms.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

This is the introductory Section 1 polymer spec point. The job here is to recognise what a polymer is, that it forms from many small monomer units joined together, that poly(ethene) is the standard example, and that the result is a very long molecule consisting of a chain of carbon atoms. The deeper polymer chemistry — drawing repeat units, working out the structure of a polymer from its monomer, distinguishing addition from condensation polymerisation — sits in Section 9 of the specification and is not required at this point.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
PolymerA very large molecule made by joining together a large number of small molecules called monomers
MonomerA small molecule that can join with many other identical (or similar) small molecules to form a polymer
PolymerisationThe chemical reaction in which many monomers join together to form a polymer
Poly(ethene)The polymer formed when many ethene (C₂H₄) molecules join together; the standard GCSE example
EtheneThe small alkene monomer, formula C₂H₄, that contains a C=C double bond and acts as the building block for poly(ethene)

*What you must be able to say in one sentence*

A polymer is a very long molecule made from many small monomer molecules that have joined together, and in a simple polymer like poly(ethene) those molecules form a long chain of carbon atoms.

Why Polymers Are Different From Everyday Small Molecules

Up to this point in Section 1 you have mostly met small molecules — water, carbon dioxide, methane, ammonia — and small ions, like Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in sodium chloride. A water molecule contains 3 atoms; a methane molecule contains 5. A single poly(ethene) molecule, by contrast, can contain thousands of carbon atoms joined end to end, and tens of thousands of hydrogen atoms attached around them. The same kind of covalent bond holds it together — a shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms — but the scale is completely different.

That change in scale is why polymers behave differently from the small molecules you met earlier. A simple molecular substance like CO₂ is a gas at room temperature because each CO₂ molecule is small and light, and the weak intermolecular forces between them are easily overcome. A poly(ethene) molecule is enormous: each long chain has many more places where it can interact with its neighbours, so the total intermolecular force adds up to something much larger. That is why poly(ethene) is a solid plastic at room temperature, not a gas.

You do not need to write a detailed explanation of those forces for spec 1.39 — that level of detail comes later — but you should understand that "polymer = simple molecular, but very long" is the right mental picture at GCSE.

Monomers and the Build-Up of a Polymer

A polymer is built by repeating one kind of small molecule — the monomer — many times. The monomer for poly(ethene) is ethene, C₂H₄. Ethene is the simplest alkene; its structure is two carbon atoms joined by a double bond, with two hydrogen atoms on each carbon:

`` H H \\ / C = C / \\ H H ``

Under the right conditions (high pressure, a catalyst, and heat) the C=C double bond can open up and the carbon atoms in one ethene molecule join to the carbon atoms in another ethene molecule. The new bond formed is a single C–C bond. As more and more ethene molecules join on, a very long chain of carbon atoms is built up. Each carbon in the chain still carries its two hydrogen atoms, so the end product is a hydrocarbon chain of the form:

`` … —CH₂—CH₂—CH₂—CH₂—CH₂—CH₂— … ``

Two important features of this picture:

  • The chain is made of carbon atoms joined by single covalent bonds. This is the "chain of carbon atoms" wording the spec uses.
  • All the bonds in the chain (and to the hydrogens) are covalent. A polymer is a covalent substance, not an ionic one.

For 1.39 you do not need to draw the repeat unit (that is Section 9), and you do not need to write a balanced equation. You only need to know that many ethene molecules join together to give a very long molecule with a chain of carbon atoms.

Poly(ethene) as the Required Example

The specification names poly(ethene) explicitly, so this is the example you should use whenever an exam question asks for one. Two practical notes:

  • Naming. "Poly(ethene)" with the brackets is the correct chemistry name. You may also see it written as polythene (the everyday word) or polyethylene (the older, American name). All three refer to the same substance. In an Edexcel exam, "poly(ethene)" is the safest spelling.
  • Uses. Poly(ethene) is the plastic used in carrier bags, shampoo bottles, drinks bottles and many wrappings and containers. You are not examined on uses for spec 1.39, but knowing the everyday context helps you remember that "poly(ethene)" really is the everyday plastic.

Comparing Ethene and Poly(ethene)

A single ethene monomer and a piece of poly(ethene) are very different substances — even though one is made entirely from the other.

FeatureEthene (monomer)Poly(ethene) (polymer)
Formula / sizeC₂H₄ — small, just two carbonsVery large; chains contain thousands of carbon atoms

The thing to notice is that the double bond in ethene is what makes it able to join up. When poly(ethene) forms, each double bond opens to give two new single bonds — one on each side of the carbon — and those new single bonds are what connect one former-monomer to the next. This means that in the polymer there are no C=C double bonds left; all the carbon–carbon bonds are single. You are not asked to discuss this mechanism at 1.39, but it is the reason the chain of carbon atoms exists in the first place.

Worked Example — Relative Formula Mass of Poly(ethene) From Its Monomer

Maths skill 5b (visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms) is the named maths skill for this SP, but the most common numerical question is a relative formula mass (Mr) comparison between a monomer and a polymer chain. It uses the same skills you meet in 1.6.1.1 Relative Formula Mass.

Worked example: A piece of poly(ethene) contains chains in which 1 000 ethene molecules have joined together. Calculate the relative formula mass of one chain. (Use Ar(C) = 12 and Ar(H) = 1.)

*Procedure:*

  1. Find the Mr of the monomer, ethene (C₂H₄):

Mr = (2 × 12) + (4 × 1) = 24 + 4 = 28

  1. When n ethene molecules join, no atoms are added or removed — the double bond simply opens to form new single bonds. So the Mr of the polymer chain is the Mr of the monomer multiplied by n.
  2. Mr (polymer chain) = 1000 × 28 = 28 000

Two points students lose marks on:

  • Using only the carbon atoms in the count (giving 1000 × 24) — you must include the hydrogens.
  • Treating polymerisation as if water were released (it isn't, for poly(ethene)) — addition polymerisation conserves all atoms.

In Foundation papers, the same calculation often appears as a simpler "Complete the table" or "State the formula" task, with the numbers chosen so the multiplication is straightforward.

1.5.6.1 Properties of Metals F+H ▼

Properties of Metals

Spec 1.40: Explain the properties of metals, including malleability and the ability to conduct electricity.

Spec 1.42: Describe most metals as shiny solids which have high melting points, high density and are good conductors of electricity whereas most non-metals have low boiling points and are poor conductors of electricity.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Metallic bondingThe strong electrostatic attraction between positively charged metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons
Delocalised electronsOuter-shell electrons that are no longer attached to any one metal atom and are free to move throughout the whole structure
Sea of electronsThe collective name for the delocalised electrons surrounding the metal ions in a metallic lattice
Metal ion (cation)A positively charged particle formed when a metal atom loses one or more of its outer-shell electrons
MalleableCan be hammered, bent or rolled into a new shape without breaking
DuctileCan be drawn out into a thin wire — a closely related property that comes from the same underlying structure
Electrical conductorA material that allows an electric current to flow because it contains charged particles that are free to move

*The particle picture for a metal*

Before any property can be explained, you need the correct picture in your head. A metal is a giant regular arrangement of positive metal ions packed closely together in layers. The outer-shell electrons of every metal atom have left the atom and now belong to the whole structure — they form a "sea" of delocalised electrons that fills the space around and between the ions. The structure is held together by the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the negative delocalised electrons. This is metallic bonding.

Two things follow immediately from this picture. First, the structure contains charged particles (the electrons) that are free to move. Second, the layers of ions are held in place by attractions that act in every direction — not by directional bonds between named atom pairs. Those two facts are the basis for every metal property on the spec.

Explaining the Properties of Metals (Spec 1.40)

*Why metals conduct electricity*

Electrical conductivity needs charged particles that are free to move. In a metal, the delocalised electrons in the sea are not held to any one ion and can drift through the whole structure when a voltage is applied. Electrons flow in from one end and out from the other, carrying charge along the wire. The positive metal ions stay where they are — they vibrate in their positions but do not move bodily through the metal. So in a metal, the charge carriers are electrons, not ions. This is the opposite of a molten or aqueous ionic compound, where ions carry the charge.

A common Foundation-tier wording slip is to say that the atoms or ions carry the charge in a metal. They do not. It is specifically the delocalised electrons (or "sea of electrons") that move and carry charge. Any explanation that does not mention "delocalised" or "free to move" risks losing the mark.

Metals also conduct in the molten state for the same reason — the delocalised electrons are still present and still mobile when the metal melts. This is different from ionic compounds, which only conduct when molten or dissolved. A metal conducts at every temperature where it still exists as a metal.

*Why metals are malleable*

Malleability comes from the layered arrangement of ions and the non-directional nature of metallic bonding. The ions in a metal sit in regular layers packed closely together. When a force is applied — for example, a hammer blow — one layer of ions can slide over the next. Because the surrounding sea of delocalised electrons is everywhere at once and is not tied to any particular pair of ions, the electrostatic attraction is preserved no matter how the layers shift. The structure changes shape rather than breaking apart. This is why metals can be bent, hammered into sheets, or rolled flat without shattering.

Compare this with an ionic compound. In an ionic lattice, the ions are in fixed alternating positions (positive next to negative). If you push the layers, like charges end up next to like charges, the lattice repels itself violently, and the crystal cleaves or shatters. The metallic equivalent never sets up that repulsion because the negative charge (the electron sea) flows around the displaced ions and keeps the attractions intact. That is also why metals are ductile (can be drawn into wires) — the same layer-sliding mechanism is at work.

*Why metals have high melting and boiling points*

To melt or boil a metal you have to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons. Because these attractions act throughout the whole giant structure and are strong, a lot of energy is needed. That energy must be supplied as heat, so the melting and boiling temperatures are high. Most metals are solid at room temperature for this reason. Mercury is the well-known exception (liquid at room temperature), and the alkali metals in Group 1 have lower melting points than the rest, but the general pattern is high melting and boiling points across the metallic block of the periodic table.

*Why metals are dense and shiny*

Density depends on how tightly packed the particles are. In a metal, the positive ions are packed as closely as the geometry of identical spheres allows, with the electron sea filling the gaps. This close packing, combined with the fact that metal atoms often have relatively large atomic masses (particularly transition metals such as iron, copper, lead, gold), gives most metals a high density.

The shiny appearance of a freshly cut metal surface is caused by the delocalised electrons. They sit very close to the surface and re-emit any light that hits them, producing the metallic lustre. This is why a freshly cut piece of sodium or a polished piece of copper looks shiny, and why metal surfaces can be polished to a mirror finish.

Metals vs Non-Metals — the Comparison Required by Spec 1.42

Spec point 1.42 asks you to describe the general property profile of metals and contrast it with that of non-metals. The word most is important — there are well-known exceptions (mercury is a liquid metal; carbon as graphite conducts even though it is a non-metal; iodine is a shiny non-metallic solid). But the general pattern is what you are expected to know.

Mathematical Skill — Visualising the Metallic Structure

The mathematical skill called up by spec 1.40 is 5b — visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms. In practice this means being able to:

  1. Draw the metallic structure as positive ions in regular layers with circled plus signs (e.g. Cu²⁺ or just +) and a sea of electrons shown as minus signs or shading around them.
  2. Label the diagram with positive metal ions and sea of delocalised electrons.
  3. Use the diagram to point at the feature responsible for a given property — for example, an arrow at the electron sea labelled "free to move — carries the current".

1.5.7.1 Limitations of Models F+H ▼

Limitations of Models

Spec 1.41: Describe the limitations of particular representations and models, to include dot and cross, ball and stick models and two- and three-dimensional representations.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Every diagram you draw of a molecule or lattice is a model — a simplified version of reality designed to make one feature easy to see. No single model can show everything at once. To answer 1.41 questions well, you need to know two things about each model: what information it does show, and what information it fails to show. The "fails to show" half is the part candidates most often miss.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Model (representation)A simplified picture, diagram or physical object used to represent a chemical structure — useful for one purpose but never a complete picture
Dot and cross diagramA 2D diagram showing the outer-shell electrons of each atom as dots or crosses, used to show electron transfer (ionic) or electron sharing (covalent)
Ball and stick modelA 3D representation in which atoms are shown as coloured spheres ("balls") and bonds as rigid rods ("sticks") joining them
2D structural formulaA flat drawing of a molecule using element symbols and lines for bonds, showing connectivity but not 3D shape
3D space-filling modelA 3D model in which atoms are shown as overlapping spheres scaled to relative atomic size, showing the overall shape and volume the molecule occupies
LimitationA feature of reality that a particular model cannot show or actively misrepresents

Why we need more than one model

A model is a tool. The tool you choose depends on the question you are trying to answer. If a student is learning where the bonding electrons come from in a water molecule, a dot-and-cross diagram is brilliant — it makes the electron sharing obvious. If the student then asks "what shape is the molecule?", dot-and-cross is suddenly useless and a ball-and-stick or space-filling model is needed instead. Real molecules have many features at once — electrons, bonds, shape, size, motion — so no single 2D drawing or 3D model can show every feature simultaneously without becoming unreadable.

This is why chemistry teaches several different models for the same molecule. Each emphasises a different feature and hides others. The skill being tested in spec 1.41 is knowing which model is appropriate for which job, and being able to state exactly what each one is missing.

Dot and Cross Diagrams

*What dot and cross diagrams show*

  • The number of outer-shell electrons on each atom
  • Which electrons come from which atom (different symbols for each atom)
  • Whether electrons are transferred (ionic bonding) or shared (covalent bonding)
  • The number of bonding pairs (covalent) or the resulting ion charges (ionic)
  • Whether atoms have full outer shells after bonding

*What dot and cross diagrams fail to show*

  • The 3D shape of the molecule — the diagram is drawn flat on the page, so bond angles are not real
  • The relative sizes of the atoms — every atom is shown as a circle of similar size regardless of which element it is
  • The motion of electrons — in a real atom or molecule, electrons are not stationary dots; they are in constant motion within orbitals or shells
  • The fact that electrons are indistinguishable — in real bonding, you cannot tell "which electron came from which atom"; the dots and crosses are a teaching convention, not physical reality
  • The distribution of electron density — electrons in covalent bonds are spread out in a cloud between the atoms, not pinned between them
  • The strength of the bonds or the energy involved in forming them
  • Anything about the structure beyond the atoms drawn — for a giant lattice, a dot-and-cross diagram only shows a small repeating fragment

Ball and Stick Models

*What ball and stick models show*

  • The positions of the atoms in 3D space
  • The bond angles between bonds at the same atom (e.g. 109.5° in methane, 104.5° in water)
  • The shape of the molecule (tetrahedral, bent, linear, trigonal planar, etc.)
  • The number of bonds to each atom
  • Whether bonds are single, double or triple (often shown by extra sticks between balls)
  • For giant structures, the repeating pattern of bonded atoms (e.g. diamond, graphite, ionic lattices)

*What ball and stick models fail to show*

  • Atoms are not really hard spheres with sharp edges — atoms have fuzzy electron clouds around them
  • Bonds are not rigid sticks — a covalent bond is a region of shared electron density, not a solid rod, and bonds can vibrate and rotate
  • The relative sizes of atoms are often misrepresented — balls are usually drawn far smaller than the atoms really are, so the gaps between atoms look much bigger than they really are
  • Electron density is not shown at all — you cannot see the bonding electrons
  • The motion of atoms and electrons is missing — atoms in real molecules vibrate, bend and rotate constantly
  • The model exaggerates empty space between atoms — in reality, the electron clouds of bonded atoms overlap and atoms touch one another

The "atoms are not really balls and bonds are not really sticks" point is the single biggest limitation of this model and is the one examiners reward most often on 1.41 questions.

2D Structural Formulae

*What 2D structural formulae show*

  • Which atoms are joined to which (the connectivity of the molecule)
  • The type of bond between each pair of atoms (single line, double line, triple line)
  • The molecular formula by inspection
  • For organic molecules, the functional groups present

*What 2D structural formulae fail to show*

  • The 3D shape of the molecule — the drawing is flat but the real molecule is 3D
  • The bond angles — angles drawn on paper rarely match the real angles in the molecule
  • The relative sizes of different atoms — every atom is just a letter
  • The fact that some molecules can have the same connectivity but different 3D arrangements
  • Anything about electrons, electron density, or where bonding electrons come from
  • Bond lengths and bond strengths

The strength of 2D structural formulae is that they are quick and easy to draw and easy to read. Their weakness is that the picture is fundamentally flat, even when the molecule is not.

3D Space-Filling Models

*What 3D space-filling models show*

  • The overall shape and volume the molecule occupies
  • The relative sizes of different atoms (hydrogen smaller, chlorine bigger, etc.)
  • How tightly packed the atoms are — the spheres overlap where atoms are bonded
  • The molecule's outer surface, which is what other molecules actually "see" and interact with
  • Why bigger molecules take up more space (useful for thinking about packing in liquids and solids)

*What 3D space-filling models fail to show*

  • The bonds are hidden or obscured — you cannot easily see where one bond ends and the next begins
  • Bond angles are hard to read because the atoms overlap
  • The fact that atoms are not really hard, opaque spheres — atoms have fuzzy electron clouds, not sharp surfaces
  • Electron density and electron sharing are not shown
  • Whether bonds are single, double or triple
  • The motion of atoms and electrons
  • Internal features of the molecule — anything tucked behind a larger atom is invisible

Space-filling models are excellent for showing shape and size, but if your question asks about bonding or electron arrangement, they are the wrong tool.

A neat way to remember this: dot-and-cross gives you electrons, ball-and-stick gives you angles and shape with bonds visible, 2D structural formulae give you connectivity quickly, and space-filling gives you shape and size. Every model trades one type of information for another.

Worked Example — Answering a 6-mark "describe the limitations" question

A typical exam stem gives you four diagrams of methane (CH₄): a dot-and-cross diagram, a 2D structural formula, a ball-and-stick model and a 3D space-filling model. The question asks: "Describe what each model shows about methane and the limitations of each model." (6 marks, levelled).

A full-mark answer needs both halves for each model: information shown and information missing. A practical structure is:

  1. Dot and cross — shows that each C–H bond is a shared pair of electrons, with one electron from carbon and one from hydrogen, and that carbon ends up with a full outer shell of 8 electrons. Limitation: it does not show that methane is tetrahedral; it shows the molecule flat with 90° angles on the page rather than the real 109.5°.
  2. 2D structural formula (H–C–H lines) — shows that there are four single C–H bonds. Limitation: also flat; bond angles drawn at 90° on paper are not the real bond angles.
  3. Ball and stick — shows the tetrahedral 3D shape and the 109.5° bond angles between the C–H bonds. Limitation: atoms are not really hard spheres and bonds are not really sticks; the gaps between atoms are exaggerated.
  4. 3D space-filling — shows the relative sizes of the carbon and hydrogen atoms and the real shape and volume of the molecule. Limitation: the bonds themselves are hidden, so you cannot see that there are four single C–H bonds.

The marks are won by saying both halves for each model. Saying only "ball and stick shows the shape" without saying what it fails to show typically caps you below full marks on a 6-mark question.

Mathematical Skills

*Spec 1.41 — Maths skill 5b: visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms, including two-dimensional representations of 3D objects.*

The mathematical demand on this spec point is spatial rather than numerical. You should be able to:

  1. Look at a 2D representation and visualise the corresponding 3D shape (e.g. read a 2D structural formula of methane and picture the tetrahedron)
  2. Look at a 3D model and describe its 2D representation (e.g. translate a ball-and-stick of water back to a dot-and-cross diagram)
  3. Recognise that 2D and 3D forms of the same molecule are showing different features of the same thing, not different molecules

No calculation is required.

How Limitations of Models Appears in Exams

This spec point most often appears as an extended-response question at the end of a Higher tier paper, where candidates are given several models of the same molecule (usually a small, familiar one such as methane or water) and asked to compare what each shows and the limitations of each.

*Higher tier (1CH0/2H):*

  • 6-mark levelled question describing the limitations of dot-and-cross, ball-and-stick and 3D representations of methane (1CH0/2H — June 2022, Q8d). Command word: Describe. Up to 6 marks.

*Foundation tier (1CH0/1F and 1CH0/2F):*

  • Shorter "give one limitation of this diagram" or "state what this model cannot show" questions tend to appear for 1–2 marks. The same chemistry applies, but the question is usually scoped to a single named model rather than a comparison across several.

Key Takeaways
  1. Every chemical model is a simplified picture that emphasises one feature and hides others — no single model can show everything at once.
  2. To answer a "limitations" question for full marks you must say both what each model does show and what it fails to show.
  3. Dot and cross diagrams show electron transfer/sharing and the origin of bonding electrons, but do not show 3D shape, real bond angles, atomic sizes or electron motion.
  4. Ball and stick models show 3D atom positions, bond angles and overall molecular shape, but atoms are not really hard spheres, bonds are not rigid sticks, and the gaps between atoms are exaggerated.
  5. 2D structural formulae show connectivity, bond types and functional groups quickly, but are flat and so cannot show 3D shape or real bond angles.
  6. 3D space-filling models show relative atomic sizes and the overall shape and volume of the molecule, but obscure the bonds and do not show electron density or motion.
  7. The right model depends on the question — choose the model that highlights the feature being asked about.
  8. Electrons in real bonds are not static dots; they are constantly moving regions of electron density between atoms.
  9. Atoms in real molecules vibrate and bonds can rotate — static models cannot show this.
  10. The 1CH0/2H June 2022 examiner report specifically flagged that candidates failed to explicitly state what models cannot show — describing only what is shown loses marks.
  11. The command word Describe with a 6-mark tariff at the end of a Higher tier paper is the most common setting for this spec point.
  12. A safe full-mark strategy: for each model, write one sentence on what it shows and one sentence on what it fails to show.
