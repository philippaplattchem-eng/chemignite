5.1.1.1 Transition Metals F+H ▼

Transition Metals

Syllabus reference 5.1C: Recall that most metals are transition metals and that their typical properties include high melting point, high density, the formation of coloured compounds, and catalytic activity of the metals and their compounds as exemplified by iron. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Transition metalA metal found in the central d-block of the Periodic Table, between Group 2 and Group 3 (Groups 3–12); shows the typical properties of high melting point, high density, formation of coloured compounds, and catalytic activity
d-blockThe central block of the Periodic Table containing the transition metals, sitting between Group 2 (alkaline earth metals) and Group 3
CatalystA substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up in the reaction
Coloured compoundA compound that absorbs some wavelengths of visible light, so its solution or solid appears coloured (e.g. blue copper(II) sulfate solution)
Alkali metalA reactive metal from Group 1 of the Periodic Table (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs); used here as a contrast to the transition metals

Position on the Periodic Table

The transition metals sit in the central d-block of the Periodic Table — the wide block running between Group 2 and Group 3 (so spanning Groups 3 to 12 in modern numbering). Common transition metals you should recognise include iron (Fe), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), nickel (Ni), chromium (Cr), manganese (Mn), cobalt (Co), silver (Ag), gold (Au) and platinum (Pt).

When the spec says "most metals are transition metals", it really does mean most — the d-block alone contains around forty metallic elements, dwarfing the small number of metals in Groups 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6.

*How to describe the position in one sentence*

Edexcel marks "state" questions strictly. A correct one-line description of the position of a transition metal is something like:

  • "It is in the d-block of the Periodic Table", or
  • "It is in the central block of the Periodic Table between Group 2 and Group 3", or
  • "It is a transition metal" (where the question allows that wording).

Vague answers — "in the middle", "between Groups 2 and 3" without further qualification, or just "it is a metal" — typically score zero on a "state" question because they do not actually identify the d-block / transition-metal region. The June 2019 examiner report makes this point explicitly (see Examiner Report Insights below).

The Four Typical Properties (Spec 5.1C)

The spec lists four properties you must recall. Each one contrasts with the equivalent property of the Group 1 alkali metals, so it is worth learning the two side by side.

*(a) High melting point*

Most transition metals are hard solids with high melting points. Iron, for example, melts at around 1538 °C and copper at around 1085 °C — both far above the melting point of any Group 1 metal. This is because the d-electrons take part in the metallic bonding alongside the s-electrons, giving stronger metal–metal bonding and a more rigid lattice. The exception you might meet is mercury (Hg), which is a liquid at room temperature, but high melting point is the general rule.

*(b) High density*

Transition metals are also dense — iron has a density of about 7.9 g/cm³, copper about 8.9 g/cm³, gold about 19.3 g/cm³. By comparison sodium has a density of only 0.97 g/cm³ (it floats on water). The high density comes from a combination of high atomic mass and tightly packed metal atoms in the lattice.

*(c) Formation of coloured compounds*

A characteristic feature of the transition metals is that their compounds and aqueous solutions are coloured, whereas Group 1 and Group 2 compounds are typically white solids that give colourless solutions. The colour comes from the partially filled d-orbitals in the transition-metal ion absorbing certain wavelengths of visible light; the transmitted/reflected light is what gives the characteristic colour.

You should be able to recognise the following solution colours, which are useful for ion-identification questions:

These colours are also the basis of the chemical tests for transition-metal ions you may meet elsewhere in the course (precipitation with sodium hydroxide solution producing coloured hydroxides).

*(d) Catalytic activity — exemplified by iron*

The spec singles out iron because of its use as a catalyst in industry. Transition metals and their compounds can act as catalysts because they can easily lose or gain electrons and switch between different oxidation states, providing alternative reaction pathways with lower activation energy.

The properties on the transition-metal side are exactly the four listed in spec 5.1C, plus "low reactivity" as the unspoken contrast that links 5.1C to everyday uses (e.g. why we use copper for plumbing and iron for construction).

Why the Properties Matter — Linking Theory to Use

Each of the four spec properties links directly to why transition metals are so useful in the real world:

  • High melting point + hardness → suitable for building, machinery, tools (iron in steel; titanium in aircraft).
  • High density → useful where mass matters (lead in radiation shielding; tungsten in counterweights — though tungsten/lead are edge cases).
  • Coloured compounds → used as pigments (cobalt blue, chromium green, iron oxide reds and yellows in paints and glazes) and for chemical tests.
  • Catalytic activity → essential for industrial processes (Haber Process, catalytic converters, hydrogenation of oils, decomposition of H₂O₂).

When an exam question asks "explain why this metal is suitable for…", the answer is almost always one of these four properties combined with low reactivity (so it does not corrode quickly in use).

Identifying a Transition Metal from Information Given

You may be given some properties of an unknown metal and asked whether it is likely to be a transition metal. The reasoning chain is:

  1. Look at the melting point — high (> ~1000 °C) → consistent with transition metal.
  2. Look at the density — high (> ~5 g/cm³) → consistent with transition metal.
  3. Look at the colour of its compounds or solution — coloured (blue, green, etc.) → strong evidence for transition metal.
  4. Look at any mention of catalytic activity — yes → strong evidence for transition metal.

A metal showing all four properties is almost certainly a transition metal. A metal that is soft, low-melting, low-density and produces white compounds is almost certainly a Group 1 or Group 2 metal.

5.1.2.1 Oxidation of Metals F+H ▼

Oxidation of Metals

Syllabus reference: 5.2C — Recall that the oxidation of metals results in corrosion. (Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry 1CH0 only, Paper 1.)

Metals do not stay shiny and pure when left out in the air. Over time — sometimes hours, sometimes years — they react with substances in their surroundings, and the metal at the surface is slowly converted into a different compound. This chemical destruction of a metal by reaction with its environment is called corrosion. The driving chemistry is oxidation: the metal atoms lose electrons (and usually gain oxygen) to become positively charged ions inside a new compound, most often a metal oxide or hydroxide. Corrosion is the everyday consequence of the fact that most metals are more stable as compounds than as the pure element — which is exactly why metals are found in nature as ores rather than as lumps of pure metal.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CorrosionThe gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with substances in its environment, especially oxygen and water
OxidationA reaction in which a substance gains oxygen, or (at Higher tier) loses electrons
RustingThe specific corrosion of iron (or steel) in the presence of both oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide
TarnishA thin layer of corrosion product (often an oxide or sulfide) that dulls the surface of a metal such as silver or copper
PatinaA green or blue-green corrosion layer that forms on copper or its alloys over long periods, mainly basic copper carbonate
Protective oxide layerA thin, impermeable layer of metal oxide (e.g. Al₂O₃) that forms on the surface of some metals and blocks further reaction

Corrosion as a form of oxidation

The simplest definition of oxidation taught at GCSE — and the one that matters for spec 5.2C — is gain of oxygen. When a metal corrodes in air, oxygen atoms from O₂ combine with metal atoms to form a metal oxide. In general terms:

metal + oxygen → metal oxide

For example, magnesium burns in air to form magnesium oxide:

2 Mg (s) + O₂ (g) → 2 MgO (s)

Magnesium burning is a fast, dramatic oxidation, but the same kind of reaction happens slowly to almost every metal exposed to air. A piece of magnesium ribbon left in the lab quickly becomes dull because a thin layer of MgO forms on the surface. Iron sheeting on a roof gradually flakes into orange-brown rust. Copper roofs on old buildings turn green. In every case the metal element has been converted into a compound — it has been oxidised.

(Higher tier) At Higher tier you also need to be comfortable with oxidation defined as loss of electrons. When iron rusts, each iron atom loses three electrons:

Fe → Fe³⁺ + 3e⁻

These electrons are picked up by oxygen (and water) which is therefore reduced. So corrosion is a redox process: the metal is oxidised, and oxygen — together with water in many cases — is reduced. Both definitions describe the same chemistry; "gain of oxygen" is just the easier face of it for Foundation tier.

Why does corrosion happen at all?

Metals are extracted from their ores using a great deal of energy — heat in a blast furnace, or electricity for reactive metals such as aluminium. Pure metal is therefore a high-energy form of the element. Reaction with oxygen converts the metal back into a lower-energy oxide, which is more chemically stable. Corrosion is the slow downhill slide back to the oxide form. The more reactive the metal, the steeper that slide and the faster the corrosion.

This is why reactivity-series knowledge from section 4 is so useful here. The more reactive the metal, the more readily it gives up electrons to oxygen, so the faster it corrodes:

  • Group 1 metals (sodium, potassium) tarnish in seconds — a freshly cut surface of sodium turns dull almost as you watch.
  • Magnesium and calcium corrode over days to weeks if left in moist air.
  • Iron and zinc corrode over months to years.
  • Copper and silver corrode over years to decades, producing thin coloured layers.
  • Gold and platinum essentially do not corrode under normal conditions, which is why gold jewellery and gold contacts in electronics stay shiny indefinitely.

This trend is the chemical reason gold has historically been used for coinage and jewellery, why platinum is used for catalytic converters, and why the most reactive metals have to be stored under oil to keep them away from air and water altogether.

Common examples of metal corrosion

The four examples below are the ones you are most likely to need at GCSE.

MetalConditionsProductAppearance
IronAir + waterHydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂OOrange-brown flaky rust
AluminiumAir (oxygen)Aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃Thin, hard, colourless protective layer
CopperAir + water + CO₂ over yearsBasic copper carbonate, Cu(OH)₂·CuCO₃Green patina (e.g. on church roofs, the Statue of Liberty)
SilverAir containing H₂SSilver sulfide, Ag₂SBlack tarnish on jewellery and cutlery

Iron rusting is the most economically important case because so much of our built environment is made of steel. The overall reaction can be summarised as:

4 Fe (s) + 3 O₂ (g) + 6 H₂O (l) → 4 Fe(OH)₃ (s)

The Fe(OH)₃ then dehydrates over time to give the familiar flaky orange-brown rust, hydrated iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O). The really important thing to remember is that rusting needs both oxygen and water. Iron in dry air is essentially fine; iron in pure water with all dissolved oxygen removed is also essentially fine. It is only the combination of the two that produces rust. (The detailed prevention methods — barriers, sacrificial protection, galvanising — belong to 5.1.2.2 Rusting of Iron and are not part of this spec point.)

Aluminium is a deceptive case. From its position high in the reactivity series you might expect aluminium to corrode faster than iron. In fact aluminium objects (drinks cans, window frames, aircraft skin) appear to last for decades without visible damage. The reason is that aluminium reacts with oxygen almost instantly to form a thin layer of Al₂O₃ on its surface:

4 Al (s) + 3 O₂ (g) → 2 Al₂O₃ (s)

Crucially, this oxide layer is hard, dense and impermeable: oxygen molecules cannot diffuse through it, so once it has formed it shields the metal underneath from any further reaction. Aluminium "doesn't corrode" only in the sense that its corrosion stops itself after a few atomic layers. Iron oxide, by contrast, is flaky and porous — it falls off and exposes fresh iron beneath, so the corrosion keeps going until the whole piece of iron has been eaten through.

Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series and does not react with water at all. In dry air copper just darkens slightly as a thin oxide film forms. Over many years in damp, CO₂-containing air it builds up the characteristic green basic copper carbonate patina. Like the Al₂O₃ layer, the patina is reasonably protective and is one reason copper roofs last centuries.

Silver does not react with oxygen at room temperature, but it does react with traces of hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) in air (from cooked eggs, certain rubbers and some industrial pollution) to form Ag₂S — the black tarnish you see on old cutlery and jewellery. This is still an oxidation in the GCSE sense — silver atoms have been converted into silver ions in a compound — even though no oxygen is involved.

Linking reactivity to corrosion rate

A common exam scenario is to be given two or more metals and asked to predict, with a reason, which will corrode fastest. The answer is anchored in the reactivity series:

  • The more reactive a metal, the more easily it loses electrons (or gains oxygen), so the faster it corrodes.
  • The less reactive a metal, the less readily it gives up electrons, so the slower it corrodes.

This is the principle behind sacrificial protection (covered in 5.1.2.2): you deliberately attach a more reactive metal (zinc or magnesium) to a less reactive one (iron) so that the more reactive metal corrodes preferentially.

Two lessons fall out of this:

  2. Do not invent a wrong reason. Iron does conduct electricity perfectly well — it is a metal. Saying iron "doesn't conduct" is wrong on the chemistry as well as wrong as a reason. Always sanity-check that any "reason" you write is actually true.

A second 2019 Higher question gave candidates an unfamiliar context — hydrazine, N₂H₄, reacting with oxygen inside heating systems to remove dissolved O₂ and protect the steel pipes from rust. Many candidates struggled to explain the link between the hydrazine reaction and the prevention of rusting:

  • 1CH0/1H — June 2019 Examiner Report: "The unfamiliarity of hydrazine caused some candidates difficulty... Some suggested that water was used up by hydrazine (therefore there was no rusting)... The second mark was often lost because the decrease in oxygen amount was not clearly linked to the fact that oxygen was needed for corrosion."

The chemistry to spell out in writing is:

  1. Hydrazine reacts with oxygen, so the amount of oxygen in the system goes down.
  2. Rusting (corrosion of iron) needs oxygen (as well as water).
  3. With less oxygen available, less rusting occurs.

Three sentences, two marks. The classic mistake is to stop after step 1 and assume the link is obvious — or, worse, to claim hydrazine removes the water (it does not; it reacts with O₂). Whenever an exam question gives you an unfamiliar substance reacting with oxygen, ask yourself: "Does this remove oxygen from the system?" If yes, that is the route to the mark.

5.1.2.2 Rusting of Iron F+H ▼

Rusting of Iron

Spec 5.3C: Explain how rusting of iron can be prevented by: a exclusion of oxygen, b exclusion of water, c sacrificial protection.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only · Paper 1.

Rusting is the everyday corrosion of iron and steel that turns shiny metal into a flaky, orange-brown solid. It only happens when both water and oxygen are present, so every prevention method works by removing one of these — or by using a more reactive metal to corrode in iron's place. You need to recognise the classic test-tube experiment, write the word and symbol equation for rust formation, and explain all three prevention methods in enough detail to score 2- and 3-mark structured questions.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CorrosionThe chemical breakdown of a metal at its surface caused by reaction with substances in its environment (typically oxygen and water)
RustingThe specific corrosion of iron (or steel) in the presence of both oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide
RustHydrated iron(III) oxide, formula Fe₂O₃·xH₂O; usually written for GCSE as Fe(OH)₃
OxidationA reaction in which a substance gains oxygen (or loses electrons)
Sacrificial protectionProtecting iron by attaching a more reactive metal that corrodes preferentially, sparing the iron
GalvanisingCoating iron or steel with a layer of zinc, which acts as both a physical barrier and a sacrificial metal

The word and symbol equations for rusting

*Word equation:*

iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide

*Symbol equation:*

4 Fe (s) + 3 O₂ (g) + 6 H₂O (l) → 4 Fe(OH)₃ (s)

Rust is sometimes written as Fe₂O₃·xH₂O to show that the amount of bound water is variable; for GCSE 1CH0 you can use Fe(OH)₃ as the product. The orange-brown colour, the flaky texture, and the fact that rust does not stick tightly to the iron underneath are all important. Because rust flakes off, fresh iron is constantly exposed to the air, so corrosion keeps eating into the object until it is destroyed. Compare this with aluminium, where the oxide layer is tight, impermeable, and protective — that's why aluminium does not need painting but iron does.

Why both oxygen and water are required

Rusting is an oxidation reaction in which iron loses electrons and combines with oxygen. The water is essential because it dissolves the oxygen and allows ions to move between the iron surface and the oxidising agent — effectively it acts as the electrolyte for the small electrochemical cells that form on the metal surface. Take away either reactant and the reaction stops:

  • No oxygen → iron has nothing to combine with; no oxide can form.
  • No water → the necessary ion movement cannot happen, and dry oxygen alone reacts very, very slowly at room temperature.

This is the single most important teaching point for this spec point. Every prevention method on the syllabus removes one of these two reactants, and every exam answer about rust prevention should make this link explicit.

Salt water makes it worse. Dissolved sodium chloride (in sea water or grit on a road in winter) increases the conductivity of the water film and so speeds up the electrochemical process. Iron objects rust noticeably faster in salt water than in fresh water, which is why cars rust quickly near the coast and on gritted roads.

The classic rusting experiment

The standard lab demonstration uses three (sometimes four) boiling tubes, each with an iron nail inside, set up for several days:

TubeContentsConditionsResult
AIron nail + tap waterWater present, air (oxygen) presentNail rusts
BIron nail + boiled water with a layer of oil on topWater present, but boiling removes dissolved oxygen and the oil stops air dissolving back inNo rust
CIron nail + anhydrous calcium chlorideDry air present (no water) — calcium chloride absorbs any moistureNo rust
D (variant)Iron nail + salt waterWater present, air present, electrolyte addedRusts faster than A

The experiment is the perfect "explain why" set-up because it isolates the two variables. Tube A is the control: both reactants present, rust forms. Tube B isolates oxygen by removing it from the water (boiling) and stopping it dissolving back in (oil layer) — no rust, so oxygen is essential. Tube C isolates water by using anhydrous calcium chloride as a drying agent — no rust, so water is essential. Adding tube D shows that salt acts as an accelerator.

*Worked answer — Nov 2020 Q7(a)(i) style (3 marks):*

> Explain why the iron nail in tube A would rust but the iron nails in tubes B and C would not rust.

  • A: Both water (in the tube) and oxygen (from the air) are present, so the iron reacts with oxygen and water to form hydrated iron(III) oxide / rust. (1)
  • B: Boiling the water has removed the dissolved oxygen, and the layer of oil prevents air dissolving back in, so there is no oxygen in contact with the nail and rusting cannot occur. (1)
  • C: The anhydrous calcium chloride absorbs any water vapour from the air, so the nail is in dry air; with no water present, rusting cannot occur. (1)

Notice how each mark explicitly names which reactant is missing and links it back to "rust needs both". That linkage is what examiners want — see the Examiner Report Insights below.

Method (a) — Exclusion of oxygen

To stop rusting by removing oxygen, you have to keep the iron away from air. In everyday life this is difficult, because air is everywhere, but two situations make it practical:

  • Storing iron under oil (e.g. in a sealed container in a lab). Oil floats on top and stops oxygen reaching the metal surface.
  • Boiling water then sealing it under oil, as in tube B of the classic experiment, to give a short-term oxygen-free environment.

For a 1-mark exam answer the key idea is that oxygen is one of the two reactants needed for rusting, so removing it stops the reaction. Just saying "store it under oil" without saying why does not score the explanation mark.

Method (b) — Exclusion of water

Excluding water is the most practical and most common approach because it can be done by applying a physical barrier to the surface of the iron. The barrier stops water from making contact with the metal, so the surface stays dry and rusting cannot start.

BarrierHow it worksTypical use
PaintingA continuous polymer film blocks water (and air) from the iron surfaceCars, ships, bridges, railings, garden tools
Oiling or greasingOil film keeps water off; can be reapplied easilyMoving parts of machinery, bike chains, hinges
Plating with tin (tin-plating)Tin does not react with food acids and provides a continuous barrierInside of food cans (steel cans coated with tin)
Plating with chromiumChromium gives a hard, shiny, corrosion-resistant finishDecorative trim on cars, taps, bathroom fittings
Plating with zinc (galvanising)Zinc barrier — and zinc is a sacrificial metal (see below)Buckets, dustbins, corrugated roofing, nails, car bodies
Anhydrous calcium chlorideAbsorbs water from the air (laboratory only)Lab demonstration in tube C

Why painting only works while it is intact: paint is a barrier, not a chemical protector. If the paint is scratched or chipped, water and air reach the exposed iron and rusting starts there. Once rust forms, it pushes the paint off and the corrosion spreads under the layer. That is why bridges (the Forth Bridge, for example) need constant repainting and why a scratched car needs the bare metal touched up quickly.

Tin-plating has a hidden weakness: tin is less reactive than iron. So if a tin-plated can gets scratched, the tin no longer protects the iron — in fact, contact between tin and iron in the presence of water can cause the iron to corrode faster than it would on its own (the iron becomes the more reactive metal in the pair and is preferentially oxidised). That is why dented tin cans should not be kept indefinitely.

Method (c) — Sacrificial protection

Sacrificial protection uses chemistry rather than a physical barrier. You attach a more reactive metal to the iron object. Because the more reactive metal loses electrons more readily than iron does, it corrodes preferentially — it is "sacrificed" — and the iron is left intact.

The choice of metal is governed by the reactivity series:

> K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > (C) > Zn > Fe > Sn > Pb > (H) > Cu > Ag > Au

Any metal above iron will work in principle. In practice zinc and magnesium are used because they are reactive enough, abundant, and reasonably cheap.

*Common examples:*

  • Zinc blocks bolted to the hulls of ships — sea water makes ship hulls rust very quickly; zinc blocks are bolted onto the steel hull and corrode in place of the steel. The blocks are inspected and replaced as they wear down.
  • Magnesium bars attached to underground pipelines and oil rigs — the bars provide sacrificial protection over long distances; engineers monitor them and replace them when they have corroded away.
  • Galvanising (zinc-coating buckets, roofing sheets, nails, dustbins) — zinc provides both a physical barrier (method b) and sacrificial protection (method c).

Why galvanising is special. Tin and zinc both act as barriers when intact, but they behave completely differently when scratched. A scratch in tin-plate exposes iron that then corrodes faster than bare iron (because tin is less reactive than iron). A scratch in galvanised iron exposes iron, but the zinc is more reactive than iron, so the zinc still corrodes preferentially and the iron stays protected even where the coating is broken. This is why galvanising is preferred for outdoor steel that will inevitably be scratched in use.

A common student trap — copper accelerates corrosion. If you connect a less reactive metal such as copper to iron in damp conditions, you create a small electrochemical cell in which the iron (now the more reactive of the pair) is preferentially oxidised. Copper rivets in a steel boiler, for example, can make the steel rust much faster than it would on its own. Sacrificial protection only works with a more reactive metal — the direction in the reactivity series matters.

Linking prevention to the rusting equation

When you write an exam answer, anchor every explanation to the rusting equation:

> 4 Fe (s) + 3 O₂ (g) + 6 H₂O (l) → 4 Fe(OH)₃ (s)

  • Method (a) removes O₂ → reaction cannot proceed.
  • Method (b) removes H₂O from the surface → reaction cannot proceed.
  • Method (c) doesn't remove either reactant; instead, the more reactive metal is oxidised in preference to the iron, so iron stays as Fe rather than becoming Fe(OH)₃.

Saying which reactant is removed (or why iron is no longer the metal that is oxidised) is what turns a vague answer into a 2- or 3-mark answer.

Key Takeaways
  1. Rusting is the corrosion of iron in the presence of both oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).
  2. Word equation: iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide. Symbol equation: 4 Fe + 3 O₂ + 6 H₂O → 4 Fe(OH)₃.
  3. If either oxygen or water is absent, rusting cannot occur — this is the key teaching point and the basis of every prevention method.
  4. Salt water (sea water, gritted roads) speeds up rusting because the dissolved ions act as an electrolyte; pure dry air does not cause rusting.
  5. The classic 3-tube experiment: A (water + air, rusts), B (boiled water + oil layer, no oxygen, no rust), C (anhydrous calcium chloride, no water, no rust).

  7. Method (b) — exclusion of water: physical barriers such as painting, oiling/greasing, and plating (tin, chromium, zinc). Cheap and widely used, but fails if the barrier is damaged.
  8. Method (c) — sacrificial protection: a more reactive metal (Zn or Mg) is attached to the iron and corrodes preferentially, sparing the iron. Used on ship hulls and pipelines.
  9. Galvanising combines methods (b) and (c): zinc is both a barrier and a sacrificial metal, so galvanised iron stays protected even when the zinc layer is scratched.
  10. Tin-plating is barrier-only — if scratched, iron rusts faster than bare iron because tin is less reactive than iron. Similarly, connecting iron to copper accelerates corrosion of the iron.
  11. Sacrificial protection only works with a metal above iron in the reactivity series. Zn ✓, Mg ✓, Cu ✗.
  12. In "explain" questions, always link the prevention method back to the rusting requirement — e.g. "the oil layer stops oxygen reaching the iron, and oxygen is needed for rusting." Examiners deduct marks when this linkage is left implicit.
5.1.2.3 Electroplating F+H ▼

Electroplating

Spec point 5.4C — Explain how electroplating can be used to improve the appearance and/or the resistance to corrosion of metal objects. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

Electroplating is one of the most common everyday applications of electrolysis. The shiny chromium trim on a car bumper, the silvery surface of a "silver" teaspoon you might find in a cutlery drawer, and the gold layer on a costume-jewellery ring are all produced by exactly the same process. By passing an electric current through a carefully chosen solution, a very thin layer of one metal is laid down on the surface of another. The object underneath can be cheap, plain, or vulnerable to corrosion; the coating gives it the appearance or chemical resistance of a much more expensive material.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ElectroplatingThe process of coating one metal with a thin layer of another metal by electrolysis
CathodeThe negative electrode in an electrolysis cell, where positive metal ions are reduced and deposited
AnodeThe positive electrode in an electrolysis cell, where metal atoms are oxidised and dissolve into solution
ElectrolyteThe ionic solution that conducts electricity between the electrodes; for electroplating it is a salt solution of the plating metal
ReductionThe gain of electrons (occurs at the cathode in electroplating)
OxidationThe loss of electrons (occurs at the anode in electroplating)

What electroplating is

Electroplating is a process of coating a metal object with a thin layer of another metal using electrolysis. The object you want to coat is dipped into a solution that contains ions of the plating metal, and a direct current is passed through the solution. Positive metal ions in the solution are attracted to the negative electrode, where they gain electrons and stick to its surface as solid metal. After a controlled time, the object is lifted out covered in a uniform layer of the new metal — typically only a few micrometres thick, but enough to change the way the surface looks and behaves chemically.

The thinness of the coating is what makes electroplating economically useful. Solid silver cutlery would be extremely expensive; silver-plated cutlery uses only a tiny fraction of the silver yet looks indistinguishable to most people. Solid chromium taps would be brittle and very costly; chromium-plated steel taps are tough, cheap, and stay shiny.

Apparatus and method

Every electroplating cell has the same four components: the object to be plated, a piece of the plating metal, an electrolyte, and a DC power supply.

ComponentWhat it isJob
CathodeThe object being plated (often a cheaper or less attractive metal)Connected to the negative terminal so it attracts positive metal ions
AnodeA pure piece of the plating metalConnected to the positive terminal; its atoms dissolve into the electrolyte to replace the ions used up at the cathode
ElectrolyteAn aqueous solution of a salt of the plating metalProvides the metal ions that travel through the solution and are deposited on the cathode
DC power supplyDirect-current source (battery or low-voltage power pack)Drives electrons through the external circuit; the direction of current must be constant so ions move the right way

The choice of electrolyte is tied to the plating metal. To copper-plate an object, the electrolyte is a copper(II) salt solution such as copper(II) sulfate (CuSO₄ (aq)). To silver-plate an object, the electrolyte is a silver salt solution, typically silver nitrate (AgNO₃ (aq)) or silver cyanide. For chromium plating the electrolyte contains chromium ions. The general rule is: the electrolyte contains the ions of the metal you are depositing.

What happens at each electrode

At the cathode (the object being plated), positive metal ions in the electrolyte are attracted to the negative surface. They gain electrons from the external circuit and are reduced to metal atoms, which build up as a coating:

`` Mⁿ⁺ (aq) + n e⁻ → M (s) ``

At the anode (the pure plating metal), the reverse happens. Metal atoms at the surface lose electrons and dissolve into the electrolyte as positive ions:

`` M (s) → Mⁿ⁺ (aq) + n e⁻ ``

These two processes are mirror images of one another. For every metal atom that plates onto the cathode, an atom of the same metal leaves the anode and enters the solution. The concentration of metal ions in the electrolyte stays essentially constant during plating, which is why a single bath of electrolyte can plate many objects in succession without being replaced.

The connection to copper purification (3.2.5.2 Explaining the Electrolysis of CopperII Sulfate) is exact: same electrolyte, same electrode reactions, same direction of ion movement. The only difference is the cathode — in purification it is a thin sheet of already-pure copper that grows thicker; in electroplating it is the object you want to coat.

Worked example — silver-plating a spoon

Suppose you want to electroplate a steel spoon with silver to make it look like solid silver cutlery.

*Apparatus*

  • Cathode: the steel spoon (connected to the negative terminal of the DC supply)
  • Anode: a strip or rod of pure silver (connected to the positive terminal)
  • Electrolyte: silver nitrate solution, AgNO₃ (aq)
  • Power supply: low-voltage DC

At the cathode (the spoon): silver ions in the solution are attracted to the spoon, gain one electron each and are deposited as a thin layer of solid silver on its surface.

`` Ag⁺ (aq) + e⁻ → Ag (s) ``

At the anode (the silver rod): silver atoms lose one electron each, leaving the rod and entering the solution as Ag⁺ ions.

`` Ag (s) → Ag⁺ (aq) + e⁻ ``

The silver rod slowly gets thinner, the spoon develops an even silver coating, and the concentration of Ag⁺ in the silver nitrate stays roughly the same. Once the layer is thick enough — usually after a few minutes to an hour, depending on current and surface area — the spoon is lifted out, rinsed, and polished. It now looks like solid silver, even though the bulk of the spoon is still steel.

The same approach works for any other metal coating; just swap the anode for the new plating metal and the electrolyte for one of its salts.

Why use electroplating? — reasons in detail

The spec point asks you to explain how electroplating can be used to improve the appearance or the resistance to corrosion of metal objects. Both are valid exam answers, but in practice industry electroplates for several overlapping reasons.

Improve appearance. A cheap base metal can be coated with a more attractive, shinier metal. Silver-plating gives cutlery and trophies a bright, polished look. Gold-plating gives costume jewellery the appearance of solid gold without the cost. Chromium-plating gives car parts and bathroom taps a mirror-like finish that resists tarnishing.

Prevent corrosion. A reactive metal underneath can be protected from oxygen and water by a less reactive coating. Chromium-plating steel car parts means the chromium — which does not corrode in everyday conditions — forms a barrier that stops the iron underneath from rusting. Tin-plating steel food cans works the same way: the tin layer keeps the iron inside the can away from air and moisture so it cannot oxidise. (See 5.1.2.2 Rusting of Iron for why barrier coatings work.)

Reduce cost. Plating with a thin layer of an expensive metal uses far less of that metal than making the whole object out of it. A silver-plated spoon contains only a tiny mass of silver, but looks like solid silver. This makes the finished product affordable while keeping the surface properties of the precious metal.

Improve hardness or wear resistance. Some coatings are chosen because they are harder than the base metal. Chromium-plating tools or engine parts gives a tough, wear-resistant surface that lasts much longer than uncoated steel.

In an exam, "improve appearance" and "protect from corrosion" are the two reasons explicitly named by spec point 5.4C. Cost and hardness are valid additional reasons but must be stated specifically — see the examiner-report insights below.

Comparison — electroplating vs alloying vs galvanising

Students often confuse these three ways of protecting or improving a metal. They are very different.

ProcessWhat happensWhere the second metal ends upExample
ElectroplatingElectrolysis deposits a thin layer of one metal onto the surface of anotherOnly on the surface, as a coatingSilver-plated cutlery; chromium-plated taps
AlloyingTwo or more metals (or a metal and a non-metal) are mixed while molten and allowed to cool togetherMixed all the way through the bulk materialSteel (iron + carbon); brass (copper + zinc)
GalvanisingSteel is coated with a layer of zinc, usually by dipping in molten zincOn the surface — but the zinc also gives sacrificial protection by corroding in place of the iron underneathGalvanised steel buckets, car bodies

The most common exam trap is to write that electroplating "makes the metal stronger" — that is alloying, not electroplating. Electroplating changes only the surface; it does not change the strength of the object underneath.

Tips for getting full marks

  • Name the metal coating and the property it gives — not just "protects" or "looks better". Say "the chromium layer stops oxygen and water reaching the iron, so the iron cannot rust" or "the silver layer makes the steel spoon look like solid silver cutlery".

  • Avoid the word "cheaper" on its own. Say specifically what makes it cheaper — for example, "uses less silver than making the whole spoon out of silver".

  2. The object being plated is the cathode (negative electrode); the plating metal is the anode (positive electrode).
  3. The electrolyte is an aqueous solution of a salt of the plating metal (e.g. silver nitrate for silver-plating; copper(II) sulfate for copper-plating).
  4. At the cathode, metal ions gain electrons and are deposited as solid metal: Mⁿ⁺ (aq) + n e⁻ → M (s).
  5. At the anode, atoms of the plating metal lose electrons and dissolve into the electrolyte: M (s) → Mⁿ⁺ (aq) + n e⁻.
  6. Because the anode replaces what the cathode removes, the concentration of metal ions in the electrolyte stays essentially constant.
  7. Electroplating is used to improve appearance (e.g. silver-plate cheap cutlery, gold-plate jewellery, chromium-plate taps).
  8. Electroplating is used to prevent corrosion (e.g. chromium-plate steel car parts, tin-plate steel food cans).
  9. Other valid reasons include saving cost (less of an expensive metal needed) and improving hardness or wear resistance (e.g. chromium-plating tools).
  10. In exam answers, be specific — "protects from corrosion" earns the mark; just "protects" does not; "makes it stronger" is alloying, not electroplating; "cheaper" alone is too vague.
  11. Electroplating differs from alloying (which mixes metals throughout the bulk) and from galvanising (zinc coating that also gives sacrificial protection).
  12. The same set-up, run with a thin pure-copper cathode in copper(II) sulfate, is used industrially to purify copper (3.2.5.2) — same chemistry, different purpose.

Alloys

Spec point 5.5C: Explain, using models, why converting pure metals into alloys often increases the strength of the product. Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only · Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AlloyA mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually other metals, sometimes carbon), designed to improve a specific property such as strength, hardness or corrosion resistance
Pure metalA metal made up of atoms of only one element, arranged in regular layers of identical-size atoms
MalleableAble to be hammered or rolled into a shape without breaking — a property that depends on layers of metal atoms being able to slide over each other
HardnessThe resistance of a material to being scratched, indented or worn down
StrengthThe resistance of a material to being deformed or broken under a force

Why pure metals are often too soft to use

Pure metals are not always strong enough for everyday jobs. A pure metal is made up of atoms of a single element, all the same size. These atoms pack together in regular layers — neat rows of identical spheres, like marbles stacked in a tray.

When a force is applied (for example, you hit it with a hammer, or you load it with weight), those regular layers can slide over each other quite easily. There is nothing to stop one layer slipping past the next, because every atom is the same size and the layers are uniform. This is why pure metals are:

  • Malleable — you can hammer them into shapes (sheets, wires, jewellery)
  • Relatively soft — easy to scratch or dent
  • Often not very strong — they bend or deform under load

Pure copper, pure aluminium and pure iron are all good examples. They conduct electricity and heat very well, but on their own they are too soft and weak for most structural uses. Pure gold (24-carat) is so soft you can scratch it with a fingernail, which is why rings and jewellery are almost never made from it on its own.

The particle model — pure metal vs alloy

Spec point 5.5C asks you to explain, using models, why alloys are stronger than pure metals. The expected "model" is the 2D particle diagram showing layers of metal atoms (math skill 5b — translating between diagrammatic and numerical forms).

*Pure metal (2D model):*

`` ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ``

Rows of identical circles in straight, regular lines. Every atom is the same size. Layers can slide cleanly over one another → soft, malleable, less strong.

*Alloy (2D model):*

`` ○ ○ ○ ● ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ● ○ ○ ○ ○ ● ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ● ○ ○ ○ ○ ``

Mostly identical circles, but with occasional larger or smaller atoms (the added element) disrupting the lattice. The packing is no longer perfectly regular. Layers can no longer slide easily over one another, because the differently-sized atoms get in the way → harder, stronger, less malleable.

When you sketch this in an exam, the two key features the examiner is looking for are:

  1. The pure metal shown as regular layers of identical atoms
  2. The alloy shown as the same regular layers, disrupted by atoms of a different size

You should label each diagram clearly ("pure aluminium" / "alloy") and, if asked, annotate the arrow showing where layers can or cannot slide.

Why alloys are stronger — the explanation in words

When a different element is added to a pure metal, its atoms are a different size to the host metal's atoms. Even a small percentage of a different element is enough to disturb the regular packing. The added atoms sit in places that no longer line up neatly with the rest of the lattice.

When a force is applied to the alloy:

  • The layers try to slide, just as they would in the pure metal
  • But the differently-sized atoms create obstacles
  • The layers cannot slide easily past each other
  • A bigger force is now needed to deform the metal

The result is that the alloy is:

  • Harder than the pure metal (more resistant to scratching/denting)
  • Stronger than the pure metal (more resistant to deformation under load)
  • Less malleable than the pure metal (harder to hammer into shape — because that "hammering" is exactly what we just stopped from happening)

This last point is important and is a common exam trap: students sometimes claim alloys are more malleable than pure metals. They are not. Disrupting the lattice always makes layer-sliding harder, so the alloy loses some malleability in exchange for the gain in strength.

Specific reasons matter — the "stainless steel vs iron" trap

5.1.3.2 Steel Alloys F+H ▼

Steel Alloys

Spec point 5.6C: Explain why iron is alloyed with other metals to produce alloy steels. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

Steel is the most economically important alloy in the world. It is made by adding controlled amounts of carbon — and sometimes other metals such as chromium, nickel, tungsten, vanadium or molybdenum — to iron. By changing what is added and how much is added, chemists and engineers can tune the properties of steel to suit very different jobs, from food cans to surgical scalpels to drill bits.

This spec point asks you to explain why iron is alloyed in the first place, and to recognise the differences between the main types of alloy steel. The exam questions almost always hinge on linking a property (hardness, strength, resistance to rusting) to a use, and to the composition that gives that property.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AlloyA mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually other metals or carbon) designed to improve the properties of the pure metal
SteelAn alloy of iron containing a small percentage of carbon, and sometimes other metals
Mild steelA low-carbon steel containing roughly 0.05–0.25% carbon; malleable and easily shaped
High-carbon steelA steel containing roughly 0.6–1.5% carbon; hard but more brittle than mild steel
Stainless steelAn alloy of iron with chromium (typically 10–25%) and often nickel; resists rusting because chromium forms a protective oxide layer
Tool steelA steel alloyed with tungsten, vanadium and/or molybdenum; very hard and retains its hardness at high temperatures

Why pure iron is unsuitable for many uses

Iron straight out of the blast furnace is not a good engineering material on its own. There are three main problems with using pure iron:

  1. It is relatively soft. The atoms in pure iron are arranged in regular layers of identical-sized particles. When a force is applied, these layers can slide over each other fairly easily, so the metal deforms and dents.
  2. It is very malleable but lacks strength. Pure iron bends and stretches more readily than is useful for load-bearing structures, blades or tools.
  3. It rusts. When iron is exposed to both oxygen and water — for example in moist air — it reacts to form hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust). Rusting flakes away from the surface, exposing fresh iron beneath, so corrosion continues until the object is destroyed.

These problems explain why almost no everyday iron object is made from pure iron — it is converted to steel first.

Why alloying changes the properties so dramatically

The structural reason alloys behave differently from the pure metal is covered in 5.1.3.1 Alloys. In short:

  • Pure iron has a regular lattice of identical iron atoms arranged in layers. Layers can slip past each other, making the metal soft and easily bent.
  • When other elements (carbon, chromium, nickel, tungsten, vanadium or molybdenum) are added, their atoms are a different size from iron atoms.
  • These different-sized atoms disrupt the regular lattice, preventing the layers from sliding over each other as easily.
  • The alloy therefore becomes harder and stronger than pure iron.

This is the central explanation you need for any "explain why alloy steel is harder than pure iron" question. The amount and type of element added then controls how hard, how strong, and what other properties (rust resistance, heat resistance) the steel ends up having.

Adding carbon to iron — the most economically important alloy

Just a small amount of carbon transforms iron. Carbon atoms are much smaller than iron atoms and they sit in the gaps in the iron lattice, distorting it. Even tiny percentages of carbon produce a dramatic change in strength and hardness.

There are two main carbon-only steels you need to know:

*Mild steel (low-carbon steel, ~0.05–0.25% C)*

  • Properties: still relatively soft and malleable, but much stronger than pure iron. Easily pressed, bent, welded and shaped.
  • Common uses: car body panels, food and drink cans, ship hulls, beams and girders for large structures.
  • Why these uses: any application where you need to bend or stamp the metal into a shape benefits from low carbon content.

*High-carbon steel (~0.6–1.5% C)*

  • Properties: much harder than mild steel and holds a sharp edge, but more brittle — it will snap rather than bend under heavy impact.
  • Common uses: cutting tools, chisels, hammers, springs, knife blades.
  • Why these uses: you want the edge or surface to resist wear, even if the object would crack if dropped.

The trade-off is clear: as carbon content increases, hardness increases but ductility and malleability decrease.

Stainless steel — the rust-resistant alloy

Stainless steel is an alloy of iron with chromium (typically 10–25%) and often nickel. It is the classic exam example because it tackles iron's biggest weakness: rusting.

*Why stainless steel does not rust*

Chromium is more reactive towards oxygen than iron is. When stainless steel is exposed to air, the chromium at the surface reacts with oxygen to form a thin, transparent, impermeable layer of chromium(III) oxide (Cr₂O₃) on the outside of the metal.

This layer:

  • Is only a few atoms thick but completely covers the surface.
  • Is non-porous, so water and oxygen cannot reach the iron beneath.
  • Is self-healing: if the surface is scratched, the freshly exposed chromium immediately reacts with oxygen in the air and the protective layer re-forms within seconds.

Because the iron underneath is sealed off from oxygen and water, the conditions required for rusting (see 5.1.2.2 Rusting of Iron) are never met, and the steel does not rust.

*Other properties of stainless steel*

  • Stronger and harder than pure iron, for the same particle-model reason as other alloys.
  • Has an attractive, shiny, polished appearance that resists tarnishing.
  • Can be sterilised easily — important for medical and food-grade uses.

*Common uses*

  • Cutlery (knives, forks, spoons)
  • Surgical instruments
  • Kitchen sinks and worktops
  • Saucepans
  • Chemical reaction vessels and pipework in chemical plants

Tool steels — hardness at high temperatures

Tool steels are iron alloyed with metals such as tungsten, vanadium and/or molybdenum. These atoms are large and dense, and they disrupt the iron lattice particularly effectively.

  • Properties: extremely hard; crucially, the hardness is retained even at high temperatures. Many tools heat up rapidly in use (a drill bit can glow red while cutting), and ordinary high-carbon steel would soften. Tool steel does not.
  • Common uses: drill bits, lathe cutting tools, milling cutters, dies for stamping metal.

Using Metals

Spec point 5.7C: Explain how the uses of metals are related to their properties (and vice versa), including aluminium, copper and gold and their alloys including magnalium and brass. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

This spec point is fundamentally about linking — connecting a specific property of a metal to a specific real-world use, and being able to explain why that property makes the metal suitable for that use. The single most common form this takes in the exam is a 6-mark open-response question on Foundation tier where you must give a property, link it to a use, and justify the link. Marks are almost never given for naming a use alone; the reasoning is what scores.

Why each property matters — the reasoning behind the table

Density. Density is mass divided by volume. A metal with a low density gives you a large structure that is still light to lift or move. Aluminium has a density of about 2.7 g/cm³ — roughly one-third that of steel — which is the single reason aluminium dominates aerospace. Less mass to lift means less fuel burnt, longer range, and a higher payload. The same logic applies to overhead power cables: a cable that sags under its own weight needs more pylons, so a lighter conductor is cheaper to install over long distances.

Electrical conductivity. Metals conduct electricity because their outer electrons are delocalised — they are free to move through the lattice when a voltage is applied. Copper and gold are among the best conductors at room temperature. Copper is cheaper, so it dominates household wiring. Gold is reserved for places where corrosion would otherwise ruin the connection — for example the gold-plated pins on a computer memory module or the bonding wires inside a microchip.

Thermal conductivity. The same delocalised electrons that carry charge also carry kinetic energy. Copper transfers heat rapidly from a hot hob into the contents of a pan, so cooking is even and responsive. Cheaper saucepans often have a copper base joined to a stainless-steel body to combine good heat transfer with the toughness and corrosion-resistance of steel.

Resistance to corrosion. Different metals resist corrosion in different ways. Aluminium is actually very reactive — it sits above iron in the reactivity series — but as soon as fresh aluminium meets air it forms a thin, hard layer of aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) on its surface. This layer is bonded tightly to the metal and seals it from further attack, so the bulk of the metal stays unreacted. Gold, by contrast, is low in the reactivity series and simply does not react with oxygen or water under normal conditions — it stays shiny without needing a protective layer at all. Copper is in between: it does not react with water, but in damp air over long periods it slowly forms a green coating (verdigris) which is why old copper roofs turn green.

Malleability. Metals are malleable because the layers of positive ions in the lattice can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bond. Gold is exceptionally malleable — a single gram can be beaten into a sheet about one square metre across (gold leaf). Copper is malleable enough to be drawn into long wires without snapping. This is why both metals appear in jewellery and electrical components.

Why alloying changes properties. Pure metals have regular layers of identical ions that slip easily, which is why pure copper and pure aluminium are relatively soft. Adding a second element of a different size disrupts the regular layers — the new atoms get in the way of layers sliding past each other. The alloy is therefore harder and stronger than the pure metal, although usually less ductile.

  • Magnalium = aluminium + magnesium. Magnesium atoms break up aluminium's regular lattice, making it stronger and stiffer while keeping the overall density low. This is why aircraft are not built from pure aluminium — they use magnalium and similar alloys to gain strength without losing the lightness advantage.
  • Brass = copper + zinc. Zinc atoms harden the copper lattice and shift the colour towards a yellow-gold appearance. Brass is also more resistant to corrosion than pure copper. The result is a hard, attractive, weather-resistant metal used for musical instruments, taps, door handles and decorative fixtures.

See 5.1.3.1 Alloys for the full mechanism of why alloys are harder than pure metals.

Foundation versus Higher tier

This SP is examined at both Foundation and Higher tier but the depth of the question can differ. At Foundation tier, the 6-mark "list and explain" version is common. At Higher tier, the same content can appear inside a longer question that also asks you to interpret data — for example a table of densities, melting points or conductivities — and choose the best metal for a given engineering job. The chemistry content is the same; the Higher-tier version expects you to extract evidence from a stimulus before reasoning.

Common pitfalls

  • Giving a property without a use (or vice versa). "Aluminium has a low density" alone scores nothing on a "state and explain" question — you need "low density, so it is used for aircraft bodies because the plane is lighter".
  • Confusing aluminium's behaviour with rusting. Aluminium does not rust (rusting is specific to iron). It forms a tough Al₂O₃ oxide layer that protects it — this is why aluminium window frames last for decades.
  • Saying brass and magnalium are pure metals. They are alloys, and the question often explicitly asks why the alloy is better than the pure metal — the answer is "harder/stronger because the different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers".
  • Listing too many properties for one metal and ignoring the others. A 6-mark question with five metals named in the stem expects roughly one mark per metal — so spread your coverage. Don't write three sentences on gold and forget magnalium.
  • Vague reasoning. "Copper is used for wires because it conducts" is weaker than "Copper is used for wires because it is an excellent conductor of electricity, so very little energy is lost as the current flows".

5.2.1.1 Using Concentrations in mol/dm³ H only ▼

Using Concentrations in mol/dm³

Syllabus reference: 5.8C — Calculate the concentration of solutions in mol dm⁻³ and convert concentration in g dm⁻³ into mol dm⁻³ and vice versa. **Higher tier only · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only.**

Concentration tells you how much solute is dissolved in a fixed volume of solution. At Foundation tier you only need to express concentration in grams per cubic decimetre (g dm⁻³). At Higher tier on the Separate Chemistry paper, you must also express it in moles per cubic decimetre (mol dm⁻³) and switch between the two units. This SP is the gateway to every titration calculation in Topic 5.2, so the recipe below needs to become automatic.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ConcentrationThe amount of solute dissolved in a known volume of solution
g dm⁻³Grams of solute dissolved per cubic decimetre (per litre) of solution
mol dm⁻³Moles of solute dissolved per cubic decimetre of solution; sometimes written M or "molar"
SoluteThe substance that dissolves (e.g. copper sulfate, sodium hydroxide)
SolventThe liquid the solute dissolves in (almost always water at GCSE)
SolutionThe mixture of solute + solvent
Mr (relative formula mass)The sum of the relative atomic masses of every atom in a formula unit; the mass in grams of one mole

Why two different units?

Both g dm⁻³ and mol dm⁻³ measure the same physical thing — how packed a solution is — but they answer different questions.

  • g dm⁻³ is what you weigh out on a balance. It is intuitive but treats every chemical the same: 10 g dm⁻³ of NaOH and 10 g dm⁻³ of HCl contain very different numbers of particles because their formula masses are different.
  • mol dm⁻³ counts particles, not mass. Because chemical reactions happen in fixed mole ratios from balanced equations, mol dm⁻³ is the unit that lets you predict how much of one solution will react with another. This is why every titration calculation works in mol dm⁻³, not g dm⁻³.

Switching between the two requires only one piece of information: the Mr of the solute. That is the bridge.

The two key equations

Both equations share the same structure — something divided by volume in dm³. The "something" is either mass or moles.

*Concentration in g dm⁻³:*

> Concentration (g dm⁻³) = mass (g) ÷ volume (dm³)

*Concentration in mol dm⁻³:*

> Concentration (mol dm⁻³) = moles ÷ volume (dm³)

Or written more compactly, with C = concentration, m = mass, n = moles, V = volume in dm³:

`` C (g dm⁻³) = m / V C (mol dm⁻³) = n / V ``

You can rearrange either equation to make mass, moles, or volume the subject. The two most useful rearrangements are:

  • moles = concentration × volume → n = C × V
  • mass = concentration × volume → m = C × V

Converting between units

Once you know the Mr of the solute, the conversion is a single step:

  • g dm⁻³ → mol dm⁻³: divide by Mr
  • mol dm⁻³ → g dm⁻³: multiply by Mr

This makes sense from the definition of the mole: if 1 mole of a substance has a mass of Mr grams, then Mr grams in 1 dm³ equals 1 mol dm⁻³. So dividing grams by Mr converts the mass into moles, and the "per dm³" part is unchanged.

Volume conversion — the #1 mark-loss source

Almost every concentration question gives a volume in cm³, but every concentration equation needs a volume in dm³. You must convert:

> 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ > volume (dm³) = volume (cm³) ÷ 1000

So 25.0 cm³ becomes 0.0250 dm³. 50.00 cm³ becomes 0.05000 dm³. 250 cm³ becomes 0.250 dm³.

Examiners flag this conversion as the single most common error candidates make on this spec point. Get into the habit of writing the dm³ value down as the very first step of every calculation, even when you think you'll remember.

The standard 5-step procedure

Use this recipe for every question in this SP. It works whether you start in mass or moles, in cm³ or dm³, and whether you finish in g dm⁻³ or mol dm⁻³.

  1. Identify what's given — is it a mass (g) or a number of moles? Is the volume in cm³ or dm³? Is the final answer wanted in g dm⁻³ or mol dm⁻³?
  2. Convert volume to dm³ — if the question gives cm³, divide by 1000 immediately. Write the dm³ value down before doing anything else.
  3. Calculate Mr — if you need to switch between mass and moles, work out the Mr of the solute by adding the Ar values of every atom in the formula. Show this on a separate line.
  4. Substitute into C = m/V or C = n/V — pick the right equation. Rearrange if you are asked to find mass, moles, or volume instead of concentration.
  5. Check sig figs / decimal places — read the question for any specified precision. If none is given, match the lowest sig-fig data in the question (usually 2 or 3).

Worked Example 1 — mol dm⁻³ from g dm⁻³ and finding moles in a fixed volume

Based on 1CH0/1H, June 2019, Q8(c) — copper sulfate (CuSO₄).

A solution of copper sulfate has concentration 15.95 g dm⁻³. Calculate the number of moles of copper sulfate in 50.00 cm³ of this solution.

Step 1 — Identify what's given: mass concentration in g dm⁻³, volume in cm³, asked for moles. We'll need to convert to mol dm⁻³ first, then use n = C × V.

*Step 2 — Convert volume to dm³:*

> 50.00 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.05000 dm³

*Step 3 — Calculate Mr of CuSO₄:*

> Mr(CuSO₄) = 63.5 + 32 + (4 × 16) = 63.5 + 32 + 64 = 159.5

*Step 4 — Convert g dm⁻³ → mol dm⁻³:*

> Concentration (mol dm⁻³) = 15.95 ÷ 159.5 = 0.100 mol dm⁻³

*Step 5 — Find moles in the 50.00 cm³ portion:*

> moles = concentration × volume > moles = 0.100 × 0.05000 = 0.00500 mol (3 sig figs)

So 50.00 cm³ of 15.95 g dm⁻³ copper sulfate solution contains 0.00500 mol (or 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol) of CuSO₄.

Note the two traps that examiners flagged on this exact question: candidates who divided 159.5 by 15.95 (the wrong way round, giving 10) and candidates who forgot to divide the volume by 1000 (using 50 directly, which gives an answer 1000× too big). A third common error was dividing 15.95 by 50 instead of by Mr.

Worked Example 2 — g dm⁻³ from mass and volume in cm³

Based on 1CH0/1F, November 2020, Q8(b) — calculate the concentration of a solution in g dm⁻³.

A student dissolves 4.50 g of sodium chloride in water and makes the solution up to 250 cm³. Calculate the concentration of the solution in g dm⁻³.

Step 1 — Identify what's given: mass (4.50 g), volume in cm³ (250 cm³), asked for g dm⁻³. We don't need Mr because we are staying in mass units.

*Step 2 — Convert volume to dm³:*

> 250 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³

Step 3 — Calculate Mr: not needed (no unit switch).

*Step 4 — Substitute into C = m/V:*

> C = 4.50 ÷ 0.250 = 18.0 g dm⁻³

Step 5 — Sig figs: the data is given to 3 sig figs, so 18.0 g dm⁻³ is correct.

If the question then asked for the concentration in mol dm⁻³, you would calculate Mr(NaCl) = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 and divide: 18.0 ÷ 58.5 = 0.308 mol dm⁻³.

Worked Example 3 — mol dm⁻³ to g dm⁻³

A solution of sodium chloride has a concentration of 0.200 mol dm⁻³. Calculate the concentration in g dm⁻³.

Step 1 — Identify what's given: concentration in mol dm⁻³, asked for g dm⁻³. We need Mr.

Step 2 — Volume conversion: not needed (no volume given).

*Step 3 — Mr(NaCl):*

> Mr = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5

*Step 4 — Multiply by Mr:*

> Concentration (g dm⁻³) = 0.200 × 58.5 = 11.7 g dm⁻³

Step 5 — Sig figs: 3 sig figs in the input, 3 sig figs in the answer. Done.

Common errors to avoid

  • Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ — your answer will be 1000× too big (or too small if you over-correct). Always convert at Step 2.
  • Dividing the wrong way round when converting units — to go from g dm⁻³ to mol dm⁻³ you divide by Mr; the units cancel correctly. If your answer for mol dm⁻³ is a huge number (like 10), you have probably done Mr ÷ concentration instead of concentration ÷ Mr.
  • Using the volume in cm³ as if it were in dm³ in n = C × V — extremely common; the dm³ value is almost always less than 1, while the cm³ value is usually larger than 10.
  • Ignoring the question's specified precision — if the question says "give your answer to 1 decimal place" or "to 3 significant figures", you must round to that precision or you'll lose a mark.
  • Using the wrong Mr — double-check by counting every atom in the formula, including atoms inside brackets (e.g. Ca(OH)₂ has 2 oxygens and 2 hydrogens, not 1 each).

`` n = m / Mr (moles from mass) C (g dm⁻³) = m / V (in dm³) C (mol dm⁻³) = n / V (in dm³) V (in dm³) = V (in cm³) / 1000 g dm⁻³ → mol dm⁻³: divide by Mr mol dm⁻³ → g dm⁻³: multiply by Mr ``

Core Practical: Acid-Alkali Titration

Spec point 5.9C — Carry out an accurate acid-alkali titration, using burette, pipette and a suitable indicator. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

This is the accurate acid-alkali titration practical. The Separate Chemistry version focuses on the quantitative side — measuring volumes precisely enough that you can later calculate an unknown concentration. It overlaps with the salt-preparation titration in 3.1.9.1 Prepare a Salt by Titration, but here the emphasis is on rough vs accurate runs, concordant results, and sources of error rather than on getting a pure dry salt out at the end.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
TitrationA technique for finding the exact volume of one solution that reacts with a known volume of another, using an indicator to detect the end-point
End-pointThe point in a titration at which the indicator changes colour, showing that the acid and alkali have just neutralised each other
TitreThe volume of solution delivered from the burette to reach the end-point, measured in cm³
Rough titrationA first, fast titration used to estimate roughly where the end-point lies

Why a two-stage method is used

A common reason candidates lose marks on this practical is treating the titration as a single procedure. In reality you carry out two distinct stages: a rough titration to find approximately where the colour change happens, then repeat accurate titrations to home in on a precise volume. The rough run lets you add acid quickly in the early stages of each accurate run — saving time — and then slow right down once you are within about 1–2 cm³ of the expected end-point. Without the rough, every accurate run would have to be done dropwise from the start, which is slow and prone to overshooting the end-point.

The 1CH0/1F — June 2019 Examiner Report noted that lower-level responses "often focused on either the titration or the crystallisation method" rather than covering the whole procedure when asked to describe how to carry out an acid-alkali experiment in full. The lesson for this practical is the same: describe both the rough and the accurate stages, not just one, if the question asks how to carry out an accurate titration.

Choosing a suitable indicator

The indicator must give a sharp colour change at the end-point — universal indicator changes through a wide range of colours gradually, so it is not suitable for titrations even though it is fine for showing whether a solution is acidic or alkaline.

For an HCl + NaOH titration, phenolphthalein is the standard choice: the flask goes from pink (alkaline) to colourless at the end-point, and a single drop of acid is enough to discharge the colour completely.

Method

The full procedure for an accurate titration of NaOH (aq) with HCl (aq):

*Stage 1 — Set up*

  1. Using a funnel, fill the burette with the acid (e.g. HCl) above the 0.00 cm³ mark, then run a little out through the tap to fill the tip and remove any air bubble below the tap.
  2. Remove the funnel from the top of the burette. Record the initial burette reading to the nearest 0.05 cm³, taking the reading from the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  3. Use a pipette filler to draw exactly 25.0 cm³ of the alkali (e.g. NaOH) into a 25 cm³ pipette, then release it into a clean conical flask. The pipette should have been rinsed with the alkali first so that any residual water doesn't dilute the solution it will deliver.
  4. Place the conical flask on a white tile under the burette. Add 2–3 drops of phenolphthalein indicator — the solution turns pink.

*Stage 2 — Rough titration*

  1. Open the burette tap and run acid into the flask fairly quickly, swirling the flask continuously with the other hand to mix the contents.
  2. Stop as soon as the solution turns from pink to colourless — this is the rough end-point. Record the final burette reading and calculate the rough titre (final − initial).

*Stage 3 — Accurate titrations*

  1. Refill the burette and use a fresh 25.0 cm³ portion of alkali in a clean conical flask. Add 2–3 drops of phenolphthalein.
  2. Run acid in quickly until you are about 1–2 cm³ short of the rough titre. Then slow right down, adding acid dropwise while swirling, until a single drop of acid just turns the solution colourless. Record the accurate titre.
  3. Repeat the accurate run until you have at least two concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other). Discard any anomalous values and the rough run.

*Stage 4 — Calculating the mean titre*

  1. Take the mean of the concordant accurate titres only. This mean titre is the volume of acid that exactly neutralises 25.0 cm³ of the alkali. The calculation step itself (turning that into a concentration or mass) belongs to spec point 5.2.3.1 Titration Calculations.

The seven-step procedure

This is the routine to run on every titration calculation, whether the unknown is a concentration or a volume.

  1. Find the mean (concordant) titre. Take the readings that agree to within 0.10 cm³ and average them. Discard the rough titre and any titres outside the concordant range.
  2. Convert volumes from cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000. This step is the single most common error in the exam — keeping volumes in cm³ gives an answer 1000 times too small or too big.
  3. Calculate moles of the known solution using n = c × V (with V in dm³).
  4. Use the balanced equation to read off the mole ratio between the known reactant and the unknown reactant. Never assume 1 : 1 — diprotic acids and dihydroxide bases give 1 : 2 or 2 : 1 ratios.
  5. Calculate moles of the unknown by scaling the known moles by the ratio.
  6. Calculate the required quantity for the unknown — usually concentration (c = n / V) or volume (V = n / c), depending on what the question asked.
  7. Round to the appropriate precision. Match the significant figures of the data (typically 3 sf) or the decimal places specified in the question. Failing to give the answer "to one decimal place" when asked is a documented mark-loser (see Examiner Report Insights below).

Key formulae

`` n = c × V (moles from concentration and volume) c = n / V (concentration from moles and volume) V = n / c (volume from moles and concentration) ``

Where:

  • n = amount in moles (mol)
  • c = concentration in mol dm⁻³
  • V = volume in dm³ (always convert cm³ → dm³ first)

A useful conversion when a concentration is given in g dm⁻³ rather than mol dm⁻³:

`` c (mol dm⁻³) = c (g dm⁻³) ÷ Mr ``

This is needed when a question gives you a concentration as a mass per volume (for example 15.95 g dm⁻³ copper sulfate) and asks for moles.

Why the mole ratio matters more than anything else

Step 4 is where most marks are dropped. Students who have done many 1 : 1 titrations (HCl + NaOH, HNO₃ + KOH) sometimes carry a 1 : 1 assumption into a question where the equation is not 1 : 1.

The two common traps are:

  • Diprotic acids — sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) donates two H⁺ ions per molecule, so 1 mol of H₂SO₄ neutralises 2 mol of a monohydroxide base like NaOH.
  • Dihydroxide bases — Ca(OH)₂ and Ba(OH)₂ release two OH⁻ ions per formula unit, so 1 mol of Ca(OH)₂ needs 2 mol of a monoprotic acid like HCl.

Always balance the equation first, then read the ratio from the balancing numbers.

Worked Example 1 — Unknown concentration

> 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide solution is exactly neutralised by 23.5 cm³ of hydrochloric acid. Calculate the concentration of the hydrochloric acid in mol dm⁻³.

*Step 1 — Equation and mole ratio*

`` HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O ``

Mole ratio HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1.

*Step 2 — Convert volumes*

  • V(NaOH) = 25.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0250 dm³
  • V(HCl) = 23.5 ÷ 1000 = 0.0235 dm³

*Step 3 — Moles of the known (NaOH)*

`` n(NaOH) = c × V = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol ``

Step 4 — Moles of the unknown (HCl) via 1 : 1 ratio

`` n(HCl) = 0.00250 mol ``

*Step 5 — Concentration of HCl*

`` c(HCl) = n / V = 0.00250 / 0.0235 = 0.10638... mol dm⁻³ ``

*Step 6 — Round to 3 sf*

`` c(HCl) = 0.106 mol dm⁻³ ``

Worked Example 2 — Unknown volume (non-1 : 1 ratio)

> Calculate the volume of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid needed to exactly neutralise 50.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ calcium hydroxide solution. Give your answer in cm³.

*Step 1 — Equation and mole ratio*

`` Ca(OH)₂ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + 2 H₂O ``

Mole ratio Ca(OH)₂ : HCl = 1 : 2. This is not 1 : 1 — Ca(OH)₂ has two OH⁻ groups, so each mole reacts with two moles of HCl.

*Step 2 — Convert volume*

`` V(Ca(OH)₂) = 50.0 ÷ 1000 = 0.0500 dm³ ``

*Step 3 — Moles of the known*

`` n(Ca(OH)₂) = c × V = 0.200 × 0.0500 = 0.0100 mol ``

Step 4 — Moles of the unknown via 1 : 2 ratio

`` n(HCl) = 2 × 0.0100 = 0.0200 mol ``

*Step 5 — Volume of HCl*

`` V(HCl) = n / c = 0.0200 / 0.500 = 0.0400 dm³ ``

*Step 6 — Convert back to cm³ (because the question asked for cm³)*

`` V(HCl) = 0.0400 × 1000 = 40.0 cm³ ``

Notice the unit-conversion bookend: cm³ → dm³ for the calculation, then dm³ → cm³ for the final answer when the question asks for cm³. Missing the back-conversion is another routine slip.

Worked Example 3 — Converting g dm⁻³ to mol dm⁻³ and finding moles in a volume

This example mirrors the exact question scenario flagged by examiners in the June 2019 Higher paper (see citation below).

> A solution of copper(II) sulfate has a concentration of 15.95 g dm⁻³. The relative formula mass (Mr) of CuSO₄ is 159.5. Calculate the number of moles of CuSO₄ in 50.00 cm³ of this solution.

*Step 1 — Convert g dm⁻³ to mol dm⁻³*

`` c(CuSO₄) = 15.95 ÷ 159.5 = 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ``

The order of division matters: it is mass per volume divided by mass per mole, which leaves moles per volume. Inverting the ratio (159.5 ÷ 15.95) is the single biggest error trap on this style of question.

*Step 2 — Convert volume*

`` V = 50.00 ÷ 1000 = 0.05000 dm³ ``

*Step 3 — Moles in the given volume*

`` n = c × V = 0.100 × 0.05000 = 0.00500 mol ``

*Step 4 — Quote to the precision the data supports*

`` n(CuSO₄) = 0.00500 mol (3 sf) ``

If a follow-up part asks for the answer "to one decimal place" — for example, a final mass in grams — make sure you write it that way. 0.6 g and 0.60 g and 0.59 g are not interchangeable in the mark scheme.

5.2.4.1 Yield F+H ▼

Yield

Spec 5.11C: Calculate the percentage yield of a reaction from the actual yield and the theoretical yield.

Spec 5.12C: Describe that the actual yield of a reaction is usually less than the theoretical yield and that the causes of this include: (a) incomplete reactions, (b) practical losses during the experiment, (c) competing, unwanted reactions (side reactions).

This is a Separate Chemistry only spec point and is examined on Paper 1 at both Foundation and Higher tier.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Theoretical yieldThe mass of product you would obtain if the reaction went 100% to completion with no losses, calculated from the balanced equation and the masses of the reactants
Actual yieldThe mass of product actually obtained from the experiment, measured on a balance after the product has been separated and dried
Percentage yieldThe actual yield expressed as a percentage of the theoretical yield; a measure of how efficient a reaction is at converting reactant into the desired product
Side reactionA competing reaction in which the reactants form an unwanted product instead of (or as well as) the desired product

The percentage yield formula

The formula you need to learn — and which appears on every yield question — is:

`` % yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100 ``

The actual yield and theoretical yield must be in the same units before you divide. Almost always this will be grams, but it could be kilograms or even moles. If the question gives you actual yield in grams and theoretical yield in kilograms (or vice versa), convert one of them first.

The answer is a percentage — never write a unit such as "g" or "mol" on the final answer. A value between 0% and 100% is the expected range for the vast majority of reactions.

*Why percentage yield matters*

In industry, chemists need to know how efficient a process is. A reaction that gives only 30% yield wastes 70% of the reactants either as unconverted starting material, lost product, or unwanted side products — all of which cost money and may need to be separated or disposed of. A high percentage yield means more product is made per kg of reactant used, lowering the cost per kg of product and reducing waste.

Why actual yield is usually less than theoretical yield

Spec point 5.12C requires you to be able to describe three reasons why the actual yield of a reaction is usually less than the theoretical yield. These map directly to parts (a), (b) and (c) of the spec, and one of these three is almost always what an examiner is looking for when a "Suggest a reason…" question is asked.

*a) Incomplete reactions*

Not all reactant molecules react. This happens for two main reasons:

  • The reaction is reversible and reaches dynamic equilibrium before all the reactants are converted to products. At equilibrium, some reactant always remains unreacted, so the product mass is less than the theoretical maximum. Reactions such as the Haber process and the contact process are reversible and never go to 100% completion.
  • The reactants are not mixed thoroughly enough, or the reaction is stopped before completion (for example, by quenching or cooling too early). Reactant molecules that never collide cannot react.

*b) Practical losses during the experiment*

In any practical procedure, some product is lost between the reaction vessel and the final balance. Common practical losses include:

  • Solution spilled when pouring between containers
  • Solid residue stuck on filter paper or in the funnel
  • Product left clinging to the sides of the beaker after transfer
  • Product lost as vapour during evaporation or heating
  • Crystals not fully recovered from a recrystallisation
  • Product lost while washing or drying

These losses are physical — the product was made, but never reached the balance. They are usually small but unavoidable in school-level practical work.

*c) Competing, unwanted reactions (side reactions)*

Some of the reactant is used up by a different reaction happening at the same time, producing an unwanted product instead of (or as well as) the desired one. For example:

  • In organic chemistry, an alkene can polymerise instead of adding to a specific reagent.
  • In a substitution reaction, an alternative product may form if a competing nucleophile is present.
  • In the combustion of a hydrocarbon, incomplete combustion (giving carbon monoxide or soot) competes with complete combustion (giving carbon dioxide).

Because the reactant is "used up" by the side reaction, less is available to form the desired product, lowering the actual yield.

Worked example 1 — straightforward % yield

A student carries out a reaction that should produce a theoretical yield of 50.0 g of potassium chloride. The actual mass of potassium chloride collected is 38.5 g. Calculate the percentage yield.

*Step 1 — Write the formula:*

`` % yield = (Actual / Theoretical) × 100 ``

*Step 2 — Substitute the values (same units, both in grams):*

`` % yield = (38.5 / 50.0) × 100 ``

*Step 3 — Calculate:*

`` % yield = 0.770 × 100 = 77.0% ``

Answer: 77.0% (to 3 significant figures)

Notice that the answer is less than 100%, which is what you would expect for a real reaction. If you ever calculate a value greater than 100% in a straightforward question, check your arithmetic — but also see the trap below.

Worked example 2 — from a balanced equation

A student reacts magnesium with excess hydrochloric acid:

Mg (s) + 2HCl (aq) → MgCl₂ (aq) + H₂ (g)

They start with 2.40 g of magnesium and obtain 8.55 g of magnesium chloride. Calculate the percentage yield. (A_r values: Mg = 24, Cl = 35.5)

*Step 1 — Calculate moles of magnesium used:*

`` moles Mg = mass / Ar = 2.40 / 24 = 0.100 mol ``

*Step 2 — Use the equation to find moles of MgCl₂ produced:*

The ratio Mg : MgCl₂ is 1 : 1, so moles of MgCl₂ formed (theoretically) = 0.100 mol.

*Step 3 — Calculate Mr of MgCl₂:*

`` Mr(MgCl₂) = 24 + (2 × 35.5) = 24 + 71 = 95 ``

*Step 4 — Calculate the theoretical yield in grams:*

`` mass MgCl₂ = moles × Mr = 0.100 × 95 = 9.50 g ``

*Step 5 — Apply the % yield formula:*

`` % yield = (Actual / Theoretical) × 100 % yield = (8.55 / 9.50) × 100 = 90.0% ``

*Answer: 90.0%*

This kind of multi-step calculation is the most common way Higher-tier students see % yield in the exam: you have to find the theoretical yield first from reacting masses before you can apply the % yield formula.

Decision tree — checking your answer makes sense

Before you write down your final % yield, run this quick check:

*Is your answer between 0% and 100%?*

  • Yes → Your answer is plausible. If the question asks you to explain why the % yield is not 100%, give one of the three spec reasons: (a) incomplete reactions, (b) practical losses, (c) side reactions.
  • No (greater than 100%) → Either you have made an arithmetic mistake (most likely), or the question is testing the special trap where actual yield exceeds theoretical. In that case, the product was probably not fully dried, contained impurities, or contained trapped water — the mass weighed was too high because it wasn't pure product.
  • No (negative or zero) → You have definitely made a mistake. Check that you have not swapped actual and theoretical.

The "yield greater than 100%" trap

Most % yield questions will give an answer below 100%. Occasionally, however, examiners ask you why a student calculated a % yield greater than 100%. This is a Higher-marks discriminator that often catches candidates out.

If a student weighs their product and gets a mass greater than the theoretical maximum, the product cannot actually exceed what the equation predicts — so something must be making the weighed mass too high. The usual reasons are:

  • The product was not fully dried and still contains water from the reaction or washing step
  • The product is not pure — it contains impurities such as unreacted starting material or by-products
  • Crystals were weighed before water of crystallisation was driven off

The fix is to dry the product more thoroughly (for example, in a low-temperature oven or desiccator) and to weigh repeatedly until the mass is constant ("dried to constant mass").

5.2.5.1 Atom Economy F+H ▼

Atom Economy

Syllabus reference: 5.13C — recall the atom economy of a reaction forming a desired product; 5.14C — calculate the atom economy of a reaction forming a desired product. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Atom economyA measure of the proportion of the total mass of reactants that ends up in the desired product of a chemical reaction, expressed as a percentage
Desired productThe product that the reaction is being carried out to make — the substance you actually want
ByproductAny other product formed alongside the desired product; usually waste, although sometimes it can be sold or reused
Atom economy formula% atom economy = (Mr of desired product / Sum of Mr of all products) × 100

Because mass is conserved in a chemical reaction, the sum of the Mr values of all products is equal to the sum of the Mr values of all reactants (taking balancing numbers into account). So the formula can also be written as:

`` % atom economy = (Mr of desired product / total Mr of all reactants) × 100 ``

Both forms give the same answer. In exam questions Edexcel uses either form, and either form can be used to answer.

What atom economy actually tells you

Atom economy answers a different question from percentage yield. Yield tells you how much of the theoretical maximum product you actually managed to isolate. Atom economy tells you, before the reaction even starts, how much of the reactant mass is theoretically capable of ending up as useful product. If the reaction produces byproducts, those byproducts contain atoms from the reactants that are not in the desired product — those atoms are wasted, even if the reaction goes perfectly.

A reaction with a high atom economy puts a large fraction of the reactant atoms into the product you want. A reaction with a low atom economy turns a large fraction of the reactant atoms into waste.

*Why atom economy matters in industry:*

  • Less waste produced — fewer raw atoms become unwanted byproducts that have to be disposed of, treated or stored
  • Lower raw-material cost per tonne of product — you are not paying for atoms that end up in the bin
  • More sustainable — less environmental impact from waste disposal and from extracting/processing the raw materials in the first place
  • Often cheaper overall — fewer separation and purification steps are needed when byproducts are minimised

Atom economy is one of the central ideas of green chemistry: choosing reaction pathways that maximise the proportion of starting material that becomes useful product.

The 100% atom-economy rule

A reaction has 100% atom economy only when there is a single product — every atom from the reactants ends up in that product. There are no byproducts because there is nothing else for the atoms to go into.

The classic 100% atom-economy reactions are addition reactions, where two molecules join together to form one larger molecule. For example, the hydration of ethene to make ethanol:

`` C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH ``

Two reactant molecules, one product molecule, nothing wasted.

Reactions that produce two or more products always have an atom economy of less than 100%, because at least one of the products is not the one you wanted. Substitution, combustion, neutralisation and decomposition reactions typically fall into this category.

Type of reactionProductsAtom economy
AdditionOne only100%

Neutralisation (acid + base)Two (salt + water)< 100%

CombustionTwo or more (CO₂ + H₂O)< 100%

This is a very useful spot-check: if the balanced equation has only one product on the right-hand side, the atom economy must be 100%. You should not need to do the full calculation to confirm it.

Worked Example 1 — Two ways of making ethanol

This is the standard textbook comparison and a very common exam scenario. Ethanol can be made by two completely different reactions, and they have very different atom economies.

Route A — Hydration of ethene (a one-product addition reaction):

`` C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH ``

  • Mr (C₂H₄) = (2 × 12) + (4 × 1) = 28
  • Mr (H₂O) = (2 × 1) + 16 = 18
  • Mr (C₂H₅OH) = (2 × 12) + (6 × 1) + 16 = 46

Atom economy = (Mr of desired product / total Mr of all products) × 100 = (46 / 46) × 100 = 100%

Or using the reactants form:

Atom economy = (46 / (28 + 18)) × 100 = (46 / 46) × 100 = 100%

Both forms agree, as expected.

Route B — Fermentation of glucose (a two-product reaction):

`` C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂ ``

  • Mr (C₆H₁₂O₆) = (6 × 12) + (12 × 1) + (6 × 16) = 72 + 12 + 96 = 180
  • Mr (C₂H₅OH) = 46 → 2 × 46 = 92 (this is the total Mr of the desired product, since 2 moles are produced per mole of glucose)
  • Mr (CO₂) = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44 → 2 × 44 = 88

Atom economy = (92 / (92 + 88)) × 100 = (92 / 180) × 100 = 51.1% (to 1 decimal place)

So roughly half the mass of glucose ends up as CO₂ rather than as ethanol. Fermentation is a much "wastier" route on paper — although in practice it has other advantages (renewable feedstock, low temperature, no high-pressure plant).

The key teaching point: two routes to the same product can have wildly different atom economies. Industry chooses between them by looking at atom economy, yield, cost of raw materials, energy required, and the rate of reaction.

Worked Example 2 — Extraction of iron from haematite

A favourite example from Section 4 of the spec, and a useful one because the byproduct (carbon monoxide) is genuinely wasted in the blast furnace.

`` Fe₂O₃ + 3 C → 2 Fe + 3 CO ``

The desired product is iron (Fe). Carbon monoxide is the byproduct.

  • Mr (Fe₂O₃) = (2 × 56) + (3 × 16) = 112 + 48 = 160
  • Mr (C) = 12 → 3 × 12 = 36
  • Mr (Fe) = 56 → 2 × 56 = 112 (this is the desired-product total)
  • Mr (CO) = 12 + 16 = 28 → 3 × 28 = 84

Atom economy = (112 / (112 + 84)) × 100 = (112 / 196) × 100 = 57.1% (to 1 decimal place)

You can sanity-check with the reactants form: (112 / (160 + 36)) × 100 = (112 / 196) × 100 = 57.1%. Same answer.

So out of every 196 g of reactant mass, only 112 g becomes the useful product. The remaining 84 g leaves the furnace as CO, which is toxic and is typically burned off rather than collected as a useful chemical. This is why processes like this — although still vital — are increasingly being scrutinised on environmental grounds.

Step-by-step procedure for any atom-economy calculation

Use this routine on every question. It maps directly onto math skills 1a, 1c, 1d, 2a and 3c.

  1. Write or check the balanced equation. If the equation is not balanced, the Mr ratios will be wrong. Balancing numbers must be multiplied through when you total the Mr of products or reactants.
  2. Identify the desired product. This is the one the question is asking about. Read the question carefully — it will name the product the company actually wants. The other product(s) are byproducts.
  3. Calculate Mr of the desired product (including any balancing number — e.g. if the equation produces 2 mol of ethanol, multiply Mr(C₂H₅OH) by 2).
  4. Calculate the total Mr of all products (or, equivalently, the total Mr of all reactants — pick whichever the question makes easier). Include balancing numbers.
  5. Apply the formula: % atom economy = (Mr desired product / total Mr) × 100.
  6. Check the decimal place / significant figures specified in the question and round correctly. Do this last — never round intermediate values, or you will lose accuracy marks.
  7. Sanity-check: is the answer between 0% and 100%? Is it 100% if and only if there is only one product? If the answer comes out greater than 100%, you have made an arithmetic error somewhere.

5.2.6.1 Choosing a Reaction Pathway H only ▼

Choosing a Reaction Pathway

Spec 5.15C — Explain why a particular reaction pathway is chosen to produce a specified product, given appropriate data such as atom economy, yield, rate, equilibrium position and usefulness of by-products. Higher tier only, Separate Chemistry only (1CH0/1H).

In industrial chemistry, the same product can often be made by more than one chemical route — fermentation versus hydration, contact-process versus alternative SO₃ syntheses, batch versus continuous reactions. The chemist's job is to pick the best pathway. "Best" never means winning on a single criterion; it means the route that best balances a set of competing factors for the specific economic, environmental and practical context.

This spec point asks you to evaluate data rather than recall facts. You will be given numbers — atom economies, percentage yields, rates, equilibrium positions, raw material costs — and expected to explain why one pathway is preferred. The key skill is reasoning across the six factors below and accepting that pathway choice is always a trade-off.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Reaction pathwayThe sequence of chemical reactions used to convert raw materials into a desired product
Atom economyThe percentage of reactant atoms that end up in the desired product, calculated from balanced equation Mr values
Percentage yieldThe actual mass of product obtained as a percentage of the theoretical maximum mass
Rate of reactionThe change in concentration of product (or reactant) per unit time — how fast the reaction goes
Equilibrium positionIn a reversible reaction, the relative amounts of reactants and products when the forward and backward rates are equal
By-productA substance formed alongside the main product; may be useful (sellable) or waste
FeedstockThe raw material fed into an industrial process

The Six Factors to Evaluate

When given data on two or more pathways, work through each of these six factors. The pathway that scores well on most — and is acceptable on the rest — is the one chosen.

*1. Atom economy*

A higher atom economy means more of the reactant atoms finish in the desired product and fewer are wasted as by-products. High atom economy is more sustainable: less raw material is used per tonne of product, and there is less waste to dispose of. A reaction with 100% atom economy has only one product, so every atom of reactant ends up where you want it.

*2. Percentage yield*

A higher yield means more product per amount of reactant actually used. Even if a reaction has perfect atom economy on paper, a low yield wastes the reactants that did not convert. Yield depends on reaction conditions, side-reactions, losses during purification and — for reversible reactions — the equilibrium position.

*3. Rate of reaction*

A faster reaction makes more product per unit time. Faster rates mean smaller reactors, lower energy costs and quicker turnover, so the cost per tonne of product is lower. A pathway that gives high yield but takes weeks (e.g. some fermentations) is less commercially attractive than one that completes in minutes, all else being equal.

*4. Equilibrium position*

For reversible reactions, the position of equilibrium sets the maximum achievable yield. If the equilibrium lies well to the right (towards products), high yields are possible. If it lies to the left, you must shift it using Le Chatelier's principle — changing temperature, pressure or concentration — which costs energy and equipment. The Haber process is the classic example: equilibrium concerns dictate moderate temperature (450 °C) and high pressure (200 atm) as a compromise between rate and yield.

*5. Cost and availability of raw materials*

Cheap, abundant, reliably-supplied feedstocks are preferred. A pathway that depends on a scarce or price-volatile raw material is risky — even a high-yield, high-atom-economy route is uneconomic if the feedstock cost swings unpredictably. Fermentation, for instance, uses sugar (glucose) whose price varies with harvests; hydration of ethene uses ethene whose supply tracks the steady output of oil/gas cracking.

*6. Usefulness of by-products*

If by-products can be sold or used elsewhere in the plant, the effective atom economy improves. A pathway that produces a "waste" gas which is actually a saleable industrial chemical — carbon dioxide for drinks and greenhouses, hydrogen for ammonia synthesis, sulfur for sulfuric acid — generates extra revenue and reduces disposal cost. By-product usefulness can rescue a pathway that looks poor on atom economy alone.

Pathway Choice as a Trade-Off

No single pathway wins on all six factors. Choosing a pathway means deciding which factors matter most for the specific product and context:

  • A high-volume commodity chemical (e.g. ammonia, ethanol, sulfuric acid) prioritises low cost per tonne — so rate, yield, atom economy and cheap feedstock all matter.
  • A high-value pharmaceutical might tolerate low yield and slow rate if the alternative is to produce dangerous by-products or use a banned reagent — here atom economy and waste minimisation can outweigh speed.
  • A process near a refinery (which produces ethene as a side-stream of cracking) can use that ethene cheaply, even if an alternative route looks attractive in isolation.

When you write an exam answer, you are essentially scoring each pathway on the relevant factors and concluding with a reason which one is preferred. The reasoning matters more than the verdict — examiners want to see you weigh the data.

Worked Example — Producing Ethanol

Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) is the most common worked example because the two routes give different scores on every factor.

*Route A — Fermentation of glucose*

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂

*Which pathway is chosen?*

Industrially, hydration of ethene is preferred wherever ethene is available. Its 100% atom economy, fast rate and continuous operation give a much lower cost per tonne of ethanol, even though the feedstock is non-renewable. Fermentation is still used where biomass is cheap and ethene is unavailable (e.g. for bioethanol fuel in countries with large sugar-cane industries), and it has the advantage of using a renewable resource.

This is the classic trade-off answer: the same product, two valid pathways, and the "right" choice depends on which factors you weight most heavily.

Worked Example — Improving the Atom Economy of Fermentation

NLM cites this exact question type: 1CH0/1F — June 2024, Q10(b)(ii) — "Explain how the atom economy of the fermentation reaction can be improved" (2 marks).

You cannot change the balanced equation (C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂), so the atom economy on paper is fixed at about 51%. But atom economy is fundamentally about waste — atoms that do not end up doing something useful. So the practical answer is:

  1. Collect the carbon dioxide by-product (don't let it vent to the atmosphere).
  2. Sell or reuse the CO₂ — for example, in fizzy drinks, in greenhouses to enhance plant growth, or for industrial chilling.

Now both products of the reaction are useful, so in a green-chemistry sense none of the atoms are wasted — the effective atom economy approaches 100%, even though the equation has not changed. This is the standard exam answer: collect and sell the CO₂.

The same logic applies to other industrial routes. The Haber process produces some unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen, which are recycled back into the reactor — same idea, applied to unreacted feedstock rather than by-product.

Worked Example — Stating Atom Economy of Ethene Hydration

NLM cites: 1CH0/1F — June 2024, Q10(b)(i) — "State why the hydration of ethene has an atom economy of 100%" (1 mark).

The standard answer: there is only one product (ethanol), so all of the atoms in the reactants (ethene and water) end up in the desired product — none are wasted. Any reaction of the form A + B → C (single product) has 100% atom economy.

Molar Volume

Spec 5.16C: Describe the molar volume of any gas at room temperature and pressure as the volume occupied by one mole of molecules of any gas at room temperature and pressure (the molar volume is provided as 24 dm³ or 24,000 cm³).

Spec 5.17C: Use the molar volume and balanced equations in calculations involving the masses of solids and volumes of gases.

Higher tier only · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Molar volumeThe volume occupied by one mole of molecules of any gas at room temperature and pressure
Room temperature and pressure (rtp)The conditions at which molar volume = 24 dm³ mol⁻¹ (approximately 20 °C and 1 atmosphere of pressure)
Decimetre cubed (dm³)A unit of volume equal to 1000 cm³, or 1 litre; the standard volume unit at GCSE for gases and solutions
Mole ratioThe whole-number ratio in which reactants combine and products form, read directly from the balancing numbers of a balanced equation

Why one mole of any gas occupies the same volume

The molar volume rule at first looks suspicious. A mole of hydrogen (H₂, Mr = 2) and a mole of carbon dioxide (CO₂, Mr = 44) clearly have very different masses, yet they occupy the same volume at the same temperature and pressure. The reason is that, in a gas, the molecules themselves are tiny compared with the empty space between them. What sets the volume of a gas is not the size of the particles but how many particles there are and how energetically they are moving. At a fixed temperature, all gas particles have the same average kinetic energy, so the same number of particles spread out into the same volume — regardless of their identity.

Avogadro's law expresses this idea formally: equal volumes of any gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. The reverse is also true — equal numbers of molecules (for example, one mole, which is 6.02 × 10²³ molecules) of any gas occupy equal volumes. At room temperature and pressure that volume is 24 dm³, equivalent to 24 000 cm³, and the Edexcel specification provides this value to you. You will not need to derive it or quote conditions to greater precision.

The three core formulae

Almost every molar volume question collapses into one of three relationships. Knowing which direction you are converting in is the most common source of error, so it is worth committing the decision rule below to memory.

Going from……toOperationFormula
Moles of gasVolume in dm³Multiply by 24V (dm³) = moles × 24
Volume in dm³Moles of gasDivide by 24moles = V (dm³) ÷ 24
Moles of gasMass in gMultiply by Mrmass (g) = moles × Mr

A useful sanity check: a single mole of gas is a fairly large volume (24 dm³, roughly the size of a small bin). If your answer for the volume produced by a few moles of gas is much smaller than the number of moles, you have probably divided by 24 instead of multiplying.

*The decision rule** — when in doubt, ask yourself: *Am I starting with moles, or ending with moles?

  • Starting with moles → multiply (you are scaling one mole up to its 24 dm³).
  • Ending with moles → divide (you are scaling a given volume back down to find how many moles it represents).

Working in cm³ versus dm³

Edexcel gives the molar volume as both 24 dm³ and 24 000 cm³, but it is far cleaner to do all gas calculations in dm³. If a question gives you a volume in cm³, convert it to dm³ first by dividing by 1000, then apply the formula. Mixing units is one of the easiest avoidable errors in this topic.

  • 480 cm³ = 480 ÷ 1000 = 0.480 dm³
  • 6.00 dm³ = 6.00 × 1000 = 6000 cm³

Worked Example 1 — Moles to volume (simple)

*What volume does 0.250 mol of oxygen, O₂, occupy at room temperature and pressure?*

You are starting with moles, so you multiply by 24.

V = moles × 24 V = 0.250 × 24 V = 6.00 dm³

Notice that the identity of the gas (O₂) was not used in the calculation. Any gas — H₂, CO₂, NH₃, SO₂ — would give the same answer because the molar volume applies to all gases equally.

Worked Example 2 — Volume to moles (reverse direction)

*How many moles of nitrogen, N₂, are present in 480 cm³ of the gas at room temperature and pressure?*

Step 1 — Convert volume to dm³.

V = 480 ÷ 1000 = 0.480 dm³

Step 2 — You are ending with moles, so divide by 24.

moles = V ÷ 24 moles = 0.480 ÷ 24 moles = 0.0200 mol

A common trap on this style of question is to forget the unit conversion and divide 480 by 24 to get 20 mol — which would be a vast amount of gas. The sanity check (20 mol of any gas would fill 480 dm³, not 480 cm³) should immediately flag the error.

Worked Example 3 — Multistep with mass, balanced equation, and percentage composition

This problem is modelled on the style of question that has appeared at the very end of recent Higher tier Paper 1 papers. It combines an unbalanced equation, a mass-to-mole conversion, a mole-ratio step, a moles-to-volume conversion, and a final percentage-composition adjustment.

*Nitrogen monoxide, NO, reacts with oxygen in the air to form nitrogen dioxide, NO₂. Air contains approximately 20% oxygen by volume. Calculate the minimum volume of air, in dm³ at rtp, needed to react completely with 1000 g of NO. (Ar of N = 14, Ar of O = 16.)*

Step 1 — Write a balanced equation.

2 NO + O₂ → 2 NO₂

Step 2 — Calculate the Mr of NO and convert mass to moles.

Mr (NO) = 14 + 16 = 30 moles NO = mass ÷ Mr = 1000 ÷ 30 = 33.3 mol

Step 3 — Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation to find moles of O₂.

From the equation, 2 mol NO reacts with 1 mol O₂, so the ratio NO : O₂ = 2 : 1.

moles O₂ = 33.3 ÷ 2 = 16.7 mol

Step 4 — Convert moles of O₂ to volume of O₂.

You are starting with moles, so multiply by 24.

V (O₂) = 16.7 × 24 = 400 dm³

Step 5 — Scale up from oxygen to air.

Air is only about 20% O₂ by volume, so to obtain 400 dm³ of O₂ you need a larger total volume of air:

V (air) = 400 × (100 ÷ 20) = 2000 dm³

This style of question is one of the most demanding on the Higher tier paper because so many steps must be sequenced correctly — and a slip at any step costs the final mark. Examiners typically award error-carried-forward marks for later steps, so even if your moles of NO are wrong, you can still pick up marks for the ratio, the multiplication by 24, and the 100/20 scale-up if your method is shown clearly.

Both are direction-of-conversion errors. Build the habit, every time, of stating to yourself: "I am going from moles, so I multiply by 24" — or — "I am going to moles, so I divide by 24."

The same report also signals that students who showed clear, labelled working were able to claim error-carried-forward marks for the later steps even when the first step was wrong. Always show every step of a molar volume calculation, with units written next to each number — it is the single biggest predictor of partial credit on these multi-step questions.

Mathematical Skills

Edexcel maps the following maths skills to this spec point: 1a, 1c, 2a, 3b, 3c — recognising and using expressions in decimal form, using ratios, calculating volumes and masses, substituting numerical values into algebraic equations, and solving algebraic equations.

The core algebraic relationship behind every molar volume question is:

`` V (dm³) = n × Vm ``

where n is moles of gas and Vm = 24 dm³ mol⁻¹ is the molar volume at rtp.

Procedure for a typical molar volume question:

  1. Read the question and identify which quantities are given (mass, volume, moles) and which is the target.
  2. If the given quantity is a mass, convert it to moles using moles = mass ÷ Mr.
  3. If the given quantity is a volume of gas in cm³, divide by 1000 to convert to dm³.
  4. Use the balanced equation to find the mole ratio between the given species and the target species.
  5. Convert moles of the target species to volume (×24) or mass (×Mr) as required by the question.
  6. Apply any final scaling (e.g. percentage composition of air, percentage yield) at the end.
  7. State the answer with correct units and to an appropriate number of significant figures.

How Molar Volume Appears in Exams

Molar volume questions appear almost exclusively on Paper 1 Higher (1CH0/1H), in the closing questions of the paper (typically Q9 or Q10). They are high-tariff, multi-step calculations testing whether you can integrate several quantitative skills — balanced equations, mole ratios, molar mass, and molar volume — within a single problem. The command word is essentially always "Calculate", and tariffs run from 2 marks for a short conversion up to 4 marks for a fully multi-step problem.

  • 1CH0/1H — 2019 June, Q10(b) (4 marks): Calculate the minimum volume of air at rtp required to react with 1000 g of NO, given that 1 mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³. Multistep: balanced equation → moles NO from mass → mole ratio NO:O₂ → volume of O₂ → scale from O₂ to air using 100/20.
  • 1CH0/1H — 2024 June, Q10(b) (2 marks): "Calculate the maximum volume of products, in dm³, that could be formed by the complete reaction of 650 dm³ of methane." This question relies on the fact that, at the same temperature and pressure, volume ratios of gases equal mole ratios — so you can apply the balanced equation directly to volumes without ever converting to moles.

The 2024 question highlights a particularly elegant short-cut: when all reactants and all products are gases at the same conditions, you can multiply the given volume by the mole ratio (product : reactant) straight away. For example, for the combustion of methane:

CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O

If H₂O is also gaseous at rtp in the question's stated conditions, then 1 mol of CH₄ produces 1 + 2 = 3 mol of gaseous products, and 650 dm³ of methane would produce 650 × 3 = 1950 dm³ of gaseous products. Always check the wording of the question to confirm whether water is gas or liquid at the stated conditions.

Key Takeaways
  1. Molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of molecules of any gas at room temperature and pressure: 24 dm³ or 24 000 cm³ (provided in the question — you do not need to memorise the conditions).
  2. The rule is the same for every gas — H₂, O₂, CO₂, NH₃ — because Avogadro's law says equal moles of any gas occupy equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure.
  3. Multiply by 24 when going from moles to volume; divide by 24 when going from volume to moles. The single most common exam error is doing this the wrong way round.
  4. Convert any volume given in cm³ to dm³ (÷ 1000) before applying the molar volume formula.
  5. The core formulae: V (dm³) = moles × 24 · moles = V (dm³) ÷ 24 · mass (g) = moles × Mr.
  6. For a multi-step problem: balance the equation → moles of given species → mole ratio → moles of target species → convert to required units.
  7. When all species are gases at the same conditions, volume ratios equal mole ratios — you can apply the balanced equation directly to volumes (as in 1CH0/1H 2024 June Q10(b)).
  8. For reactions involving air, remember that air is approximately 20% O₂ by volume, so multiply the volume of O₂ by 100/20 (= 5) to obtain the volume of air needed.

  11. Sanity-check your answer: one mole of gas is 24 dm³, so a few moles should produce tens of dm³, not fractions of a dm³.

5.2.8.1 Avogadro's Law H only ▼

Avogadro's Law

Spec 5.18C: Use Avogadro's law to calculate volumes of gases involved in a gaseous reaction, given the relevant equation. **Higher tier only · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only**.

Avogadro's law is the rule that lets you turn gas volumes straight into stoichiometric ratios — no balancing tricks, no molar-volume conversion, no rearranging PV = nRT (which isn't on the GCSE spec anyway). It's the cleanest tool in the quantitative chemistry kit for purely gaseous reactions, but it has a tight set of conditions, and Edexcel sets it at the very end of Higher tier papers where time pressure causes candidates to slip on the simple "ratio in, ratio out" logic.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Avogadro's lawEqual volumes of any gas, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules (and therefore equal numbers of moles)
Mole ratioThe ratio of moles of substances in a balanced chemical equation, taken directly from the stoichiometric coefficients
Volume ratio (gases)For gases at the same T and p, the ratio of their volumes is identical to their mole ratio
rtpRoom temperature and pressure (20 °C, 1 atm); the standard "same T and p" conditions assumed in GCSE questions unless stated otherwise
Gaseous reactionA reaction in which every species relevant to the volume calculation is in the gas state (g)

What Avogadro's law actually says

The full statement is: equal volumes of any gas, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. Because one mole always contains the same number of particles (the Avogadro constant, 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹), "equal numbers of molecules" is the same as "equal numbers of moles." So 100 cm³ of hydrogen at 20 °C and 1 atm contains the same number of moles as 100 cm³ of oxygen, carbon dioxide, methane, or any other gas at the same conditions.

That sounds counter-intuitive at first — surely a heavy molecule like CO₂ should take up more room than a light one like H₂? At GCSE level, the answer is that gases are mostly empty space. The volume a gas occupies is determined almost entirely by how the molecules move (their kinetic energy, set by temperature) and how hard they push on the container walls (the pressure), not by how big the molecules are. So at the same T and p, the number of molecules per unit volume is the same for every gas.

Why this matters for calculations: the mole ratio from a balanced equation is also the volume ratio for any species in the gas state. You can skip the usual step of "moles → volume" via the molar volume entirely.

The volume-ratio shortcut

For a purely gaseous reaction, the workflow is:

  1. Write the balanced equation.
  2. Read the coefficients of the gas species.
  3. Use those coefficients as the volume ratio directly.
  4. Scale the known volume up or down to get the unknown volume(s).

That's it. No multiplication by 24, no conversion to grams, no Mr.

This is a meaningful simplification compared with 5.2.7.1 Molar Volume, where you must convert between volume and moles using the factor 24 dm³ mol⁻¹ (at rtp). Avogadro's law calculations skip that step because both the reactant and product are gases — the molar-volume conversion would apply to both sides, so it cancels out.

A quick sanity check before you start: are all the substances relevant to the calculation gases at the conditions stated? If water is a liquid product, or if a solid is forming, only the gaseous species obey the volume-ratio rule. Mixing in a liquid or solid is the most common way candidates get this wrong.

The rule of thumb: if you're given a gas volume and asked for another gas volume in the same reaction at the same conditions, use Avogadro's law (ratios only). If you're given mass/moles and need volume, or volume and need mass/moles, use molar volume.

Worked Example 1 — Combustion of hydrogen

Balanced equation:

2 H₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2 H₂O (g)

(All species are gases — assume the water is produced as steam, as it would be in a hot combustion environment.)

Question: 10 dm³ of hydrogen reacts completely with oxygen. Calculate (a) the volume of oxygen needed and (b) the volume of water vapour produced. All measurements are made at the same T and p.

Step 1 — Read the coefficients. H₂ : O₂ : H₂O = 2 : 1 : 2.

Step 2 — Anchor on the species you know. You have 10 dm³ of H₂, and H₂ has coefficient 2.

*Step 3 — Scale.*

  • Oxygen: ratio 2 (H₂) : 1 (O₂), so volume of O₂ = 10 × (1/2) = 5 dm³.
  • Water vapour: ratio 2 (H₂) : 2 (H₂O), so volume of H₂O = 10 × (2/2) = 10 dm³.

Step 4 — Sanity check. Did the volume decrease, stay the same, or increase? Total reactant gas volume = 10 + 5 = 15 dm³. Total product gas volume = 10 dm³. The total volume of gas has decreased — which makes sense because the equation goes from 3 moles of gas on the left (2 + 1) to 2 moles on the right. Volume changes mirror mole changes for gas-phase reactions.

Worked Example 2 — Complete combustion of methane (1CH0/1H June 2024 Q10(b))

Balanced equation:

CH₄ (g) + 2 O₂ (g) → CO₂ (g) + 2 H₂O (g)

Question (paraphrasing the 2024 paper): Calculate the maximum volume of products, in dm³, that could be formed by the complete reaction of 650 dm³ of methane.

Step 1 — Coefficients of gas products. CO₂ has coefficient 1, H₂O has coefficient 2. CH₄ has coefficient 1.

Step 2 — CO₂ produced. CH₄ : CO₂ = 1 : 1, so volume of CO₂ = 650 × (1/1) = 650 dm³.

Step 3 — H₂O produced. CH₄ : H₂O = 1 : 2, so volume of H₂O = 650 × (2/1) = 1300 dm³.

Step 4 — Total volume of products. 650 + 1300 = 1950 dm³.

Why the answer is so big: the question scales up familiar mole-ratio reasoning to an industrial-sized volume of methane. Don't be put off by the magnitude — the ratio logic is identical to the same question with 6.5 dm³ of methane (which would give 19.5 dm³ of products). If your method is correct, scale takes care of itself.

Common pitfall here: candidates sometimes ignore the water and only report the CO₂ volume, or forget to add the two product volumes together. The command "maximum volume of products" is plural — you need every gaseous product.

Worked Example 3 — The Haber process

Balanced equation:

N₂ (g) + 3 H₂ (g) ⇌ 2 NH₃ (g)

Question: Assuming the reaction goes to completion at constant T and p, what volume of ammonia is produced from 100 dm³ of nitrogen?

Step 1 — Mole/volume ratio. N₂ : NH₃ = 1 : 2.

Step 2 — Scale. Volume of NH₃ = 100 × (2/1) = 200 dm³.

Step 3 — Volume of hydrogen needed. N₂ : H₂ = 1 : 3, so volume of H₂ = 100 × (3/1) = 300 dm³.

Sanity check: total reactant gas volume = 100 + 300 = 400 dm³. Product = 200 dm³. The gas volume halves overall — which is why high pressure pushes the Haber equilibrium towards ammonia (fewer moles of gas on the right). For more on this, see 4.2.2.1 The Haber Process.

In practice the Haber reaction is reversible and never goes to completion in one pass, but the volume-ratio logic still applies to the gas actually converted on each pass.

Why the volume ratio = the mole ratio (the proof in one line)

If equal volumes of gas at the same T and p contain equal moles, then for any two gases A and B in the same reaction:

`` V_A / V_B = n_A / n_B ``

And from the balanced equation:

`` n_A / n_B = coefficient_A / coefficient_B ``

So:

`` V_A / V_B = coefficient_A / coefficient_B ``

That's the only equation you need. There is no rearrangement, no R, no T, no p — they cancel because the question always specifies "at the same T and p."

Mathematical Skills

Math skills assessed here: 1a (recognise and use expressions in decimal form), 1c (use ratios, fractions and percentages), 1d (make estimates of the results of simple calculations).

The arithmetic is deliberately light — Edexcel uses these questions to test whether you can read a balanced equation, identify which species are gases, and apply a ratio. The numbers are usually whole-number multiples (650 → 1300, 100 → 200, 10 → 5) so a student who has the method should be able to do the working in their head or on a single line.

*The general procedure:*

  1. Check that the equation is balanced. If it isn't, balance it first (see 1.6.9.1 Writing & Balancing Equations).
  2. Identify which species are gases (state symbol (g)).
  3. Read the coefficients of the gaseous species you care about.
  4. Set up the ratio: V_unknown = V_known × (coefficient_unknown / coefficient_known).
  5. Apply the ratio. Round to an appropriate number of significant figures (usually 2 or 3 for GCSE).
  6. If the question asks for total product volume, add the gas-product volumes together.

5.3.1.1 Predicting the Conditions H only ▼

Predicting the Conditions

Spec 5.19C: Describe the Haber process as a reversible reaction between nitrogen and hydrogen to form ammonia. Spec 5.20C: Predict how the rate of attainment of equilibrium is affected by (a) changes in temperature, (b) changes in pressure, (c) changes in concentration, (d) use of a catalyst.* **Higher tier · Separate Chemistry only.*

This spec point asks a very specific question: how fast does the system reach equilibrium when you change a condition? That is not the same as asking which side the equilibrium settles on. The distinction is what trips up most candidates, so before anything else you need to lock it down.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Dynamic equilibriumThe state in a closed system where the forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates, so the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant
Rate of attainment of equilibriumHow quickly the forward and reverse rates become equal — i.e. how long it takes the system to reach equilibrium from the starting mixture
Position of equilibriumA description of the relative amounts of reactants and products once equilibrium has been established; "lies to the right" means more products, "lies to the left" means more reactants
Yield (equilibrium yield)The amount of product present once equilibrium has been reached, often expressed as a percentage of the theoretical maximum
Reversible reactionA reaction that can proceed in both forward and reverse directions, shown with the ⇌ symbol
CatalystA substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up; in a reversible reaction it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally

Spec 5.19C — Haber process as a reversible reaction

The Haber process is the industrial synthesis of ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen:

N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) ΔH negative (forward reaction is exothermic)

The ⇌ symbol is essential — it tells you the reaction does not go to completion. Nitrogen and hydrogen react to form ammonia, but ammonia also decomposes back into nitrogen and hydrogen. In a sealed reactor at a steady temperature and pressure, the two opposing reactions eventually reach equal rates, so concentrations stop changing. That is the dynamic equilibrium.

Three features of the Haber reaction matter for predictions:

  1. It is exothermic in the forward direction. Heating the mixture favours the reverse (endothermic) direction — so high temperature reduces yield but increases rate.
  2. There are 4 moles of gas on the left, 2 moles on the right. High pressure favours the side with fewer gas moles — so high pressure increases yield, and (separately) increases rate.
  3. All species are gases. Both pressure and concentration changes apply.

These three features mean the Haber process is the standard worked example for spec 5.20C, because every variable (temperature, pressure, concentration, catalyst) does something visible.

Spec 5.20C — Rate of attainment vs position of equilibrium

This is the heart of the spec point. For each change in conditions you must be ready to give two separate answers:

  • What it does to the rate at which equilibrium is reached
  • What it does to the position (yield) of equilibrium

The two answers are independent — temperature can speed equilibrium up and reduce yield at the same time, and a catalyst can speed equilibrium up while leaving yield unchanged. Confusing the two ideas — or trying to use one to explain the other — is exactly the candidate error flagged in the examiner reports below.

*Rate vs Position Summary Table*

ChangeEffect on rate of attainmentEffect on position (yield)

*Why these rules work — collision theory in one place*

Rate effects all come from one underlying idea: a reaction goes faster when more reactant particles collide successfully per second.

  • Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy. Two consequences: collisions happen more frequently, and a larger fraction of those collisions clear the activation-energy barrier. So rate rises sharply with temperature.
  • Higher pressure (for gases) squashes the same number of particles into a smaller volume, so there are more particles per unit volume. Collision frequency rises and rate increases.
  • Higher concentration of a reactant in solution does the same thing — more reactant particles per unit volume, more collisions per second.
  • A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, so a greater fraction of collisions are successful at any temperature. Crucially, the catalyst lowers the activation energy for the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount, which is why equilibrium is reached sooner without the position moving.

*The critical distinction that examiners care about*

Temperature is the only variable in this spec point that changes the average kinetic energy of the particles. Pressure and concentration do not "give particles more energy." They increase the frequency of collisions because there are more particles per unit volume, but the particles themselves carry the same average kinetic energy as before.

This is exactly the slip 1CH0/1H — June 2019 candidates made (see Examiner Report Insights below). If you ever find yourself writing "increasing pressure gives particles more energy," stop and rewrite — pressure increases the number of collisions per second, not the energy of each collision.

How position effects work (briefly — for the full treatment see 4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium)

The position-of-equilibrium predictions on the right-hand column of the table above follow from Le Chatelier-style reasoning: when a closed equilibrium system is disturbed, the equilibrium shifts to oppose the change.

  • Heat a system → it shifts in the direction that absorbs heat (the endothermic direction).
  • Increase pressure → it shifts to the side with fewer gas molecules, reducing the total pressure.
  • Add a reactant → it shifts away from the added species, i.e. to the right.
  • Add a catalyst → no disturbance to either side, so position is unchanged.

You must be able to state these effects for the Haber reaction at GCSE Higher level. The full mechanistic explanation belongs in the position-of-equilibrium note linked above; here, the point is to pair each position-effect with the corresponding rate-effect when you answer extended-response questions.

Worked example — Haber process at 450 °C and 200 atm

The Haber process is run industrially at roughly 450 °C and 200 atm with an iron catalyst. Predict, with reasoning, the effect of each chosen condition on (a) the rate of attainment of equilibrium and (b) the equilibrium yield of ammonia.

N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) forward reaction exothermic

*(1) High temperature (450 °C)*

  • Rate of attainment: increased. Particles have more kinetic energy, so collisions are more frequent and a larger fraction exceed the activation energy. Equilibrium is reached faster.
  • Yield: decreased. The forward reaction is exothermic, so heating shifts the equilibrium to the left (the endothermic, ammonia-decomposing direction). Yield of NH₃ falls.

Comment: 450 °C is a compromise — much hotter and the yield collapses; much cooler and the reaction is too slow to be commercially viable, even with a catalyst. See 5.3.1.2 Choosing the Conditions for the full cost-vs-yield trade-off.

*(2) High pressure (200 atm)*

  • Rate of attainment: increased. Gas molecules are forced into a smaller volume, raising collision frequency. Equilibrium is reached faster.
  • Yield: increased. There are 4 moles of gas on the left and 2 on the right. Raising pressure shifts the equilibrium to the side with fewer gas moles — the right-hand side. Yield of NH₃ rises.

Comment: 200 atm is also a compromise. Even higher pressures would push yield higher, but the engineering cost (thick-walled reactors, energy to compress) becomes prohibitive.

*(3) Iron catalyst*

  • Rate of attainment: increased. The catalyst provides a lower-activation-energy pathway, speeding up the forward and reverse reactions equally.
  • Yield: no change. A catalyst does not shift the position of equilibrium. The same amount of ammonia is present at equilibrium — it just arrives sooner.

This pairing — rate increased, yield unchanged — is the cleanest "test" of whether a candidate understands the rate/position distinction. If a student writes "the catalyst increases the yield of ammonia," they have misunderstood the spec point.

  • 1CH0/1H — June 2024, Q10(a)(ii): "Explain what effect there would be on the rate... and the equilibrium yield" for a temperature/pressure change (6 marks).

Across both sittings the question structure is the same: one prompt asking for both rate-of-attainment and equilibrium-yield effects, each with reasoning. The mark scheme awards roughly half the marks for rate and half for yield, so a one-sided answer caps near 50%.

Strategy for the 6-mark extended-response question

If you are asked a 6-mark "Explain the effect of [condition] on rate and yield" question on Paper 1H, work through this skeleton:

  1. Name the condition the question gives you (e.g. "increasing the temperature").
  2. State the rate effect ("the rate of attainment of equilibrium increases").
  3. Give the rate reason (more kinetic energy → more frequent successful collisions / more particles per unit volume → more frequent collisions / catalyst lowers activation energy).
  4. State the yield effect ("the equilibrium yield of ammonia decreases" / "the position shifts to the left").
  5. Give the yield reason linked to the equation — which direction is endothermic, which side has more moles of gas, what the catalyst does to position (nothing).
  6. Repeat for the second condition if the question names two.

That structure picks up all six marks consistently. Skipping step 4 or 5 is the most common reason candidates score 3/6 instead of 6/6.

Key Takeaways
  1. The Haber process is a reversible reaction: N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g); the forward direction is exothermic.
  2. Rate of attainment of equilibrium is how quickly the forward and reverse rates equalise — it is not the same as the position of equilibrium.
  3. Higher temperature speeds attainment (more kinetic energy → more successful collisions) but reduces the yield of an exothermic-forward reaction such as Haber (equilibrium shifts to the endothermic side).
  4. Higher pressure (for gas equilibria) speeds attainment (more molecules per unit volume → more collisions) and shifts position to the side with fewer gas moles — for Haber, more ammonia.
  5. Higher concentration of a reactant speeds attainment and shifts position to remove the added species (i.e. towards products).
  6. A catalyst speeds attainment in both directions equally and has no effect on the position of equilibrium or the yield.
  7. Energy of particles depends on temperature only. Pressure and concentration increase collision frequency, not particle energy — writing the opposite is the single most-flagged error on this spec point (1CH0/1H, June 2019).
  8. A 6-mark "Explain" question always wants both the rate-of-attainment effect and the yield effect, each with a reason — answers that cover only one half cap at around half marks (1CH0/1H, June 2019 + November 2020).
  9. Industrial Haber conditions (≈ 450 °C, ≈ 200 atm, iron catalyst) are a compromise between rate and yield — see 5.3.1.2 Choosing the Conditions for the cost-vs-yield trade-off.
  10. The mark-scheme template for full-marks 6-mark answers: state rate effect → reason → state yield effect → reason, for each condition the question names.
5.3.1.2 Choosing the Conditions H only ▼

Choosing the Conditions

Spec 5.21C: Explain how, in industrial reactions, including the Haber process, conditions used are related to: (a) the availability and cost of raw materials and energy supplies, (b) the control of temperature, pressure and catalyst used to produce an acceptable yield in an acceptable time. Higher tier · Separate Chemistry only · Paper 1.

This spec point is the evaluation half of equilibrium industrial chemistry. The companion SP (5.3.1.1 Predicting the Conditions) asks what each condition does to a reversible reaction. This SP asks WHY industry has settled on the conditions it actually uses — which are almost never the conditions that maximise yield on paper. The single most important idea is compromise: the best yield is not the best industrial choice.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
YieldThe amount of product formed at equilibrium, usually expressed as a percentage of the theoretical maximum
Rate of attainment of equilibriumHow quickly the reaction reaches its equilibrium position — separate from the position itself
Compromise conditionsConditions chosen to give an acceptable yield in an acceptable time at an acceptable cost, rather than maximising any single factor
CatalystA substance that increases the rate of reaction without being used up or shifting the position of equilibrium
Acceptable yieldA yield high enough to make the process economically worthwhile, but not necessarily the maximum theoretically possible
Acceptable rateA rate fast enough to produce product on an industrial timescale, even if a slower rate would give a higher yield

Why "best yield" is not the same as "best industrial conditions"

For a reversible exothermic reaction, Le Chatelier's principle says a low temperature pushes the equilibrium towards products and so increases the yield. But at a low temperature the reaction runs slowly — both the forward and the reverse rates fall. A factory running at the "perfect yield" temperature might take weeks to reach equilibrium for a single batch, which wipes out the economic benefit of the higher yield.

The opposite is also true. A very high temperature speeds the reaction up, but for an exothermic reaction it shifts the equilibrium backwards towards reactants, so the yield collapses. The factory now reaches equilibrium quickly — but the equilibrium it reaches contains very little product.

A real industrial process therefore has to balance three things at once:

  • Yield — how much product comes out of each batch
  • Rate — how quickly that yield is reached
  • Cost — the price of fuel, the price of high-pressure equipment, the price of catalysts, the price of safety measures

The choice of temperature, pressure and catalyst is whatever trade-off between these three gives the lowest cost per tonne of product — even if any individual factor is not at its maximum.

The Haber process — the worked example

The Haber process makes ammonia (NH₃) from nitrogen and hydrogen:

N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) ΔH = negative (exothermic forward)

Industrial conditions: 450 °C, 200 atm, iron catalyst. None of these is what pure Le Chatelier reasoning would pick. Each one is a compromise.

Temperature: 450 °C

The forward reaction is exothermic. A low temperature would therefore shift the equilibrium towards more ammonia and give a higher yield. So why doesn't the Haber process run at, say, 100 °C?

  • At low temperatures, both forward and reverse rates are very slow. Reaching equilibrium would take far too long for a profitable industrial process.
  • At high temperatures, the rate is fast — but the equilibrium shifts backwards (reverse is endothermic), so the yield of ammonia falls sharply.
  • 450 °C is a middle path. The rate is fast enough that equilibrium is reached in minutes, and the yield is still high enough (around 15% per pass) to be economic. Unreacted N₂ and H₂ are recycled, so a modest single-pass yield is still worthwhile.

Pressure: 200 atm

Look at the moles of gas on each side: 4 moles of gas on the left (1 N₂ + 3 H₂) react to give 2 moles of gas on the right (2 NH₃). Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium to the side with fewer moles of gas, so high pressure favours ammonia.

In principle, the higher the pressure, the higher the yield. Why stop at 200 atm?

  • Very high pressures need extremely strong pipework and reaction vessels, which are expensive to build and maintain.
  • Compressing gas to very high pressures takes enormous amounts of energy — the cost of fuel to drive the compressors eventually outweighs the extra yield.
  • Safety risk increases sharply with pressure, so insurance and engineering costs rise.
  • 200 atm is the point where the marginal benefit of a higher yield no longer pays for the marginal cost of stronger equipment and more compressor power.

Iron catalyst

The iron catalyst speeds up the rate at which equilibrium is reached. It does not shift the position of equilibrium — the final yield is unchanged. So why bother?

  • A catalyst lets the process reach equilibrium at a lower temperature than it would need otherwise. That saves fuel and protects the yield (low T favours yield for an exothermic forward reaction).
  • Iron is cheap and abundant, which matters at industrial scale where tens of tonnes of catalyst may be used.
  • Without the catalyst, the rate at 450 °C would be far too slow, and pushing the temperature up to compensate would destroy the yield.

Raw materials and energy supply (part a of the spec)

The spec point also asks about availability and cost of raw materials and energy supplies. For the Haber process:

  • Nitrogen is taken from the air by fractional distillation — effectively unlimited and cheap.
  • Hydrogen is produced mainly from natural gas (methane) by steam reforming. The cost of natural gas therefore strongly influences the price of ammonia.
  • Energy is needed both to compress the gases to 200 atm and to maintain 450 °C. Most Haber plants are co-located with a natural gas supply so that one feedstock provides both raw material and fuel.

Industrial location, feedstock cost and energy cost are part of the compromise: even if 300 atm gave a slightly better yield, no operator would choose it if it doubled the gas bill.

Other industrial equilibria — applying the same reasoning

The Haber process is the spec-named example, but the same compromise logic applies to other reversible industrial reactions. Conditions look different in each case because the stoichiometry and the direction of ΔH are different.

Structuring a 6-mark "Explain the conditions" answer

Edexcel Higher Paper 1 routinely tests 5.21C as a 6-mark extended-response question. The marks split fairly evenly between rate points and yield points, with synthesis marks for explaining the trade-off. Use this template:

  1. State the chemistry — is the forward reaction exo- or endothermic? How many moles of gas on each side?
  2. Predict the effect on rate — does the changed condition speed the reaction up or slow it down? Use collision theory ideas if helpful.
  3. Predict the effect on yield / position of equilibrium — apply Le Chatelier's principle to the changed condition.
  4. Bring rate and yield together — note whether they point in the same direction or in opposite directions.
  5. State the industrial compromise — what does industry actually choose, and why? Mention cost if relevant (high pressure equipment, fuel for high temperatures, catalyst price).

Worked example — explain why the Haber process runs at 450 °C rather than 200 °C:

  • The forward reaction is exothermic, so a lower temperature shifts the equilibrium towards ammonia and increases the yield.
  • However, a lower temperature slows the rate of both forward and reverse reactions, so equilibrium is reached more slowly.
  • At 200 °C the yield would be higher, but the reaction would be uneconomically slow.
  • At a much higher temperature, the rate would be fast, but the yield would collapse because the equilibrium shifts backwards.
  • 450 °C is a compromise — it gives an acceptable yield in an acceptable time.

That is six clear mark-worthy points, two each on rate, yield and synthesis.

Common student errors to avoid

  • Talking only about rate, never about yield (or vice versa). Examiners flag this every sitting — answers must address both.
  • Assuming higher pressure is always better. It's only better when there are fewer moles of gas on the product side, and even then cost limits how high you can go.
  • Forgetting that a catalyst doesn't change the yield. It only changes the rate at which equilibrium is reached.
  • Copying Haber reasoning onto a different equation. If the forward reaction is endothermic, the temperature argument flips. If the moles of gas are equal on both sides, pressure has no effect on yield at all.
  • Treating "compromise" as a buzzword. You only get the mark if you explicitly link it to acceptable yield AND acceptable time (and ideally cost).

5.3.2.1 Fertilisers F+H ▼

Fertilisers

Spec points 5.22C and 5.23C — fertilisers may contain nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium compounds to promote plant growth; ammonia reacts with nitric acid to produce ammonium nitrate, a salt used as a fertiliser. Examined on Foundation and Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
FertiliserA substance added to soil to supply nutrients that plants need for healthy growth
NPK fertiliserA fertiliser that contains compounds providing the three main plant nutrients — nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K)
Ammonium saltA salt containing the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺, formed when ammonia reacts with an acid
NitrateA salt or compound containing the NO₃⁻ ion; nitrates are a key source of nitrogen for plants
SolubleAble to dissolve in water; fertiliser salts must be soluble so plant roots can absorb the dissolved nutrients

Why plants need nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium

Plants need a range of chemical elements to grow. Three of these — nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium — are needed in much larger quantities than the others, which is why they are the focus of fertiliser chemistry. If the soil does not contain enough of any one of them, plant growth slows down or stops, and crop yields fall.

Nitrogen (N) is essential for making proteins, which plants need to build new cells and to grow leaves and stems. Plants cannot use nitrogen gas (N₂) directly from the air because the triple bond in N₂ is too strong for them to break. Instead, plants absorb nitrogen from the soil as soluble ions — either as nitrate ions (NO₃⁻) or as ammonium ions (NH₄⁺). A plant short of nitrogen has yellowing leaves and stunted growth.

Phosphorus (P) is essential for root growth, for transferring energy inside cells (it is a key part of ATP, the molecule that stores chemical energy) and for forming DNA. Plants take in phosphorus as soluble phosphate compounds. A plant short of phosphorus has poor root development and discoloured leaves.

Potassium (K) is essential for enzyme activation, for regulating water movement in and out of cells, and for healthy growth of flowers and fruit. Plants absorb potassium as the soluble K⁺ ion. A plant short of potassium has poor fruiting and weak stems.

A balanced fertiliser supplies all three of these nutrients together, which is why the bag is often labelled with an NPK number — for example, an NPK fertiliser labelled 10-5-10 contains 10% nitrogen compounds, 5% phosphorus compounds and 10% potassium compounds.

Why fertiliser compounds must be soluble

The most important physical property of any compound used in a fertiliser is that it must be soluble in water. There are two reasons for this:

  1. Plants absorb nutrients only as dissolved ions. Roots take up water from the soil and the nutrients have to be already dissolved in that water so they can move into the root cells.
  2. Rain dissolves the fertiliser into the soil moisture. If the compound were insoluble, it would just sit on the surface as a solid and the plant would not be able to take it up.

This is the reason ammonium salts and nitrates are used so heavily as fertilisers: they are all very soluble in water, they release the NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻ ion straight into the soil solution, and the plant can absorb the nitrogen almost immediately.

This is exactly the point that the November 2020 Higher Paper examiner flagged (see Examiner Report Insights below) — many candidates were asked to suggest a reason for using ammonium salts as fertilisers and just repeated the word "fertiliser". The mark was for explaining why those particular salts work: soluble in water, so the plant roots can absorb the nitrogen and use it for growth.

The reaction of ammonia with nitric acid

Ammonia (NH₃) is a weak base, and nitric acid (HNO₃) is a strong acid. When they are mixed together they react in a typical acid-base neutralisation reaction, producing a salt and (in this case) no water — because both the ammonia and the acid contribute directly to the salt:

NH₃ (aq) + HNO₃ (aq) → NH₄NO₃ (aq)

The salt produced is ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃. The ammonia molecule accepts a proton (H⁺) from the nitric acid to form the ammonium ion (NH₄⁺), and the rest of the nitric acid molecule becomes the nitrate ion (NO₃⁻). The two oppositely charged ions sit together in the solution as dissolved ammonium nitrate, which can be crystallised out by evaporating the water.

Writing the formula of ammonia correctly

Recalling the formula of ammonia is a small but critical point on this spec line. Ammonia is NH₃ — one nitrogen atom bonded to three hydrogen atoms.

Foundation candidates regularly lose the mark by writing NH₂ or NH₄ instead (see Examiner Report Insights). The way to keep them straight:

  • NH₃ — neutral ammonia molecule, the gas / weak base
  • NH₄⁺ — ammonium ion, the positive ion formed when NH₃ gains a proton (only exists as an ion, with a positive charge)
  • NH₂ does not exist as a normal stable species at this level — if you write NH₂ for ammonia it is simply wrong

If you are asked to write the equation for ammonia + nitric acid, the equation will not balance unless you start with NH₃.

Why the equation does not need balancing numbers

The 1:1 ratio is convenient because every ammonia molecule has one lone pair on the nitrogen and can accept exactly one H⁺ from the nitric acid. Every nitric acid molecule has exactly one acidic H⁺ to donate. So one molecule of NH₃ + one molecule of HNO₃ gives one formula unit of NH₄NO₃ — no big balancing numbers needed.

Compare this with the equation for ammonium sulfate (next SP, 5.3.2.2):

2 NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄

Sulfuric acid has two acidic H⁺ ions, so two ammonia molecules are needed to neutralise it.

And for ammonium phosphate, which provides both N and P:

3 NH₃ + H₃PO₄ → (NH₄)₃PO₄

Phosphoric acid has three acidic H⁺ ions, so three ammonia molecules are needed.

Worked example — writing the word and symbol equations

Question: Ammonia reacts with nitric acid to produce a salt used as a fertiliser. Write (i) the word equation and (ii) the balanced symbol equation for the reaction.

*Answer:*

(i) Word equation:

ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate

(ii) Symbol equation:

NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃

The equation is already balanced as written — one N, four H and three O on each side — so no balancing numbers are needed. State symbols (aq for the dissolved species) can be added if asked for.

5.3.2.2 Making Fertilisers F+H ▼

Making Fertilisers

Spec point 5.24C — Describe and compare the **laboratory preparation** of ammonium sulfate from ammonia solution and dilute sulfuric acid on a small scale, and the **industrial production** of ammonium sulfate used as a fertiliser on a much larger scale. Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1, Foundation + Higher.

Ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄, is one of the most widely used nitrogen fertilisers in the world. It is made by neutralising sulfuric acid with ammonia. The chemistry of the reaction is identical whether you make a tiny batch in a school lab or thousands of tonnes a day in an industrial plant — but the practical methods, raw materials, scale, and cost differ dramatically. Spec point 5.24C asks you to describe both methods and compare them, so you must be able to give similarities and differences clearly.

The chemical reaction (same in both methods)

The reaction is a straightforward acid–base neutralisation:

`` 2 NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ ``

Or, if the ammonia is supplied as aqueous solution (which it is in the lab):

`` 2 NH₃ (aq) + H₂SO₄ (aq) → (NH₄)₂SO₄ (aq) ``

Ammonia acts as the base; sulfuric acid is the acid; ammonium sulfate is the salt. There is no water in the equation when the ammonia is written as NH₃ — the H from the acid joins the ammonia to form the NH₄⁺ ion directly.

You should be able to write and balance this equation from memory. In the November 2020 examiner report (1CH0/1H), examiners noted that "candidates also found it hard to write the balanced equation for the reaction" — so practise it.

Laboratory preparation (small scale)

The laboratory method uses a titration approach so that you end up with a pure salt solution — no leftover acid, no leftover ammonia, and no indicator dye contaminating the crystals.

Apparatus: burette, pipette (with safety filler), conical flask, white tile, clamp stand, indicator (methyl orange or phenolphthalein), evaporating basin, beaker, Bunsen burner / heating mantle, tripod, gauze, filter funnel and filter paper, watch glass.

*Method:*

  1. Use a pipette to transfer 25 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid into a conical flask.
  2. Add 2–3 drops of indicator (methyl orange or phenolphthalein) to the acid.
  3. Fill the burette with dilute ammonia solution and record the starting volume.
  4. Add the ammonia from the burette into the flask, swirling continuously, until the indicator changes colour (the end-point).

- With methyl orange, the colour changes from red to yellow. - With phenolphthalein, the colour changes from colourless to pink.

  1. Record the final burette reading and calculate the volume of ammonia used.
  2. Now repeat the procedure without indicator, adding exactly the volume of ammonia you just measured to a fresh 25 cm³ of acid. This second run produces a pure salt solution that has not been contaminated by indicator dye.
  3. Transfer the solution to an evaporating basin and heat gently to the point of crystallisation — evaporate roughly half the water so the solution is saturated.
  4. Leave the saturated solution to cool slowly so that crystals form.
  5. Filter to collect the crystals, then pat dry between two pieces of filter paper or leave in a warm oven to dry.

The key clever step is the repeat without indicator. The first titration tells you the exact volume of ammonia needed to neutralise the acid; the second titration uses that volume to make a pure, uncontaminated salt. This same trick is used whenever you prepare a soluble salt from a soluble base in a school lab — see 3.1.9.1 Prepare a Salt by Titration.

Why heat gently and not boil dry: ammonium sulfate decomposes on strong heating, and crystals form best from a saturated solution that cools slowly. Crystals that grow slowly are larger and purer than crystals slammed out of solution by violent boiling.

A 6-mark "describe in detail" answer needs the apparatus, the titration steps, the repeat without indicator, and the crystallisation steps in a logical order.

Industrial production (large scale)

Industrially, ammonium sulfate is made by reacting ammonia (made on site by the Haber process) with sulfuric acid (made on site by the Contact process — though the spec explicitly says you do not need the details of how sulfuric acid is made industrially).

*Raw materials:*

  • Ammonia is made on site by the Haber process from nitrogen (from the air) and hydrogen (usually obtained from natural gas, mainly methane). See 4.2.2.1 The Haber Process.
  • Sulfuric acid is made on site by the Contact process from sulfur, oxygen and water. Details not required by the spec.

*Method:*

  1. Ammonia and sulfuric acid are produced on site in large quantities by separate plants.
  2. The two streams are mixed in a large continuous-flow reactor where they react to form ammonium sulfate solution.
  3. The solution is crystallised on an industrial scale — typically by evaporation under reduced pressure or in large crystallisers.
  4. The crystals are dried, bagged or stored, and shipped as fertiliser.

The whole plant is designed to run continuously, 24 hours a day, producing many tonnes of ammonium sulfate per day. Heat released by the neutralisation reaction and by other on-site processes is often recycled through heat exchangers to keep energy costs down.

Why no indicator industrially: continuous flow plants use pH electrodes and automated control systems to monitor the reaction. Sensors detect when the mixture is neutral and adjust the flow of ammonia or acid automatically. This is far more reliable than indicator dyes at industrial scale, and it avoids contaminating the product with dye.

5.4.1.1 Electrochemical Cells F+H ▼

Electrochemical Cells

Spec point 5.25C: Recall that a chemical cell produces a voltage until one of the reactants is used up. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 1 of the Separate Chemistry (1CH0) course only — this spec point is not in Combined Science.

A chemical cell (also called an electrochemical cell or a simple cell) is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy using a redox reaction. Every common battery you have used — the AA in a remote control, the button cell in a watch, the lead-acid block under a car bonnet — is a chemical cell or a group of cells joined in series. The spec for 5.25C is short, but the underlying chemistry pulls together the reactivity series, redox, and half-equations, so it rewards a clear conceptual picture rather than rote learning.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Chemical cellA device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy using a redox reaction between two different electrodes in an electrolyte
ElectrodeA conductor (usually a metal) through which electric current enters or leaves the electrolyte
ElectrolyteA liquid or solution that contains free-moving ions and can conduct electricity
Voltage (potential difference)The "push" that drives electrons through the external circuit, measured in volts (V)
Negative electrodeThe electrode where oxidation happens — electrons leave here and flow into the external circuit
Positive electrodeThe electrode where reduction happens — electrons arrive here from the external circuit
Redox reactionA reaction in which one species is oxidised (loses electrons) and another is reduced (gains electrons) at the same time
Flat cellA cell that no longer produces a voltage because one of the reactants has been used up

How a simple chemical cell is built

A simple chemical cell needs three things:

  1. Two different metal electrodes — for example, a strip of zinc and a strip of copper.
  2. An electrolyte — a solution containing ions, typically a salt solution such as sodium chloride solution, or a dilute acid such as sulfuric acid.
  3. An external circuit — a wire (usually with a voltmeter or bulb) connecting the two electrodes so that electrons can flow.

When the two metals are dipped into the electrolyte and connected externally, a voltage develops between them and electrons flow through the wire. The key idea is that the two metals must be different. If you used two identical zinc strips, no overall reaction could occur — both electrodes would have the same tendency to release electrons, so there would be no net flow and no voltage.

Why a voltage develops — the role of reactivity

The reactivity series ranks metals by how readily they form positive ions by losing electrons. Potassium, sodium, magnesium and zinc are high up because they lose electrons easily; copper, silver and gold are near the bottom because they hold onto their electrons more tightly.

In a chemical cell:

  • The more reactive metal loses electrons more readily. Those electrons accumulate on its surface and then flow out into the wire. This metal is therefore the negative electrode and is oxidised.
  • The less reactive metal accepts the electrons arriving through the wire. At its surface, positive ions in the electrolyte gain those electrons. This metal is therefore the positive electrode, and the reduction happens at it.

The voltage produced depends on the difference in reactivity between the two metals. The further apart the two metals are in the reactivity series, the larger the voltage produced. The closer together they are, the smaller the voltage.

The exact numbers depend on conditions (concentration, temperature) but the relative ordering is what the Edexcel spec expects you to predict:

Half-equations at each electrode (Higher tier)

At Higher tier, you may be asked to write half-equations for the reactions at each electrode. These follow the same rules as half-equations for electrolysis (see 3.2.4.1 Electrolysis & Redox) — the more reactive metal is oxidised; the ion of the less reactive metal is reduced.

For a zinc/copper cell with copper(II) sulfate solution as the electrolyte:

  • Negative electrode (oxidation): Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻
  • Positive electrode (reduction): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
  • Overall cell reaction: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

For a magnesium/copper cell with the same electrolyte:

  • Negative electrode (oxidation): Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
  • Positive electrode (reduction): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
  • Overall cell reaction: Mg + Cu²⁺ → Mg²⁺ + Cu

When you write these, the electrons must balance between the two half-equations. The number of electrons released by the metal being oxidised must equal the number gained by the ions being reduced. State symbols are not always required at GCSE but writing them correctly does not lose marks: Zn (s), Zn²⁺ (aq), Cu²⁺ (aq), Cu (s).

Why cells go flat — the heart of spec point 5.25C

The spec wording is very specific: a chemical cell produces a voltage until one of the reactants is used up. This is the central exam-tested idea.

In a chemical cell, the redox reaction is finite. There is a fixed mass of metal at the negative electrode and a fixed quantity of metal ions in the electrolyte. As current flows:

  • The more reactive metal (negative electrode) slowly dissolves into the electrolyte as positive ions — its mass decreases.
  • The less reactive metal ions in the electrolyte are converted to metal atoms and deposited at the positive electrode — their concentration falls.

Eventually, one of these reactants runs out. The negative electrode may dissolve away completely, or the electrolyte may run low on the ions being reduced, or the products may build up to a level that stops the reaction. Once a reactant is used up, no more electrons can be transferred, no more current flows, and the voltage drops to zero. The cell is described as flat or discharged.

This is why a non-rechargeable battery only lasts a finite time. In a rechargeable cell (such as a lead-acid car battery), electrical energy can be put back in to reverse the redox reaction and regenerate the reactants — but a non-rechargeable cell stays flat once it is finished.

Chemical cells vs fuel cells — a comparison

Fuel cells (spec point 5.4.2.1) are also electrochemical devices, but they work differently to chemical cells. A comparison clarifies why fuel cells don't "go flat" in the same way:

Commercial examples of chemical cells

You don't need to memorise the detailed chemistry of commercial batteries for 5.25C, but it is helpful to recognise them as real-world examples of the same idea:

  • Alkaline batteries (zinc / manganese(IV) oxide) — used in torches, remote controls, toys. Non-rechargeable.
  • Button cells (silver oxide, lithium) — used in watches and small electronics. Compact, long-life, non-rechargeable.
  • Lead-acid batteries (lead / lead(IV) oxide in sulfuric acid) — used as car batteries. Rechargeable.
  • Lithium-ion batteries — used in phones, laptops, electric cars. Rechargeable.

In every case the cell goes flat once one of the reactants is used up. Rechargeable cells can be returned to a charged state by passing an electric current through them in the reverse direction, restoring the reactants.

5.4.2.1 Fuel Cells F+H ▼

Fuel Cells

Spec point 5.26C — "Recall that in a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell hydrogen and oxygen are used to produce a voltage and water is the only product." Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only, Paper 1.

A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell is an electrochemical device that turns the chemical energy stored in hydrogen and oxygen directly into electrical energy. Unlike the simple chemical cells in spec point 5.4.1.1 — which contain a fixed amount of reactant inside the cell and stop working when those reactants are used up — a fuel cell has its reactants supplied continuously from outside. As long as hydrogen and oxygen keep flowing in, the cell keeps producing a voltage.

The single fact you must be able to recall for 5.26C is: in a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell, hydrogen and oxygen are used to produce a voltage and water is the only product. That last clause — water is the only product — is the most heavily examined idea in the whole spec point.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Fuel cellAn electrochemical cell in which a fuel and oxygen react to produce electrical energy, with the reactants supplied continuously from outside the cell
Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cellA fuel cell in which hydrogen is the fuel and oxygen (often taken from air) is the oxidant; the only product is water
ElectrodeA conductor where the half-reaction takes place; in a fuel cell each electrode is usually a porous material coated with a catalyst (e.g. platinum)
OxidationThe loss of electrons by a species (occurs at the hydrogen electrode)
ReductionThe gain of electrons by a species (occurs at the oxygen electrode)
VoltageThe potential difference produced by the cell that drives electrons through an external circuit

The overall reaction

For 5.26C, the absolute minimum chemistry to memorise is the overall reaction of the cell:

`` 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O ``

Or equivalently, written per mole of water:

`` H₂ + ½ O₂ → H₂O ``

Hydrogen reacts with oxygen to make water. There is no carbon dioxide, no carbon monoxide, no nitrogen oxide, no sulfur dioxide, no soot. The chemistry contains no carbon, nitrogen or sulfur, so none of those products are possible. The only substance leaving the cell on the materials side is water — usually as water vapour because fuel cells run warm.

The single most commonly mis-recalled idea on this spec point is what the product is. Examiners have flagged that many candidates write the names of the reactants (hydrogen, oxygen) or a confused product such as "hydroxide" when asked. The product is water, and only water.

How a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell works

A working fuel cell has three essential parts: two electrodes (negative and positive) and an electrolyte (in many GCSE-level diagrams this is shown as an acidic solution that allows H⁺ ions to move between the electrodes; in real cells a polymer membrane often does the same job).

Step by step, here is what happens as the cell runs:

  1. Hydrogen gas is fed into the negative electrode. It flows over the porous electrode surface, which is usually coated with a catalyst such as platinum. The catalyst speeds up the splitting of the H–H bond.
  2. Hydrogen is oxidised. Each hydrogen molecule loses electrons, breaking down into hydrogen ions (H⁺) and electrons. The electrons are released into the metal of the electrode; the H⁺ ions move out into the electrolyte.
  3. Electrons flow through the external circuit. Because the electrons cannot pass through the electrolyte, they travel from the negative electrode round the external wire to the positive electrode. This flow of electrons through the wire is the electrical current — it is the useful output of the cell. Anything connected in the wire (a motor, a light bulb, an electronic circuit) is powered by this current.
  4. Hydrogen ions move through the electrolyte. The H⁺ ions produced at the negative electrode migrate through the electrolyte towards the positive electrode.
  5. Oxygen gas is fed into the positive electrode. Oxygen (often pulled directly from the air) flows over the other porous, catalyst-coated electrode.
  6. Oxygen is reduced. At the positive electrode, oxygen molecules combine with the arriving electrons (from the external circuit) and the arriving H⁺ ions (from the electrolyte) to form water.
  7. Water is produced and leaves the cell. The water is the only chemical product. It is usually released as warm water vapour.
  8. The cycle continues for as long as the fuel flows. Because hydrogen and oxygen are constantly being topped up from outside, the voltage is sustained. Stop the fuel supply and the cell stops producing electricity.

A useful mental picture: the hydrogen "delivers" electrons and H⁺ ions into the cell at one end. The electrons take the long way round (through the external wire, doing useful work) while the H⁺ ions take the short cut (through the electrolyte). They meet up again at the other electrode, where they combine with oxygen to produce water.

Half-equations (Higher tier)

The recall of the overall reaction is enough for Foundation. Higher-tier students must also be able to write the two half-equations that go on at each electrode.

At the negative electrode (the hydrogen side — where oxidation happens):

`` 2 H₂ → 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ ``

Hydrogen molecules lose electrons (oxidation), producing hydrogen ions and free electrons. Two H₂ molecules supply four electrons in total.

At the positive electrode (the oxygen side — where reduction happens):

`` O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 2 H₂O ``

Oxygen molecules gain electrons (reduction). Each O₂ molecule needs four electrons and four H⁺ ions to be fully converted to two water molecules.

Adding the two half-equations together (the four electrons cancel, and the four H⁺ ions cancel) gives the overall reaction:

`` 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O ``

*A check for your half-equations:*

  • Are the atoms balanced on each side?
  • Are the charges balanced on each side? (At the negative electrode: left has 0, right has +4 from four H⁺ and −4 from four e⁻, total 0. At the positive electrode: left has +4 from four H⁺ and −4 from four e⁻, total 0; right has 0. Both balance.)
  • Do the electrons cancel when you add the two halves together? (They must, because all the electrons released at one electrode end up being used at the other.)

If you cannot remember which electrode the H₂ goes to, anchor on the chemistry: hydrogen is being oxidised, and oxidation means losing electrons. The species that loses electrons is at the negative electrode (it pushes electrons into the external circuit). Then oxygen is reduced at the positive electrode — it pulls electrons in from the external circuit.

Why a fuel cell is not the same as a "battery"

This is a common source of confusion. A fuel cell looks a bit like a battery in a circuit diagram — it has two electrodes, an electrolyte, and produces a voltage. But the energy source is fundamentally different.

The phrase to use in an exam answer is that fuel cells do not run down in the way that chemical cells do. A chemical cell is a closed system: it has a finite amount of reactant, and once that has been converted to product, the cell is dead. A fuel cell is an open system: reactant flows in, product flows out, and the voltage is held steady.

Why water is the only product (and why that matters)

The chemistry is simple. The only elements present in the inputs are hydrogen and oxygen. The only stable, non-toxic substance you can make from hydrogen and oxygen at fuel-cell temperatures is water. There are no carbon atoms anywhere in the system, so there is no CO₂. There is no nitrogen in the fuel feed (oxygen is often supplied via air, but the nitrogen passes through unreacted at the operating temperature), so no nitrogen oxides. There is no sulfur, so no SO₂.

This is the central environmental selling point of hydrogen fuel-cell technology: at the point of use, the only emission is water. A fuel-cell car driving down a road releases no CO₂, no NOₓ, no particulates from the chemistry of the cell itself. (Whether the overall system is "clean" depends on how the hydrogen was made in the first place — that argument belongs to 5.4.2.2 Evaluating Fuel Cells.)

Where fuel cells are used

You do not need to give a list of uses to answer 5.26C, but a couple of real-world examples help anchor the concept:

  • Spacecraft — NASA used hydrogen–oxygen fuel cells on the Apollo missions and the Space Shuttle. They had the bonus that the water produced could be used for drinking.
  • Submarines — fuel cells run quietly and without needing oxygen from outside the vessel.
  • Hydrogen cars — production models such as the Toyota Mirai and Honda Clarity use a fuel-cell stack to power an electric motor, with only water coming out of the exhaust pipe.
  • Backup power — some hospitals, data centres and remote installations use fuel cells as a steady, low-emission emergency power source.

A full pros-and-cons evaluation of these uses sits in 5.4.2.2 Evaluating Fuel Cells.

Worked example — half-equations at Higher tier

A Higher-tier question might ask you to write the two half-equations and the overall equation for a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell.

*Step 1 — write the oxidation half-equation (negative electrode, the hydrogen side):*

Hydrogen molecules lose electrons to form hydrogen ions:

`` 2 H₂ → 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ ``

(You can equally write the simpler version H₂ → 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻, but the 4-electron version is more useful because it matches the oxygen half-equation directly.)

*Step 2 — write the reduction half-equation (positive electrode, the oxygen side):*

Oxygen gains electrons and combines with H⁺ ions to form water:

`` O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 2 H₂O ``

*Step 3 — add the two half-equations to get the overall reaction:*

The 4 electrons on the right of the first equation cancel with the 4 electrons on the left of the second. The 4 H⁺ on the right of the first cancel with the 4 H⁺ on the left of the second. What remains is:

`` 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O ``

*Step 4 — check:*

  • Hydrogens: 4 on the left (two H₂), 4 on the right (two H₂O). ✓
  • Oxygens: 2 on the left (one O₂), 2 on the right (two H₂O). ✓
  • Charges: zero on each side (no ions remain). ✓

Key Takeaways
  1. A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell produces electricity from the reaction of hydrogen and oxygen; the overall reaction is 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O.
  2. Water is the only product. This is the single most heavily examined fact for 5.26C and the most commonly mis-recalled — examiners flagged "hydroxide", "oxygen" and "hydrogen" as frequent wrong answers in 1CH0/1F June 2019.
  3. The fuel cell produces a voltage that can drive current through an external circuit; it converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy.
  4. Hydrogen and oxygen are supplied continuously from outside the cell, so the cell does not "go flat" — unlike a chemical cell whose internal reactants run out.
  5. At the negative electrode, hydrogen is oxidised: it loses electrons and forms H⁺ ions. (Higher tier: 2 H₂ → 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻.)
  6. At the positive electrode, oxygen is reduced: it gains electrons and combines with H⁺ ions to make water. (Higher tier: O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 2 H₂O.)
  7. Electrons flow from the hydrogen electrode through the external circuit to the oxygen electrode — that flow of electrons is the electrical current that powers the load.
  8. H⁺ ions move internally through the electrolyte from the negative electrode to the positive electrode.
  9. The electrodes are typically porous and platinum-coated, with platinum acting as a catalyst for the electrode reactions.
  10. At the point of use, the only emission is water; this is the key environmental advantage of fuel-cell vehicles such as the Toyota Mirai and of historic uses in spacecraft and submarines (full evaluation in 5.4.2.2 Evaluating Fuel Cells).
  11. On 1CH0/1F, expect a 1-mark recall question (e.g. "Give the name of the product formed in this fuel cell" — 1CH0/1F 2019 Q7(c)).
  12. On 1CH0/1H, expect questions worth 2–6 marks including writing balanced equations and half-equations; the November 2021 examiner report flagged that many Higher candidates left these blank or wrote prose instead of equations — practise writing them as actual equations.

Evaluating Fuel Cells

Spec point 5.27C: Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of fuel cells for given uses. Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only · Paper 1.

This spec point is not about how a fuel cell works — that is covered in 5.4.2.1 Fuel Cells. Here you are expected to weigh up advantages and disadvantages of using a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell for a given application (typically a vehicle, a spacecraft, or a portable power supply) and reach a supported judgement. The command word is almost always evaluate, which means you must give both sides and then conclude.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Fuel cellAn electrochemical cell in which a fuel (usually hydrogen) reacts with oxygen to produce a voltage; the cell continues to deliver electrical energy as long as fuel and oxygen are supplied
Hydrogen-oxygen fuel cellA specific fuel cell in which hydrogen is oxidised at one electrode and oxygen is reduced at the other, with water as the only chemical product
Chemical cell (battery)A cell that produces a voltage from a fixed quantity of reactants stored inside it; runs flat when the reactants are used up and is then either recharged or discarded
EvaluateA command word requiring advantages and disadvantages and a justified overall conclusion — not a one-sided list

EfficiencyThe fraction of chemical energy in the fuel that the cell converts to useful electrical energy, often expressed as a percentage

A quick reminder of how the cell works

You do not need to redo the mechanism here — link out to 5.4.2.1 Fuel Cells for the electrode equations. The two facts that drive the evaluation are:

  • The overall reaction is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, so the only chemical product leaving the cell is water.
  • The cell converts the chemical energy of hydrogen directly into electrical energy in a single step, instead of burning the fuel to make heat and then converting heat to motion (the route taken by a petrol or diesel engine).

Those two features — clean exhaust and direct conversion — are the source of most of the strengths listed below. Almost every weakness is downstream of the choice of hydrogen as the fuel, not of the fuel-cell device itself. Keeping that distinction in mind is what the November 2021 examiners are flagging (see Examiner Report Insights).

Strengths of hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells

Only product is water. No carbon dioxide, no nitrogen oxides, no sulfur dioxide, and no particulates are emitted from the cell. At the point of use this is genuinely clean — important in submarines, sealed spacecraft cabins, and built-up urban areas where air quality is a problem.

High efficiency. A hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell typically converts about 50–60% of the chemical energy of the hydrogen into electrical energy. A petrol or diesel internal combustion engine, by contrast, converts only about 25–30% of the chemical energy of the fuel into useful mechanical work — the rest is lost mainly as heat through the exhaust and cooling system. The fuel cell is more efficient because it avoids the heat-engine step entirely.

Continuous output. A fuel cell will keep producing electricity for as long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied. A chemical cell (battery) contains a fixed amount of stored chemicals and will go flat once they are used up. This is why fuel cells are attractive for long missions — a spacecraft, a submarine, or a long-distance vehicle can refuel rather than wait for a battery to recharge.

Quiet operation. Fuel cells have no moving parts (no pistons, no crankshaft, no turbines). That makes them mechanically simple, low-maintenance, and very quiet — useful in submarines (stealth) and in passenger vehicles (cabin noise).

Lightweight relative to power produced. Compared with a chemical-cell battery pack capable of delivering the same total amount of energy over a long mission, a fuel-cell system can be lighter, because you carry only the tanks of hydrogen and oxygen, and these can be topped up. A battery must contain all of its stored energy in its electrodes from the start, which scales mass very quickly with mission length. This mass advantage is the central reason fuel cells have been used on crewed spacecraft.

Weaknesses of hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells

Hydrogen is expensive to produce on an industrial scale. Although hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe, almost none of it exists as free H₂ on Earth — it is locked up in water and in hydrocarbons. Getting it out costs energy and money.

Hydrogen production is often not clean. Most commercial hydrogen today is made by steam reforming of natural gas, which produces carbon dioxide as a by-product. So even though the fuel cell itself emits only water, the overall carbon footprint of using hydrogen depends on where the hydrogen came from. "Green" hydrogen made by electrolysis of water (see 3.2.4.1 Electrolysis & Redox) using renewable electricity is genuinely low-carbon — but it is currently more expensive than reformed hydrogen, and the renewable electricity itself is a limited resource.

Hydrogen is difficult to store. Hydrogen is the lightest gas in existence, so to carry a useful quantity in a small space you must either compress it to very high pressure or cool it to a liquid below −253 °C. Both are technically demanding, energy-hungry, and expensive. Hydrogen is also flammable across a wide range of concentrations in air, and hydrogen-oxygen mixtures are explosive — so the tanks need to be safety-engineered to a very high standard.

Low energy density by volume. Hydrogen has a very high energy density by mass (each kilogram of hydrogen stores a lot of energy), but a poor energy density by volume compared with petrol or diesel. That means hydrogen fuel tanks tend to be large for a given driving range, which is awkward in passenger cars.

Infrastructure is not in place. There are very few hydrogen refuelling stations in most countries. Until the network of stations matches that of petrol stations, hydrogen vehicles are impractical for everyday motorists, regardless of how good the cell technology is.

Expensive catalysts. Current hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells use platinum catalysts on the electrodes. Platinum is rare and very expensive, and its supply is limited and concentrated in a small number of countries. This pushes up the cost of the cell itself and raises sustainability concerns.

Worked 6-mark "Evaluate" answer — spacecraft application

The Edexcel framing for this spec point is almost always: evaluate the use of a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell, rather than a chemical cell, for a given application. To get full marks on a 6-mark evaluation you need two or three advantages, two or three disadvantages, and a supported conclusion.

Below is a worked answer at the level required for a 6-mark Higher tier question modelled on 1CH0/1H November 2020 Q7(b) — "Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of providing electrical energy in a spacecraft using hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells rather than chemical cells."

> A hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell has several advantages over a chemical cell in a spacecraft. First, it produces electricity continuously as long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied, whereas a chemical cell stores a fixed amount of energy and would go flat on a long mission. Second, the only product of the cell is water, which the crew can drink, so the cell delivers both electricity and a useful by-product — this is especially valuable on a spacecraft where every kilogram of mass and every litre of water has to be carried up. Third, the fuel cell is about 50–60% efficient, much higher than a heat engine. > > The disadvantages are mostly to do with hydrogen itself. Hydrogen has to be carried at very high pressure or as a liquid below −253 °C, both of which need heavy and well-engineered tanks; these tanks add mass to the spacecraft. Hydrogen is also flammable, and hydrogen-oxygen mixtures are explosive, so the system must be carefully designed to be safe. The cells use expensive platinum catalysts. > > Overall, for a long spacecraft mission, the advantages outweigh the disadvantages: the continuous power supply, the drinkable water by-product, and the lower mass for long missions are more important than the storage and cost problems, which can be engineered around. A hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell is therefore the better choice over a chemical cell for this use.

That conclusion sentence is non-negotiable for an evaluate question — without it, even an answer that lists everything correctly is typically capped below full marks.

The list of advantages and disadvantages does not change much from question to question, but which of them you emphasise depends on the application. Use this rough mapping:
