6.1.1.1 Group Classification F+H ▼

Group Classification

Spec point 6.1 — Explain why some elements can be classified as alkali metals (group 1), halogens (group 7) or noble gases (group 0), based on their position in the periodic table. Foundation + Higher tier, examined on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H).

The modern periodic table is arranged into vertical columns called groups. Elements in the same group share similar chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell. Three of these groups have specific names and well-defined characteristics that you are expected to recognise: Group 1 — the alkali metals, Group 7 — the halogens, and Group 0 — the noble gases. Knowing the correct group name, the correct group number, and the underlying reason for each name is the entire content of this spec point.

Why elements are classified into groups

Every element in a given group of the periodic table has the same number of electrons in its outer shell. It is this outer-shell arrangement that controls how an atom bonds and reacts. Because chemistry is dominated by what happens to the outer electrons, elements with the same outer-shell count behave in very similar ways — they form the same kinds of compounds, take part in the same kinds of reactions, and show the same trends in physical properties as you move down the column.

This is why we can group elements together and give the families a name: their similar electronic structure produces similar chemistry.

  • Group 1 atoms each have 1 electron in their outer shell.
  • Group 7 atoms each have 7 electrons in their outer shell.
  • Group 0 atoms have a full outer shell (helium has 2; neon, argon and the others have 8).

The group number on the modern periodic table tells you directly how many outer-shell electrons an atom has, with the single exception of Group 0 (where "0" indicates "no spare electrons available for reaction" — the outer shell is already full).

Group 1 — the alkali metals

Group 1 is the first vertical column of the periodic table (on the far left, ignoring hydrogen which sits at the top for historical reasons but is not an alkali metal). The members you need to recognise are lithium (Li), sodium (Na), potassium (K), rubidium (Rb), caesium (Cs) and francium (Fr).

They are called the alkali metals because:

  • They are all metals — shiny when freshly cut, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable.
  • They react with water to produce a metal hydroxide solution, which is an alkali (a soluble base with a pH above 7).

For example, sodium reacts with water:

2Na (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2NaOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

The sodium hydroxide produced makes the solution alkaline. The same pattern applies all the way down the group, so the family name "alkali metals" captures both the metallic character and the alkaline products of their reaction with water.

Because they all have exactly one outer-shell electron, alkali metals tend to lose that electron easily in reactions, forming 1+ ions (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ and so on). This shared electronic structure is the underlying reason their chemistry is so similar.

Group 7 — the halogens

Group 7 sits on the right-hand side of the periodic table, in the second-to-last column. The members you need to recognise are fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br), iodine (I) and astatine (At).

They are called the halogens — a word that means "salt-former" — because they react with metals to produce salts. For example, sodium reacts with chlorine to make sodium chloride (table salt):

2Na (s) + Cl₂ (g) → 2NaCl (s)

Key features of the halogens to recognise:

  • They are non-metals.
  • They exist as diatomic molecules: F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. The two atoms are joined by a single covalent bond.
  • They each have 7 electrons in their outer shell, so they tend to gain one electron in reactions, forming 1− ions (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻). These ions are called halides — note carefully that "halide" is the name of the ion, not the name of the group. Mixing these two up is a very common mistake.
  • They have coloured vapours: chlorine is pale green/yellow, bromine is orange-brown, iodine is purple.

The halogens are the most reactive group of non-metals. As with Group 1, the shared 7-electron outer shell is what gives all halogens such similar chemical behaviour.

Group 0 — the noble gases

Group 0 is the final column on the right-hand side of the periodic table. The members you need to recognise are helium (He), neon (Ne), argon (Ar), krypton (Kr), xenon (Xe) and radon (Rn).

They are called the noble gases because:

  • They are all gases at room temperature.
  • They are "noble" — chemically unreactive, almost completely inert under normal conditions. In older chemistry texts they were also called the "inert gases" for this reason.

The reason they are so unreactive is that their outer electron shell is full:

  • Helium has 2 electrons, which fills the first shell.
  • Neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon all have 8 electrons in their outer shell.

A full outer shell is a very stable electronic arrangement. Because the atoms do not need to gain, lose or share electrons to become stable, they do not form ions and rarely form bonds. That is also why noble gases exist as single atoms (monatomic) — they don't even bond with themselves, so they are not diatomic like the halogens.

The label "0" in "Group 0" reflects this: there are zero spare outer-shell electrons available for bonding.

This comparison is the kind of overview question Foundation papers often probe in their early multiple-choice or short-answer items: "give the name of Group 7", "state which group contains the noble gases", "name an element from Group 1".

Using the periodic table to predict classification

Because group membership is fixed by an element's position in the periodic table, you can answer any classification question simply by looking at the column:

  1. Find the element in the periodic table.
  2. Read off the group number at the top of the column.
  3. Apply the rule:

- Column labelled 1 → alkali metal - Column labelled 7 → halogen - Column labelled 0 → noble gas

  1. From the group number, you can also state the number of outer-shell electrons (1, 7 or full).

For example, asked "what type of element is potassium?" — potassium is in column 1, so it is an alkali metal, with 1 outer-shell electron, and it will react with water to produce an alkaline solution of potassium hydroxide.

Common errors to avoid

  • Halogens are Group 7, not Group 6 or Group 8. A clean way to remember it: there are 7 letters in "halogen" if you spell it out — but only use that as a memory aid, not in the exam answer.
  • "Halides" and "halogens" are different. A halogen is a Group 7 element (e.g. Cl₂); a halide is the 1− ion formed from that element (e.g. Cl⁻). On exam papers asking for the name of Group 7, "halides" is not accepted.
  • Noble gases are not just "gases". Many elements are gases (oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen). The Group 0 family name is noble gases because they are unreactive.
  • Hydrogen is not an alkali metal. Hydrogen sits at the top of Group 1 on most periodic tables for historical reasons, but it is a non-metal and is not classed with the alkali metals.
  • Don't confuse "Group 0" with "no elements in it". The "0" refers to the lack of reactive outer electrons, not to an absence of members.

Key Takeaways
  1. Elements in the same vertical column of the periodic table form a group and share similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons.
  2. Group 1 — alkali metals: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium. One outer-shell electron; form 1+ ions; react with water to give alkaline metal hydroxide solutions.
  3. Group 7 — halogens: fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine. Seven outer-shell electrons; form 1− ions called halides; exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂); react with metals to form salts.
  4. Group 0 — noble gases: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon. Full outer shell; exist as single atoms (monatomic); chemically very unreactive.
  5. Helium has 2 outer-shell electrons (a full first shell); the rest of Group 0 have 8 outer-shell electrons (a full outer shell).
  6. The label "0" in Group 0 reflects that there are no spare outer-shell electrons available for reaction — the shell is already full.
  7. Group 1 elements are metals; Group 7 and Group 0 elements are non-metals.
  8. A halide is the 1− ion (e.g. Cl⁻); a halogen is the Group 7 element (e.g. Cl₂). The two names are not interchangeable.
  9. To classify any element, look up its column number on the periodic table: column 1 → alkali metal, column 7 → halogen, column 0 → noble gas.
  10. Exam questions on this spec point are usually 1-mark recall items on Foundation Paper 2; the marks are awarded for the exact group name, so avoid vague answers like "gases" or wrong terms like "halides".
  11. Hydrogen is shown at the top of Group 1 on most periodic tables, but it is not an alkali metal — it is a non-metal.
  12. Knowing the group instantly tells you the number of outer-shell electrons, the type of ion formed, and the general reactivity, which is why group classification is so powerful in predicting chemistry.
6.1.1.2 Physical Properties in Group 1 F+H ▼

Physical Properties in Group 1

Spec point 6.2 — "Recall that alkali metals: a) are soft, b) have relatively low melting points." Examined on Foundation and Higher tier in both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

Group 1 — lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium — sits on the far left of the periodic table. Every element in this group is a metal, yet their physical properties are unlike the typical metals students meet earlier in the course (iron, copper, aluminium). They are soft, low-density, and have low melting points compared with most other metals. These features are not random — they are direct consequences of the bonding inside the metal, and Edexcel will expect you to recall them as factual properties at GCSE without necessarily explaining the deeper "why" at Foundation tier.

Key Definitions

Alkali metalAn element in Group 1 of the periodic table (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) — a soft, reactive metal with one electron in its outer shell

The two properties Edexcel wants you to recall

Spec point 6.2 specifies just two physical properties that must be recalled for Group 1:

  1. They are soft — soft enough to be cut with a knife, exposing a shiny silvery surface that quickly tarnishes in air.
  2. They have relatively low melting points — "relatively" means compared with most other metals. Iron melts at 1538 °C and copper at 1085 °C; sodium melts at only 98 °C, and caesium melts below body temperature.

Other features (low density, silvery appearance, good conductors of heat and electricity) are true of alkali metals but are not the two properties listed in spec point 6.2. Always lead with soft and low melting point in a recall question.

A typical-metal comparison

Most metals you've met earlier in the course behave very differently from Group 1. The table below puts the two side by side so the contrast is clear.

The shared metallic features (good conductors, shiny when freshly cut) come from delocalised outer-shell electrons. The differences (soft, low melting point, low density) come from how weak the metallic bonding is in Group 1 — and that is the higher-level "why" behind the two recall facts.

Why are alkali metals soft and low-melting?

Although spec point 6.2 only asks for recall, understanding the reason makes the property easy to remember and helps with explain-style questions in related spec points.

Why they're soft. In a metallic lattice, atoms are held in place by the attraction between positive metal ions and the surrounding sea of delocalised electrons. Group 1 atoms only release one outer electron each into that sea, so the lattice has a relatively low density of delocalised electrons and weak attraction holding the layers of ions together. Layers of ions can therefore slide over each other easily — and a knife blade only needs to push the layers apart a small distance. In contrast, transition metals release several outer electrons each into the sea, giving stronger, harder-to-disrupt metallic bonding.

Why they have low melting points. Melting a metal means giving the ions enough energy to break free from their fixed positions in the lattice. With only one electron per atom in the delocalised sea, the electrostatic attraction between ions and electrons is comparatively weak — less energy (i.e. a lower temperature) is needed to break the lattice. Group 1 metallic bonds are therefore weaker than the metallic bonds in most other metals, and the melting points are correspondingly lower.

You don't need to write this reasoning in a simple recall question, but it sits behind any question that asks you to explain why Group 1 metals are soft or have low melting points.

Softness — what it looks like in practice

When potassium or sodium is taken out of its oil storage and placed on a tile, you can press a clean scalpel through it as if it were hard cheese. The freshly cut surface is a bright silvery colour for a second or two before going dull as the metal reacts with oxygen and moisture in the air. The fact that the surface tarnishes almost instantly is one reason why these metals are stored under oil — not for the physical property itself, but to prevent the reaction with air and water. Storage is covered in 6.1.1.3 How Alkali Metals React with Water.

In the core practical demonstrations and the photographs that often appear in Edexcel papers, you'll see:

  • A small lump of metal cut with a knife or scalpel
  • A freshly exposed shiny surface
  • Rapid dulling/tarnishing of that surface within seconds

If a photograph shows a teacher cutting a silvery metal with a knife and the surface goes dull, the metal is almost certainly a Group 1 element — and you can use those two visual cues (soft enough to cut, tarnishes fast) to identify it.

Common student confusions

"All metals are hard." This is true of the metals you meet in Section 5 (iron, steel, copper, aluminium) but not of Group 1. Make sure you reach for the word soft when the question is about alkali metals, not the generic metallic properties.

"Low melting point means non-metal." Mercury is a metal that's liquid at room temperature — low melting point alone doesn't make something a non-metal. Group 1 elements remain solid at room temperature (because their melting points, though low for metals, are still above 20 °C for Li through Cs), and they show the other metallic features (shiny, conductive). They are still metals — just unusual ones.

Mixing up melting point with reactivity. Reactivity and melting point both change as you go down Group 1, but in opposite directions: reactivity increases down the group while melting point decreases. Don't link the two in a sentence like "they are more reactive because they melt at lower temperatures" — that is not the reason. Reactivity is governed by how easily the outer electron is lost (covered in 6.1.2.1 Group 1: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations).

"Soft" applied to chemical reactivity. In everyday English "soft" can mean "gentle". In chemistry it means physically easy to deform. A potassium metal that's soft is still chemically very reactive — softness is a mechanical property, not a chemical one.

6.1.1.3 How Alkali Metals React with Water F+H ▼

How Alkali Metals React with Water

Spec 6.3 — Describe the reactions of lithium, sodium and potassium with water (Foundation + Higher tier, 1CH0 + 1SC0, Paper 2).

The Group 1 metals — lithium, sodium and potassium — all react with cold water in the same general way. Each metal reacts to form an aqueous metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas. The differences are in how vigorously the reaction happens: you should be able to describe the visual observations for each metal in turn, link them to the trend in reactivity going down the group, and write balanced equations for the reactions.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Alkali metalA Group 1 element (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) that reacts with water to form an alkaline solution
EffervescenceThe visible release of gas bubbles from a reaction mixture (also called fizzing — these count as the same observation)
HydroxideAn ionic compound containing the OH⁻ ion; Group 1 hydroxides are soluble bases (alkalis)
AlkaliA soluble base; in water it produces a solution with pH > 7 and turns universal indicator blue/purple
IndicatorA substance whose colour changes with pH — used to confirm an alkaline product (e.g. universal indicator, phenolphthalein, red litmus)

The general reaction

Every Group 1 metal reacts with cold water to produce a metal hydroxide solution and hydrogen gas:

metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen

In symbol form, using M to represent any Group 1 metal:

2M (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2MOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

The metal hydroxide dissolves to give a clear, colourless alkaline solution. If a few drops of universal indicator are added to the water before the reaction, the solution turns purple (pH around 12–14) as the hydroxide forms. With red litmus paper, the solution turns blue; with phenolphthalein, it turns pink.

The hydrogen gas given off can be tested with a lit splint — it produces a characteristic squeaky pop. With potassium, the hydrogen is often ignited during the reaction itself, so you may not need a separate test.

Lithium and water

When a small piece of lithium is dropped onto water in a trough, the following observations are made:

  • The lithium floats on the surface (it is less dense than water).
  • The metal fizzes steadily — bubbles of hydrogen gas come off around it.
  • The lithium moves slowly across the surface, pushed by escaping hydrogen.
  • The lithium gradually shrinks and disappears as it is used up.
  • The solution turns universal indicator purple (lithium hydroxide is alkaline).
  • The reaction does not produce a flame; lithium is the least reactive of the three.

Symbol equation:

2Li (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2LiOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

Sodium and water

Sodium is more reactive than lithium. When a small, freshly cut piece of sodium is added to water:

  • The sodium floats on the surface (less dense than water).
  • It fizzes vigorously — much faster than lithium.
  • The heat released melts the sodium into a silvery ball (sodium has a low melting point and this reaction is exothermic enough to melt it).
  • The molten ball whizzes around the surface of the water, pushed by escaping hydrogen.
  • The metal disappears within a few seconds.
  • The resulting solution is alkaline (turns universal indicator purple) — sodium hydroxide has formed.
  • A lilac flame is sometimes seen if the hydrogen ignites.

Symbol equation:

2Na (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2NaOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

Potassium and water

Potassium is the most reactive of the three. When a small piece of potassium is dropped onto water:

  • The potassium floats on the surface.
  • It reacts so vigorously that the hydrogen gas produced ignites immediately, burning with a distinctive lilac flame.
  • The metal melts into a ball and whizzes rapidly across the water surface.
  • There may be a small explosion or popping/spitting as the reaction nears completion (this is the hydrogen igniting in air).
  • The metal is used up very quickly.
  • The resulting solution is alkaline — potassium hydroxide has formed.

Symbol equation:

2K (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2KOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

Comparing the three reactions

MetalSpeed of reactionFloats?Melts?Flame?Solution alkaline?
LithiumSlow — steady fizzingYesNoNoYes
SodiumFast — vigorous fizzingYesYes (silver ball)Sometimes (lilac if H₂ ignites)Yes

Why reactivity increases down the group. All three metals have one electron in their outer shell, and the reaction with water involves losing that electron to form a 1+ ion (M⁺). As you go down Group 1, the outer electron is in a shell that is further from the nucleus and is shielded by more inner shells of electrons. The pull from the positive nucleus on the outer electron therefore gets weaker. This means the outer electron is lost more easily, so each metal reacts faster and more vigorously than the one above it. Lithium → sodium → potassium therefore shows a clear increase in reactivity. (For the full electronic-configuration explanation see 6.1.2.1 Group 1: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations and 1.2.3.1 Electronic Configurations.)

Word and symbol equations to learn

Make sure you can write all three in both word and symbol form, with state symbols:

lithium + water → lithium hydroxide + hydrogen 2Li (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2LiOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen 2Na (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2NaOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

potassium + water → potassium hydroxide + hydrogen 2K (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2KOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

The general form 2M (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2MOH (aq) + H₂ (g) works for any Group 1 metal, including rubidium and caesium (see 6.1.1.4 Trends in Group 1 for predicted behaviour of the lower members).

Safety in the demonstration

These reactions are normally done as a teacher demonstration:

  • Only a very small piece of metal is used (rice-grain size for potassium).
  • The metal is cut fresh and the oily storage liquid is blotted off before adding to water.
  • A safety screen is placed between the trough and the class.
  • Students wear eye protection.
  • Potassium is not used with anything larger than a small piece because of the risk of the hydrogen igniting violently.

You won't be asked to do these reactions yourself — but you may be asked to describe what you would observe, or to predict observations for rubidium or caesium using the trend.

6.1.1.4 Trends in Group 1 F+H ▼

Trends in Group 1

Spec point 6.4: Describe the pattern in reactivity of the alkali metals, lithium, sodium and potassium, with water; and use this pattern to predict the reactivity of other alkali metals. Foundation + Higher tier; Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0); Paper 2.

Group 1 — the alkali metals — show one of the clearest reactivity trends in the whole Periodic Table. As you move down the group from lithium to caesium, reactivity with water increases. You are expected to describe this pattern using lithium, sodium and potassium (which can all be demonstrated safely in a school lab), and then predict the behaviour of rubidium, caesium and francium by extending the trend.

Describing the pattern (the spec-point answer)

The core idea you must be able to write down is short but precise: as you go down Group 1, the reactivity of the alkali metals with water increases.

A typical "describe the trend" answer can build that up using the three metals named in the spec:

  • Lithium floats, fizzes gently and gradually disappears
  • Sodium floats, melts into a ball (because the reaction is exothermic enough to melt sodium's low-melting-point metal), and fizzes more vigorously than lithium
  • Potassium reacts so vigorously that the hydrogen produced ignites, burning with a lilac flame, and the potassium often disappears with a small explosion

For each metal, the products are the same type: a Group 1 hydroxide solution (alkaline, hence the name "alkali metal") and hydrogen gas. The general word and symbol equations are:

> alkali metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen

> 2 M (s) + 2 H₂O (l) → 2 MOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

For the three named metals:

  • 2 Li (s) + 2 H₂O (l) → 2 LiOH (aq) + H₂ (g)
  • 2 Na (s) + 2 H₂O (l) → 2 NaOH (aq) + H₂ (g)
  • 2 K (s) + 2 H₂O (l) → 2 KOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

The resulting solutions are all alkaline (they turn red litmus blue and universal indicator purple) because they contain hydroxide ions, OH⁻ (aq).

Why reactivity increases down the group

You are not directly assessed on the explanation under 6.4 — that lives in 6.1.2.1 Group 1: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations — but understanding the why stops the trend from being a list to memorise.

When a Group 1 atom reacts, it loses its single outer electron to form a 1+ ion. The easier that outer electron is to lose, the more reactive the metal is. Going down the group:

  • The atoms get larger because each successive element has an extra full electron shell
  • The outer electron is therefore further from the nucleus
  • It is also shielded by more inner electron shells
  • The electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker

So the outer electron is held less tightly and is lost more easily. That is why rubidium reacts more violently than potassium, which reacts more violently than sodium, which reacts more violently than lithium. The same logic lets you predict caesium will be even more reactive again.

Predicting reactions of unstudied alkali metals

The most common exam task is to use the trend to predict what would happen if a piece of rubidium (or caesium) were dropped onto water. The expected style of answer extends the lithium/sodium/potassium descriptions one step further. For rubidium on water, you would expect:

  • Rubidium would float on the water (low density)
  • It would react more vigorously than potassium
  • The reaction would be exothermic — heat released would melt the metal
  • The hydrogen produced would ignite — possibly with a small explosion
  • The rubidium would appear to disappear as it reacts and dissolves
  • The resulting solution would be alkaline (rubidium hydroxide, RbOH)
  • A few centimetres of the metal would be enough to give a violent reaction

If asked for a symbol equation:

> 2 Rb (s) + 2 H₂O (l) → 2 RbOH (aq) + H₂ (g)

The same template works for caesium — just intensify the language ("explosive", "very vigorous", "ignites instantly"). Francium is so rare and radioactive that you would only be asked to predict behaviour, never observations from a real reaction.

6.1.2.1 Group 1: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations F+H ▼

Group 1: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations

Spec point 6.5: Explain this pattern in reactivity in terms of electronic configurations. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H), and on the equivalent Combined Science papers.

This spec point builds directly on the descriptive trend you learn in 6.1.1.4: as you go down Group 1 (Li → Na → K → Rb → Cs), the elements become more reactive. Spec point 6.5 asks you to go one step further and explain why that pattern exists, using the electronic configurations of the atoms. Examiners reward precise language about the outer electron and the number of shells, not vague statements about "electrons" or "outer shells".

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Group 1The first column of the periodic table; the alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr)
Electronic configurationThe arrangement of electrons in shells around the nucleus of an atom, written as a series of numbers (e.g. 2,8,1 for sodium)
Outer shellThe highest occupied energy level of an atom; the shell furthest from the nucleus that contains electrons
Outer electronThe electron in the outer (highest) shell; for Group 1 atoms, this is the single electron each atom must lose to react
ReactivityA measure of how readily an element takes part in a chemical reaction; for Group 1, how readily the atom loses its outer electron

Electrostatic attractionThe pulling force between particles of opposite charge — here, between the positive nucleus and the negative outer electron

Electronic configurations of the Group 1 metals

Every Group 1 atom has exactly one electron in its outer shell. That is the single most important fact for this spec point. What changes as you go down the group is the number of inner shells sitting between the nucleus and that outer electron.

Rb and Cs are shown here for context — you would not be expected to write their full configurations in an exam, but you should know they continue the same pattern: one outer electron, more inner shells than the element above.

Why Group 1 elements are reactive in the first place

A Group 1 atom reacts by losing its single outer electron to form a 1+ ion. For example:

  • Li → Li⁺ + e⁻ (electronic configuration changes from 2,1 to 2)
  • Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ (from 2,8,1 to 2,8)
  • K → K⁺ + e⁻ (from 2,8,8,1 to 2,8,8)

Once the outer electron is lost, the ion has the same stable electronic configuration as the noble gas in the previous period (Li⁺ is like He; Na⁺ is like Ne; K⁺ is like Ar). Because losing just one electron achieves a full outer shell, Group 1 atoms are highly reactive metals — they only have to give up a single electron to become stable.

The easier it is for an atom to lose that outer electron, the more reactive the element is. So the question of "why is reactivity what it is?" becomes "how strongly is the outer electron held?"

Why reactivity increases down Group 1

Three factors decide how strongly the outer electron is held by the nucleus:

  1. Distance of the outer electron from the nucleus — set by the number of shells.
  2. Shielding by the inner-shell electrons — also set by the number of inner shells.
  3. Nuclear charge — the number of protons in the nucleus.

As you go down Group 1:

  • The number of occupied shells increases (Li has 2 shells, Na has 3, K has 4, Rb has 5, Cs has 6).
  • So the outer electron is further from the nucleus.
  • There are more inner-shell electrons between the nucleus and the outer electron, which shield the outer electron from the full pull of the nucleus.
  • The electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is therefore weaker.
  • The outer electron is lost more easily, so the atom reacts more readily.

Nuclear charge does increase as well (Li has 3 protons, Cs has 55), but the effects of greater distance and increased shielding outweigh the increased nuclear charge. The net result is that the outer electron is held less tightly in Cs than in Li, even though Cs has many more protons.

This is the explanation examiners want — written in terms of the outer electron and the number of (whole) shells, not vague references to "electrons" or "outer shells".

Worked example — explaining why potassium is more reactive than lithium

This is the classic exam-style question (see the citation in the Exam Appearance section below). Here is how to answer it for full marks.

Question (3 marks): Explain, in terms of their electronic configurations, why lithium is less reactive than potassium.

*Model answer:*

  • Lithium has 2 shells (electronic configuration 2,1) whereas potassium has 4 shells (electronic configuration 2,8,8,1). (1)
  • The outer electron in potassium is further from the nucleus and is more shielded by inner-shell electrons than the outer electron in lithium. (1)
  • So the electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker in potassium, the outer electron is lost more easily, and potassium is more reactive than lithium. (1)

*Why this scores 3 and a vague answer scores 1 or 0:*

  • The answer names the outer electron specifically — not just "electrons".
  • It compares the number of shells — not the number of "outer shells" (every Group 1 atom has just one outer shell, so saying potassium has "more outer shells" is wrong).
  • It links the weaker attraction to the outer electron being lost more easily, which is what reactivity actually means here.

That is why all three react by losing one electron to form a 1+ ion — and why all three undergo the same type of reaction with water (M + H₂O → MOH + ½H₂). The rate of that reaction is what changes, and that rate is what the electronic configuration explanation accounts for.

Linking back to observed reactions with water

You already know (from 6.1.1.3) that the visible reactions with water get more vigorous down the group:

  • Lithium fizzes steadily and floats on the surface.
  • Sodium fizzes more vigorously, melts into a ball from the heat released, and moves rapidly across the surface.
  • Potassium ignites the hydrogen produced with a lilac flame and can pop or explode.

Spec point 6.5 is asking you to take these descriptive observations and connect them to the electronic structure. The reactions get more vigorous because the outer electron is held less tightly in K than in Na, and less tightly in Na than in Li — for the reasons set out above (more shells, more shielding, outer electron further from the nucleus).

Common errors to avoid

Examiners highlight the same imprecise wording every year. Make sure you do not:

  • Refer to "electrons" in general when you should be referring specifically to the outer electron. Group 1 atoms only lose one electron per atom — the outer one — so vague references to "electrons" will not get the mark.
  • Say that potassium has "more outer shells". Every Group 1 atom has just one outer shell. What potassium has is more shells in total — i.e. more inner shells between the nucleus and the outer electron.
  • Confuse this trend with Group 7. In Group 7, reactivity decreases down the group, because the atom is gaining an electron rather than losing one — the same electronic-configuration ideas (distance, shielding) apply, but the conclusion is the opposite.
  • Talk about "energy levels" without linking them to distance and shielding. The mark scheme expects a cause-and-effect chain: more shells → outer electron further away and more shielded → weaker attraction → easier to lose.

6.2.1.1 Physical Properties in Group 7 F+H ▼

Physical Properties in Group 7

Spec 6.6: Recall the colours and physical states of chlorine, bromine and iodine at room temperature. Spec 6.7: Describe the pattern in the physical properties of the halogens, chlorine, bromine and iodine, and use this pattern to predict the physical properties of other halogens. Group 7 — the halogens — sits in the second-to-last column of the periodic table. The three halogens you need to recall for Edexcel GCSE are chlorine (Cl₂), bromine (Br₂) and iodine (I₂). Although fluorine (F₂) and astatine (At) are also Group 7, the syllabus only requires recall of colours and states for the middle three; fluorine and astatine appear as targets for the "predict" half of the spec point.

All halogens exist as diatomic molecules — two atoms covalently bonded together. This is a key feature: when you write a halogen in an equation, it is always Cl₂, Br₂ or I₂, never a single atom. The diatomic structure also explains why their physical properties trend so smoothly down the group: the only force you have to overcome to melt or boil them is the intermolecular force between separate molecules, not any covalent bond.

The pattern down the group (Spec 6.7 — describe and predict)

Going down Group 7 (from fluorine at the top to astatine at the bottom), three trends apply:

  1. Melting point increases down the group.
  2. Boiling point increases down the group.
  3. Colour gets darker down the group, and the state changes gas → liquid → solid.

The colour trend is straightforward to memorise: pale yellow fluorine, yellow-green chlorine, red-brown bromine, grey-black iodine, and dark/almost-black astatine.

Why melting and boiling points increase down the group

This is the WHY behind the spec point and it is examinable as a "Explain why…" question at Higher tier.

Each halogen molecule (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂) is held to its neighbours by weak intermolecular forces. To melt or boil a halogen you must put in enough energy to overcome these forces, not to break the strong covalent bond inside the diatomic molecule itself.

As you go down the group:

  • Each atom has more electron shells, so each molecule has more electrons overall.
  • Larger molecules with more electrons have stronger intermolecular forces between them.
  • Stronger intermolecular forces need more energy to overcome, so the melting and boiling points are higher.

That is why iodine (54 electrons per molecule) is a solid at room temperature, while chlorine (34 electrons per molecule) is a gas: the forces holding chlorine molecules together at room temperature are far too weak to keep them in a liquid or solid form.

A common student error is to say that the covalent bonds get stronger down the group — they do not. The covalent bond inside the molecule is irrelevant to the melting and boiling point; only the intermolecular force matters.

Predicting properties — using the pattern

The "predict" half of spec 6.7 is normally tested by giving you a table like the one above and asking you to estimate either:

  • A property of a halogen not shown (typically astatine, At — below iodine), or
  • A property of a fictional "next" halogen if the trend continued.

The standard approach is to look at the size of each jump in the data and continue that pattern.

Worked example — predicting the melting point of astatine

Use the melting points from the data table to predict the melting point of astatine (At₂).

Step 1 — write down the known melting points in order:

`` Fluorine: −220 °C Chlorine: −101 °C Bromine: −7 °C Iodine: +114 °C Astatine: ? ``

Step 2 — work out the jump between each consecutive pair:

`` F → Cl : −101 − (−220) = +119 °C Cl → Br : −7 − (−101) = +94 °C Br → I : +114 − (−7) = +121 °C ``

The jumps are 119, 94 and 121 °C — they are not identical, but they are all roughly 100 °C ± 25 °C.

Step 3 — apply a similar-sized jump to predict the next value:

A reasonable estimate is to add roughly +100 °C to iodine's value of 114 °C, giving an astatine melting point in the region of +210 to +240 °C. Any sensible value within that window (often quoted as around 200–300 °C) is acceptable in exam mark schemes, provided you can justify it from the trend.

Step 4 — sanity-check the state: a melting point of 210 °C is well above room temperature, so astatine is predicted to be a solid at room temperature — consistent with the gas → liquid → solid trend down the group.

This is precisely the kind of working an examiner wants to see: the answer is less important than the reasoning. If you only write "200 °C" with no working, you risk losing the method mark.

Mathematical Skills — interpolation and extrapolation (skills 1d and 2c)

This spec point is one of Edexcel's commonest hooks for testing Maths Skill 1d (use orders of magnitude) and Maths Skill 2c (extract and interpret information from charts, graphs and tables). The procedure for either skill is the same three steps:

```

  1. Read the data points you need from the table or graph.
  2. Identify whether the value you want is BETWEEN known points (interpolation)

or BEYOND them (extrapolation).

  1. Continue the pattern by using the size of the jumps between consecutive

points, then state your predicted value with units and the appropriate state at room temperature. ```

A worked interpolation example: if you were given boiling points for chlorine (−35 °C) and iodine (184 °C) but not bromine, you could interpolate bromine's boiling point as roughly halfway between them — around (−35 + 184)/2 ≈ +75 °C. The true value is 59 °C, so the halfway estimate is within ~20 °C — close enough for a "predict" mark, especially if you note that bromine sits closer to chlorine than to iodine in atomic number and so the value should be a little below the midpoint.

A worked extrapolation example would be the astatine prediction above: you are stepping beyond iodine, so you continue the jump pattern rather than averaging.

6.2.1.2 Testing Chlorine F+H ▼

Testing Chlorine

Spec point 6.8 — Describe the chemical test for chlorine. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 2 of both the Separate (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0) qualifications.

Chlorine is a pale yellow-green, toxic gas. A reliable laboratory test must give a clear, unambiguous positive result, must distinguish chlorine from other coloured or bleaching gases, and must be safe enough to perform on a small scale. The test you need for the GCSE specification uses damp blue litmus paper, which turns red and then is bleached white.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ChlorineA pale yellow-green diatomic gas (Cl₂) at room temperature; the element from Group 7
ChlorideThe negatively charged ion (Cl⁻) formed when chlorine gains an electron; found dissolved in solutions such as sodium chloride solution
Damp blue litmus paperA strip of blue litmus paper moistened with water; used as a chemical test for acidic or bleaching gases
BleachingThe chemical removal of colour, turning a coloured substance white or colourless

The test for chlorine

The full procedure expected at GCSE is:

  1. Hold a piece of damp blue litmus paper at the mouth of the test tube or container holding the unknown gas.
  2. Observe the paper.
  3. If the gas is chlorine, the blue litmus paper turns red first, then is bleached white.

Both observations are needed for full credit. The full answer to a "Describe the test" 2-mark question is therefore:

  • Mark 1: Use damp blue litmus paper (the apparatus and condition).
  • Mark 2: It is bleached / turns white (the positive result).

In some mark schemes the red-then-white sequence is accepted, but the key observation that distinguishes chlorine from other acidic gases is the bleaching — the paper ending up white.

Why damp litmus paper? Why both colour changes?

Chlorine is a slightly soluble gas. When chlorine touches the water film on the damp litmus paper, it reacts with the water to form a mixture of two acids:

Cl₂ (g) + H₂O (l) → HCl (aq) + HOCl (aq)

The two products do different jobs:

  • Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is a strong acid. It makes the surrounding solution acidic, so the blue litmus turns red. This is the same reaction that happens with any acidic gas.
  • Chloric(I) acid (HOCl), also called hypochlorous acid, is a powerful oxidising bleach. It removes the colour from the litmus dye, turning the paper white.

This explains why the test only works with damp paper. Dry blue litmus paper would not give the colour changes because chlorine cannot react without water present to form the two acids. If a student in an exam writes "blue litmus paper" without specifying damp, mark schemes routinely refuse the mark.

The "red then white" sequence often happens so quickly that students only see the final bleached paper. Examiners accept "turns white" or "is bleached" as the positive result — what they do not accept is "turns red" on its own, because several other acidic gases (HCl, SO₂, CO₂, NO₂) also turn damp blue litmus paper red. Bleaching is the step that singles out chlorine.

Chloride vs chlorine — the critical distinction

This is the single most common error in this part of the spec, and the Edexcel examiner report calls it out explicitly. Chlorine and chloride are different chemical species with different tests:

SpeciesSymbolState at room tempTestPositive result
Chlorine (the element)Cl₂Pale yellow-green gasDamp blue litmus paper held in the gasPaper is bleached white
Chloride (the ion)Cl⁻Dissolved in aqueous solutionAdd dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solutionWhite precipitate of silver chloride forms

Writing the wrong test scores zero. If the question asks for the test for chlorine and you describe the silver nitrate test for chloride ions, you have answered a completely different question. The same is true in reverse — describing damp litmus when asked for the test for chloride ions earns no marks.

A useful mental check before you write the answer:

  • Is the chemical a gas? It is most likely the element — use damp blue litmus paper.
  • Is the chemical a solution or dissolved? It is most likely the ion — use silver nitrate.

Distinguishing chlorine from other gases

Several gases on the GCSE specification can give partly overlapping results with chlorine. Knowing the differences keeps you out of trouble in multi-step "identify the gas" questions:

Safety and laboratory practice

Chlorine is toxic by inhalation and was historically used as a chemical weapon. In a school lab the test is always carried out:

  • In a fume cupboard, or with a very small amount of gas
  • With the test tube pointed away from the face
  • Using only a small piece of damp litmus paper held with tweezers or at the end of a glass rod

You will not be examined directly on this safety procedure for spec point 6.8, but understanding why only a small amount of damp litmus paper is held at the mouth of the tube (rather than dipped inside) helps you write a more authentic exam answer.

6.2.2.1 Reactions with Metals F+H ▼

Reactions with Metals

Spec point 6.9: Describe the reactions of the halogens, chlorine, bromine and iodine, with metals to form metal halides, and use this pattern to predict the reactions of other halogens. Foundation + Higher tier, Combined Science + Separate Chemistry, Paper 2.

The halogens — Group 7 of the periodic table — react with metals to form a family of ionic compounds called metal halides. The pattern is the same for every halogen: the metal hands over one or more electrons, the halogen accepts one electron per atom, and the resulting positive and negative ions stick together by ionic bonding. Once you know the pattern for chlorine, you can predict the reactions of fluorine, bromine, iodine and even astatine with the same metal.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
HalogenA non-metal element in Group 7 of the periodic table (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine)
Metal halideAn ionic compound formed when a halogen reacts with a metal; the halogen exists as a 1− halide ion
Halide ionThe negative ion (1−) formed when a halogen atom gains one electron, e.g. Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻
Diatomic moleculeA molecule made of two atoms of the same element joined by a covalent bond; halogens exist as Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂
SaltAn ionic compound containing a metal cation and a non-metal anion; metal halides are salts

The general pattern

Every halogen reacts with a reactive metal to produce a metal halide. In words, the general equation is:

`` metal + halogen → metal halide ``

The product is named by taking the metal first, then changing the halogen ending to -ide:

HalogenHalide endingExample product
Fluorine (F₂)fluoridesodium fluoride (NaF)
Chlorine (Cl₂)chloridesodium chloride (NaCl)
Bromine (Br₂)bromidesodium bromide (NaBr)
Iodine (I₂)iodidesodium iodide (NaI)
Astatine (At₂)astatidesodium astatide (NaAt)

The metal atom loses electrons to become a positive ion (cation), and each halogen atom gains one electron to become a 1− halide ion (anion). The oppositely charged ions are then held together by strong electrostatic forces — this is ionic bonding.

Reactions with Group 1 metals (alkali metals)

The alkali metals (Li, Na, K) react vigorously with the halogens. Sodium burning in chlorine gas is the classic demonstration: the sodium glows brightly with an orange-yellow flame and a thick white smoke of solid sodium chloride is produced.

*Word equation:*

`` sodium + chlorine → sodium chloride ``

*Balanced symbol equation:*

`` 2Na (s) + Cl₂ (g) → 2NaCl (s) ``

The same pattern works for any Group 1 metal with any halogen. You just balance the equation so the metal-to-halide ratio is 1:1 (because both ions carry a single charge):

  • 2Li (s) + Br₂ (l) → 2LiBr (s)
  • 2K (s) + I₂ (s) → 2KI (s)
  • 2Na (s) + F₂ (g) → 2NaF (s)

Why the 2:1 ratio? Each halogen molecule contains two atoms (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂). When the molecule reacts, both atoms each gain one electron and each end up as a 1− ion, so two metal atoms are needed for every one halogen molecule.

Reactions with Group 2 metals

Group 2 metals (Mg, Ca) also react with halogens, but they form 2+ ions, so the formula of the product has the metal-to-halide ratio of 1:2:

  • Mg (s) + Cl₂ (g) → MgCl₂ (s)
  • Ca (s) + Br₂ (l) → CaBr₂ (s)

Burning magnesium ribbon in a gas jar of chlorine produces white magnesium chloride.

Reactions with transition metals

Transition metals such as iron also react with halogens. Hot iron wool will glow and burn in chlorine to form iron(III) chloride, a brown solid:

`` 2Fe (s) + 3Cl₂ (g) → 2FeCl₃ (s) ``

The iron forms a 3+ ion in this reaction, so three chloride ions are needed for every one iron ion.

What you would observe

Whatever the metal, the visible signs of a halogen–metal reaction follow a recognisable pattern. You should be able to describe these observations for the Foundation paper:

ObservationWhat it tells you
Bright flame or glowAn exothermic reaction is taking place
Coloured smoke or fumesThe metal halide product is forming as a solid
White solid (often)Sodium and magnesium halides are typically white
Coloured solidIron(III) chloride is brown; copper(II) chloride is green/blue
Coloured gas/liquid disappearsThe halogen (Cl₂ green, Br₂ orange/brown) is being used up

The reactivity trend down the group

The halogens get less reactive as you go down Group 7. This means:

  • Fluorine reacts most vigorously with metals — often explosively, even with relatively unreactive metals
  • Chlorine reacts readily with hot or burning metals to give bright flames
  • Bromine reacts more slowly; the metal usually has to be heated strongly
  • Iodine reacts only sluggishly, often needing strong heating, and the reaction is much less vigorous

So when you compare the same metal — say sodium — reacting with each halogen in turn, you would see the brightest, most vigorous reaction with fluorine and the dullest, slowest reaction with iodine.

This trend lets you predict reactions you haven't seen. If the spec asks you to predict the reaction of astatine (At₂, below iodine) with a metal, you should expect it to be even less reactive than iodine — the reaction would be very slow or barely happen, but the product would still be a metal astatide with the same formula pattern (e.g. 2Na + At₂ → 2NaAt).

Why the trend exists

A short mention is enough at this level (the full explanation belongs in 6.2.4.1 Group 7: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations):

  • A halogen reacts by gaining one electron to complete its outer shell
  • Going down the group, atoms get bigger and the outer shell is further from the nucleus
  • The attraction between the nucleus and an incoming electron weakens
  • The outer shell is also shielded by more inner electron shells
  • So heavier halogens find it harder to attract that extra electron — they are less reactive

Predicting reactions of other halogens

The spec specifically asks you to use the pattern to predict reactions of halogens you have not been shown. The recipe is:

  1. Write the word equation: metal + halogen → metal halide
  2. Work out the charge on the metal ion (Group 1 = 1+, Group 2 = 2+, aluminium = 3+)
  3. The halide ion is always 1−
  4. Balance the formula of the product so total positive charge = total negative charge
  5. Balance the equation, remembering halogens are diatomic (X₂)

*Worked example — predict the reaction of potassium with bromine*

  1. Word equation: potassium + bromine → potassium bromide
  2. Potassium is in Group 1, so K⁺
  3. Bromine forms Br⁻
  4. Product formula: KBr (charges balance: +1 and −1)
  5. Balanced equation: 2K (s) + Br₂ (l) → 2KBr (s)

Observations to predict: potassium would burn vigorously with a lilac flame; an orange-brown bromine vapour would be used up; a white solid (potassium bromide) would form on the inside of the apparatus. The reaction would be less vigorous than potassium with chlorine, because bromine is below chlorine in the group.

*Worked example — predict the reaction of calcium with iodine*

  1. Word equation: calcium + iodine → calcium iodide
  2. Ca²⁺
  3. I⁻
  4. Product formula: CaI₂ (need two I⁻ to balance one Ca²⁺)
  5. Balanced equation: Ca (s) + I₂ (s) → CaI₂ (s)

Observations to predict: the reaction would be slow and would need strong heating; the purple-black iodine solid (and purple iodine vapour on warming) would be used up; a white solid (calcium iodide) would form. This reaction is much less vigorous than calcium with chlorine.

6.2.2.2 Hydrogen Halides F+H ▼

Hydrogen Halides

Spec 6.10: Recall that the halogens, chlorine, bromine and iodine, form hydrogen halides which dissolve in water to form acidic solutions, and use this pattern to predict the reactions of other halogens. Foundation + Higher tier · 1CH0 + 1SC0 · Paper 2.

The halogens in Group 7 all react with hydrogen to form a family of compounds called hydrogen halides. When these gases are bubbled into water they dissolve to give acidic solutions. The pattern is so consistent across the group that you can use it to predict the behaviour of halogens you have not been told about directly — exactly what the spec asks you to do.

Key Definitions

Formation of the Hydrogen Halides

When a halogen reacts with hydrogen, a single covalent bond forms between one hydrogen atom and one halogen atom. Each reactant supplies one atom per molecule, so the equations always show diatomic reactants and a diatomic product:

  • H₂ (g) + Cl₂ (g) → 2HCl (g)
  • H₂ (g) + Br₂ (g) → 2HBr (g)
  • H₂ (g) + I₂ (g) → 2HI (g)

Why every species in these equations is diatomic. Hydrogen exists as H₂, and chlorine, bromine and iodine all exist as Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂ at the temperatures you meet at GCSE. None of these elements exist as single atoms in normal conditions because the unpaired outer electron on each atom is unstable on its own — pairing up into a diatomic molecule completes a shared pair and lowers the energy. The hydrogen halide product (HCl, HBr or HI) is also diatomic because it contains one H atom covalently bonded to one halogen atom. Because the reactants come as pairs of atoms, two product molecules are formed per reaction (hence the 2 in front of HCl, HBr or HI).

The reactivity trend. The reactions become less vigorous as you move down Group 7. Chlorine reacts rapidly with hydrogen — in the presence of sunlight the reaction is explosive. Bromine reacts more slowly and needs a hot catalyst. Iodine reacts only partially, even at high temperatures, and the reaction is reversible. This mirrors the general Group 7 trend: reactivity decreases down the group because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so it is harder for a halogen atom to attract an electron.

Dissolving in Water — Acidic Solutions

All hydrogen halides are colourless gases at room temperature. When bubbled into water they dissolve readily and ionise to give H⁺ (aq) ions, which is what makes the solution acidic:

  • HCl (g) + water → H⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) (hydrochloric acid)
  • HBr (g) + water → H⁺ (aq) + Br⁻ (aq) (hydrobromic acid)
  • HI (g) + water → H⁺ (aq) + I⁻ (aq) (hydroiodic acid)

The presence of H⁺ (aq) ions is the definition of an acidic solution, so each of these has a pH below 7 and turns blue litmus red. They are all strong acids — fully ionised in water.

Naming the Acid Formed When a Hydrogen Halide Dissolves

There is a simple naming pattern: take the halide stem (chlor-, brom-, iod-) and combine it with "hydro-" and "-ic acid".

HalogenHydrogen halideFormulaName of acidic solution in water
ChlorineHydrogen chlorideHClHydrochloric acid
BromineHydrogen bromideHBrHydrobromic acid
IodineHydrogen iodideHIHydroiodic acid

A common error to avoid. The acid is named after the halide ion, not after a hydroxide. Hydrogen bromide in water is hydrobromic acid — it is not "bromine hydroxide". Hydroxide (OH⁻) is the alkaline ion in compounds like sodium hydroxide; it does not appear when a hydrogen halide dissolves. The species in solution are H⁺ and the halide ion (Cl⁻, Br⁻ or I⁻), so the acid name always ends in -ic acid with a "hydro-" prefix.

A quick way to remember the pattern: "hydro-" tells you there is a hydrogen, the middle ("chlor-", "brom-", "iod-") tells you which halogen, and "-ic acid" tells you it is an acid. Match the middle of the name to the middle of the halogen and you will not go wrong.

Predicting the Behaviour of Other Halogens

The spec specifically wants you to use this pattern to predict what other halogens will do. The two halogens you may be asked about are fluorine (above chlorine) and astatine (below iodine).

Fluorine. Following the trend that reactivity decreases down the group, fluorine — which sits above chlorine — must be more reactive than chlorine. It will react explosively with hydrogen even in the dark and at low temperatures:

  • H₂ (g) + F₂ (g) → 2HF (g)

Hydrogen fluoride dissolves in water to form hydrofluoric acid (HF (aq)), an acidic solution. You apply the same naming pattern: hydro + fluor + -ic acid.

Astatine. Astatine sits below iodine and is therefore predicted to be less reactive than iodine. The reaction between hydrogen and astatine would be very slow and incomplete; hydrogen astatide (HAt) would form only in small amounts. When dissolved in water it would form hydroastatic acid, again an acidic solution.

You will not be expected to recall properties of fluorine or astatine from memory; the spec wording is "use this pattern to predict", so the marks come from applying the trend you already know.

Writing Balanced Equations — Watch the Diatomic Elements

When you write equations for these reactions, you must show hydrogen and the halogen as diatomic molecules. Writing "H + Cl → HCl" is wrong on two counts: it shows monatomic reactants (hydrogen and chlorine never exist as single atoms in these reactions), and it is not balanced once you fix the diatomic issue.

The correct working is:

  1. Identify the reactants: hydrogen gas (H₂) and chlorine gas (Cl₂) — both diatomic.
  2. Write the product: hydrogen chloride (HCl).
  3. Balance: there are 2 H atoms and 2 Cl atoms on the left, so you need 2HCl on the right.

Final equation: H₂ (g) + Cl₂ (g) → 2HCl (g)

The same logic applies to HBr and HI. If you ever find yourself writing a halogen as a single atom in a Group 7 equation, stop and re-balance from the diatomic formula.

6.2.2.3 Displacement Reactions F+H ▼

Displacement Reactions

Spec point 6.11: Describe the relative reactivity of the halogens chlorine, bromine and iodine, as shown by their displacement reactions with halide ions in aqueous solution, and use this pattern to predict the reactions of astatine. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 2.

A displacement reaction between halogens and halide ions is the central piece of evidence Edexcel uses to establish the reactivity trend in Group 7. The rule is simple and worth committing to memory before you go any further: a more reactive halogen will displace a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide. Reactivity in Group 7 decreases going down the group, so chlorine (Cl₂) is the most reactive of the three named halogens, bromine (Br₂) sits in the middle, and iodine (I₂) is the least reactive.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Displacement reactionA reaction in which a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in a compound
HalogenA Group 7 element existing as a diatomic molecule (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂); a non-metal that gains one electron to form a 1– ion
Halide ionThe 1– ion formed when a halogen gains an electron (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻); usually encountered in aqueous solution as part of a soluble metal halide such as potassium chloride
Aqueous halide solutionA solution of a soluble halide salt — typically potassium chloride, potassium bromide or potassium iodide — used as the source of halide ions in the test

Why a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one

A halogen atom needs to gain one electron to complete its outer shell and form a stable 1– ion. The easier this is, the more reactive the halogen. As you go down Group 7, atoms get larger because each element has an additional electron shell. The outer shell is therefore further from the nucleus and is more shielded by the inner electrons. Both of these effects weaken the attraction the nucleus has for an incoming electron, so the halogen finds it harder to gain that electron. That is why chlorine (small, top of the group) is more reactive than iodine (large, lower down).

When chlorine is added to a solution of potassium bromide, chlorine is the "stronger" electron-grabber. It pulls the electrons away from the bromide ions, becoming chloride ions itself. The bromide ions, having lost their electrons, become bromine molecules (Br₂) which colour the solution orange. The potassium ions take no part in the reaction — they are spectator ions — which is why students who write that "potassium displaced the halogen" are scoring zero credit on this idea (more on that below).

This table is the single most important thing to learn for this spec point. Each row lists a halogen added to three different halide solutions; "✓" means a reaction occurs, "✗" means no reaction occurs because the halogen is already less reactive than the halide.

A few practical points students often miss:

  • The starting halide solutions (KCl, KBr, KI) are all colourless before the halogen is added. The colour you see at the end comes from the halogen that has been displaced, not from the halide.
  • Chlorine water itself is pale green or pale yellow, bromine water is orange, and iodine solution (or iodine in potassium iodide) is brown.
  • A "no reaction" looks like nothing happening or the added halogen's own colour persisting; for example, adding orange bromine to colourless KCl just gives an orange solution because the bromine has not reacted with anything.
  • The pattern of ✓s sits along and above the diagonal: chlorine reacts with two solutions, bromine with one, iodine with none. The triangular shape is your evidence for the reactivity order Cl > Br > I.

The equations

You should be confident writing the balanced symbol equations for each displacement. Foundation candidates can usually get full credit by writing either the full equation or the ionic equation; Higher tier candidates should be ready to write the ionic version.

Chlorine displaces bromide:

Cl₂ (aq) + 2KBr (aq) → 2KCl (aq) + Br₂ (aq)

Ionic equation: Cl₂ (aq) + 2Br⁻ (aq) → 2Cl⁻ (aq) + Br₂ (aq)

Chlorine displaces iodide:

Cl₂ (aq) + 2KI (aq) → 2KCl (aq) + I₂ (aq)

Ionic equation: Cl₂ (aq) + 2I⁻ (aq) → 2Cl⁻ (aq) + I₂ (aq)

Bromine displaces iodide:

Br₂ (aq) + 2KI (aq) → 2KBr (aq) + I₂ (aq)

State symbols matter here: every species is dissolved in water, so all four are (aq). Note the space between the formula and the state symbol in line with Edexcel's preferred presentation.

Predicting reactions of astatine

Astatine (At) sits below iodine in Group 7. Its atoms are even larger than those of iodine and the outer shell is even more shielded from the nucleus, so astatine is predicted to be less reactive than iodine. From this prediction you can deduce three things you can quote in an exam:

  1. Astatine will not displace chloride, bromide or iodide from any of their potassium halide solutions, because it is less reactive than chlorine, bromine and iodine.
  2. The reverse is true: chlorine, bromine and iodine would all displace astatide ions (At⁻) from a solution of potassium astatide. For example: Cl₂ (aq) + 2KAt (aq) → 2KCl (aq) + At₂ (aq).
  3. Because the trend in physical properties also continues — atoms get larger and intermolecular forces get stronger going down the group — astatine is predicted to be a solid at room temperature and to be even darker than iodine. (You are not asked to give a specific colour; "dark solid" or "black solid" is the safe wording.)

If you are asked to "predict the reaction of astatine" in an exam, structure your answer as: state the position in the group, state that reactivity decreases down the group, state the consequence (less reactive than iodine), then give an example reaction it would or would not undergo. That structure scores far better than a single sentence.

6.2.3.1 Halogen Displacement & Redox H only ▼

Halogen Displacement & Redox

Spec point 6.12 (Higher tier only): Explain why these displacement reactions are redox reactions in terms of gain and loss of electrons, identifying which of these are oxidised and which are reduced.

This Higher-tier-only spec point takes the displacement observations from 6.2.2.3 and reframes them as electron-transfer (redox) reactions. You need to be able to write ionic half-equations, identify which species is oxidised and which is reduced, and explain the link to halogen reactivity.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
OxidationThe loss of electrons by an atom, molecule or ion
ReductionThe gain of electrons by an atom, molecule or ion
Redox reactionA reaction in which oxidation and reduction occur together; electrons are transferred from one species to another
Oxidising agentA species that gains electrons (and so is itself reduced) — it oxidises something else
Reducing agentA species that loses electrons (and so is itself oxidised) — it reduces something else
Half-equationAn ionic equation that shows only the species gaining or only the species losing electrons, with electrons (e⁻) written explicitly
Displacement reactionA reaction in which a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in one of its compounds
Spectator ionAn ion that is present in solution but does not take part in the reaction (it appears unchanged on both sides of the full ionic equation)

The mnemonic — OIL RIG

The standard memory aid for redox is:

  • Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons)
  • Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

Use this for every halogen-displacement question — once you have decided which species lost electrons and which gained them, the labels follow directly.

Why halogen displacement is a redox reaction

When a more reactive halogen is added to a solution of a less reactive halide ion, electrons are transferred. The free halogen molecule pulls electrons off the halide ion in solution, becoming a halide ion itself, while the original halide ion loses its extra electron and becomes a neutral halogen molecule.

Two things happen at the same time — one species gains electrons (reduction) and one species loses electrons (oxidation) — so the overall reaction is redox.

A halogen atom has 7 electrons in its outer shell. To complete its outer shell it only needs to gain one electron, forming a –1 ion. The halide ion (X⁻) is the reverse situation: it has the extra electron, and a more reactive halogen can take that electron away from it. This is why halogen displacement is always an electron-transfer reaction, never just a swap of names on paper.

Working through Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂

The full molecular equation is:

Cl₂ (aq) + 2KBr (aq) → 2KCl (aq) + Br₂ (aq)

Strip out the spectator ions — the K⁺ ions are unchanged on both sides — and you are left with the ionic equation:

Cl₂ (aq) + 2Br⁻ (aq) → 2Cl⁻ (aq) + Br₂ (aq)

Now split this into two half-equations, one for each species that changes:

*Reduction half-equation (chlorine gains electrons):*

Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻

*Oxidation half-equation (bromide loses electrons):*

2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻

*Identifying the species:*

  • Cl₂ is reduced — each Cl atom in Cl₂ gains one electron to become Cl⁻ (gain of electrons = reduction). Cl₂ is therefore the oxidising agent.
  • Br⁻ is oxidised — each Br⁻ loses one electron to become a neutral Br atom, and two Br atoms then form Br₂ (loss of electrons = oxidation). Br⁻ is therefore the reducing agent.

The number of electrons lost by bromide (2e⁻ total) equals the number of electrons gained by chlorine (2e⁻ total) — electrons must balance in any redox equation.

Working through Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂

The ionic equation (after removing K⁺ spectator ions):

Cl₂ (aq) + 2I⁻ (aq) → 2Cl⁻ (aq) + I₂ (aq)

Reduction: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ (Cl₂ is reduced; it is the oxidising agent)

Oxidation: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ (I⁻ is oxidised; it is the reducing agent)

Working through Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂

The ionic equation:

Br₂ (aq) + 2I⁻ (aq) → 2Br⁻ (aq) + I₂ (aq)

Reduction: Br₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Br⁻ (Br₂ is reduced; it is the oxidising agent)

Oxidation: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ (I⁻ is oxidised; it is the reducing agent)

Reaction (ionic)Species reduced (gains e⁻)Species oxidised (loses e⁻)Oxidising agentReducing agent

Notice the pattern: the free halogen (the one starting as X₂) is always reduced and is always the oxidising agent. The halide ion (X⁻) is always oxidised and is always the reducing agent. The free halogen is the more reactive of the two — it is reactive precisely because it is good at pulling electrons off something else.

Why this links to reactivity down Group 7

Reactivity decreases down Group 7 because each successive halogen atom has its outer shell further from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons — so its ability to attract an extra electron (i.e. to be reduced) decreases. Chlorine sits highest in the group of the three you meet, so it most strongly pulls electrons from Br⁻ or I⁻. Iodine is least able to do this, so iodine cannot displace bromide or chloride.

This is why redox language and reactivity language are saying the same thing in different words:

  • "More reactive halogen" = "better oxidising agent" = "more readily gains an electron".
  • "Less reactive halide" = "harder to oxidise" — but the halide of a less reactive halogen (e.g. I⁻) is more easily oxidised, because the outer electron is held less tightly and is more easily given up.

Writing redox answers in the exam — common structure

For a typical 2-mark "Explain why this is a redox reaction" question, examiners are looking for both halves of the electron-transfer story:

  1. State which species loses electrons (oxidation) — give the symbol or name.
  2. State which species gains electrons (reduction) — give the symbol or name.

A model answer for Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ would be:

> Bromide ions (Br⁻) lose electrons to form Br₂, so bromide is oxidised. Chlorine molecules (Cl₂) gain electrons to form Cl⁻, so chlorine is reduced. Because oxidation and reduction occur together, the reaction is a redox reaction.

For a 3–4 mark version, add the half-equations and identify the oxidising and reducing agents.

Common student errors

  • Confusing direction of electron flow. Halide ions (X⁻) have the extra electron — they give it up. Free halogens (X₂) take electrons. A common slip is to say "chlorine loses electrons to bromine" — the opposite of what happens.
  • Mixing up the agent labels. The species that is oxidised is the reducing agent, not the oxidising agent. Many students name them the wrong way round because the word "oxidised" sounds like it should mean "oxidising agent". Use OIL RIG to fix the half first, then label the agent as the opposite.
  • Forgetting to balance electrons. In every half-equation, electrons in must equal electrons out across the two halves. Cl₂ gains 2 electrons; this means 2 Br⁻ must lose 2 electrons (not one Br⁻).
  • Treating the potassium as reactive. K⁺ is a spectator ion — it is unchanged on both sides and is not involved in the redox process. In the June 2019 Higher paper, examiners flagged that some candidates wrote about "potassium displacing the halogen," which is a fundamental misreading of what is being displaced. The reactive species are the halogens and halide ions, not the metal.
  • Describing colour changes only. "The solution went orange so bromine was made" describes the observation but does not explain the redox — you also need to say which species lost electrons and which gained them.

6.2.4.1 Group 7: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations F+H ▼

Group 7: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations

Spec point 6.13: Explain the relative reactivity of the halogens in terms of electronic configurations. Examined at both Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 2 for Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

Group 7, the halogens, is the most reactive non-metal group in the periodic table. The crucial pattern you need to be able to explain is that reactivity decreases as you go down the group — fluorine is the most reactive, then chlorine, bromine, iodine, and astatine is the least reactive of the elements you meet at GCSE. This is the opposite of Group 1, where reactivity increases down the group, and the contrast is one of the most heavily tested ideas in Section 6.

The reason the trend goes in opposite directions for the two groups comes down to one simple fact: Group 1 metals lose an electron when they react, but Group 7 non-metals gain an electron. Once you have that, the rest of the reasoning falls into place.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
HalogenAn element in Group 7 of the periodic table; halogens have 7 electrons in their outer shell
ReactivityA measure of how readily an element undergoes chemical reactions
Electronic configurationThe arrangement of electrons in shells around the nucleus of an atom
Outer shellThe shell furthest from the nucleus that contains electrons; also called the valence shell
ShieldingThe repulsion of outer-shell electrons by inner-shell electrons, which reduces the effective pull from the nucleus
Halide ionThe 1− ion formed when a halogen atom gains one electron (e.g. F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻)
Nuclear attractionThe electrostatic pull of the positively charged nucleus on negatively charged electrons

Electronic configurations of the halogens

Every halogen has 7 electrons in its outer shell. That single fact controls everything they do chemically. The configurations you need are:

HalogenSymbolAtomic numberElectronic configurationNumber of shells
FluorineF92,72
ChlorineCl172,8,73
BromineBr352,8,18,74
IodineI532,8,18,18,75

Notice two things from the table. First, the last digit is always 7 — that is what puts these elements in Group 7. Second, the number of shells increases by one each time you go down the group. Both of these features are essential when explaining the reactivity trend.

Why halogens react: gaining one electron

Atoms are most stable when they have a full outer shell, like the noble gases in Group 0. A halogen atom has 7 outer-shell electrons and is just one electron short of a full shell. The easiest way for a halogen to reach a full outer shell is to gain one electron, forming a 1− ion (a halide ion):

X + e⁻ → X⁻

For example:

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (electronic configuration 2,8,8 — same as argon)

This is why halogens are powerful oxidising agents — they take electrons from other species. When a halogen reacts with a metal it forms an ionic halide; when it reacts with another non-metal it forms a covalent compound by sharing electrons. Either way, the driving force is the same: completing the outer shell.

The reactivity of a halogen is essentially a measure of how strongly it pulls in an extra electron. Whichever halogen attracts the incoming electron most strongly is the most reactive, and that depends on the size of the atom and the position of the outer shell.

The reactivity trend: why it decreases down the group

As you go down Group 7, three changes happen at the same time:

  1. The number of occupied shells increases, so the outer shell is further from the nucleus.
  2. Shielding increases because there are more full inner shells of electrons between the nucleus and the outer shell.
  3. The nuclear charge also increases (more protons), but the effect of extra shells and extra shielding outweighs this increase.

The combined effect of (1) and (2) is that the attraction between the nucleus and an incoming electron becomes weaker as you go down the group. An incoming electron joining the outer shell of iodine is much further from the nucleus than one joining the outer shell of fluorine, and it is more shielded by inner electrons. So iodine grabs electrons less strongly than fluorine — iodine is the less reactive halogen.

In plain terms: the further out the outer shell is, the harder it is for the nucleus to pull in another electron, and the less reactive the halogen.

Comparison: Group 1 versus Group 7

This is the comparison Edexcel love to test. Although the direction of the reactivity trend is opposite for the two groups, the reasoning is built from the same three ideas (distance from nucleus, shielding, and electron transfer). The trick is that Group 1 atoms lose an electron while Group 7 atoms gain one — and that flips the trend.

FeatureGroup 1 (alkali metals)Group 7 (halogens)

The common student-error trap here is to invert the Group 7 reasoning and write something like "reactivity increases down Group 7 because shielding increases", treating halogens as if they were like Group 1 metals. Always remember: if the element loses an electron, more shielding helps (Group 1, reactivity up). If the element gains an electron, more shielding hurts (Group 7, reactivity down).

Worked example: predicting the most reactive halogen

Question: Using ideas about electronic configuration, explain which of fluorine, chlorine, and bromine is the most reactive.

*Step 1 — State the configurations:*

  • Fluorine: 2,7 (2 shells)
  • Chlorine: 2,8,7 (3 shells)
  • Bromine: 2,8,18,7 (4 shells)

Step 2 — State what halogens do in reactions: Halogens react by gaining one electron into their outer shell to complete it (forming a 1− ion).

Step 3 — Compare the atoms: Fluorine has the fewest shells, so its outer shell is closest to the nucleus. There are also fewer inner-shell electrons to shield the incoming electron. Bromine has the most shells, so its outer shell is furthest from the nucleus and is most shielded.

Step 4 — Link to reactivity: The closer the outer shell is to the nucleus, and the less shielding there is, the stronger the attraction for an incoming electron. So fluorine attracts an electron most strongly.

Conclusion: Fluorine is the most reactive of the three because its outer shell is closest to the nucleus and least shielded, so it most strongly attracts an extra electron to complete its outer shell.

Common student-error traps

  • Inverting the trend. Writing "reactivity increases down Group 7" — usually because the student has memorised the Group 1 pattern and not flipped it. Always anchor your reasoning to whether the element gains or loses an electron.
  • Saying halogens lose an electron. Halogens gain one electron because they already have 7 outer electrons. Only metals (Groups 1, 2, and 3) lose electrons easily.
  • Forgetting to mention shielding and distance. Top-band answers need both — the outer shell being further from the nucleus, and more inner-shell electrons getting in the way.
  • Talking about "extra protons" without resolving the conflict. Yes, nuclear charge increases down the group, but the effect of extra shells and shielding outweighs it. You can mention this, but make clear which effect wins.
  • Saying "the atom is bigger so it is less reactive" with no reasoning. Size on its own is not an explanation — you need to link size to nuclear attraction on the incoming electron.
  • Confusing reactivity with melting/boiling point. The physical-property trend (boiling point increases down the group) is separate from the chemical reactivity trend (reactivity decreases). Don't blur them.

6.3.1.1 Group 0 (Noble Gases) F+H ▼

Group 0 (Noble Gases)

Spec point 6.14 — Explain why the noble gases are chemically inert, compared with the other elements, in terms of their electronic configurations. Foundation + Higher tier · Paper 2.

Group 0 (sometimes called Group 8 or Group 18 in older textbooks) sits on the far right of the periodic table. It contains helium (He), neon (Ne), argon (Ar), krypton (Kr), xenon (Xe) and radon (Rn). The defining feature of this group — and the only feature Edexcel asks you to explain at GCSE — is that these elements are chemically inert: they essentially do not react with anything under normal conditions. The reason is electronic, and the explanation always comes back to the same idea: a full outer shell of electrons.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Noble gasAn element in Group 0 of the periodic table that has a full outer shell of electrons and is therefore chemically inert
InertChemically unreactive; does not readily form chemical bonds with other atoms
Electronic configurationThe arrangement of electrons in the shells (energy levels) around the nucleus of an atom
Outer shellThe highest-energy occupied electron shell in an atom; the electrons in this shell determine the atom's chemical behaviour
Full outer shellAn outer shell holding the maximum number of electrons it can — 2 for the first shell, 8 for the second and third shells at GCSE level
Stable electronic configurationAn arrangement of electrons (typically a full outer shell) that gives the atom very low energy and very little tendency to gain, lose or share electrons

Why noble gases are inert — the underlying reason

Chemical reactions are essentially changes in how electrons are arranged. Atoms react because they can reach a lower-energy, more stable arrangement by either:

  • losing electrons (metals — e.g. sodium loses 1 electron to become Na⁺)
  • gaining electrons (non-metals — e.g. chlorine gains 1 electron to become Cl⁻)
  • sharing electrons (covalent bonding — e.g. two hydrogens share their electrons in H₂)

In every case, the "target" arrangement that atoms are driving towards is the electronic configuration of the nearest noble gas — a full outer shell.

Noble gases are inert because they already have what every other element is trying to achieve. There is no energetic incentive for a noble gas atom to lose, gain or share electrons, so it does not react. This is the single idea Edexcel wants you to be able to express clearly.

The electronic configurations of the noble gases

The first three noble gases are the ones you must be able to write out. At GCSE you only need to fill shells in the order 2, 8, 8 — that is, up to 2 electrons in the first shell and up to 8 in the second and third shells.

Noble gasAtomic numberElectronic configurationOuter shellFull?

Argon (Ar)182, 8, 83rd shell (max 8 at GCSE)Yes

You are not required to write out the electronic configuration of krypton, xenon or radon at GCSE — but you should know that every noble gas has a full outer shell of electrons.

Helium is the special case. Helium has only 2 electrons total, both in the first shell. The first shell can only ever hold 2 electrons, so helium's outer shell is full at 2 — not 8. This is why helium is still a noble gas even though it only has 2 outer electrons: "full outer shell" is what matters, not "eight outer electrons specifically". A common Foundation-tier mistake is to insist a noble gas must have 8 outer electrons; helium proves otherwise.

Neon and argon both have 8 outer electrons. Neon completes its second shell at 8 electrons; argon completes its third shell at 8 electrons (at GCSE level, where d-electrons are ignored). This is where the phrase "stable octet" comes from — an octet meaning eight.

Linking electronic configuration to inertness

Once you have written the electronic configuration, the explanation flows in three sentences:

  1. The noble gas has a full outer shell of electrons.
  2. This is a stable electronic configuration, so the atom has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons.
  3. Because chemical reactions involve transferring or sharing electrons, the noble gas does not react — it is inert.

For a 2-mark "Explain, in terms of electrons, why these gases are inert" question (the wording used in 1CH0/2H 2024 Q7(c)(i)), the two marks are almost always:

  • MP1: full outer shell (of electrons)
  • MP2: so does not need to gain / lose / share electrons (so does not react)

You will get the mark for "stable arrangement" or "stable electronic configuration" in place of MP1 wording in most schemes, but the safest answer states full outer shell explicitly.

Comparing noble gases with other elements

The clearest way to see why noble gases are inert is to compare them with their nearest neighbours in the periodic table — Group 1 metals (one electron away on one side) and Group 7 non-metals (one electron away on the other side).

ElementGroupElectronic configurationOuter shellWhat it does
Sodium (Na)12, 8, 11 electronLoses 1 electron → Na⁺ (becomes 2, 8 — like neon)
Neon (Ne)02, 8Full (8)Does nothing — already stable
Fluorine (F)72, 77 electronsGains 1 electron → F⁻ (becomes 2, 8 — like neon)

Sodium reacts vigorously because losing one electron drops it into the same configuration as neon. Fluorine reacts vigorously because gaining one electron lifts it into the same configuration as neon. Neon itself is already there — so it has no reason to react. The same pattern repeats for argon (Group 1 potassium loses one electron to reach 2, 8, 8; Group 7 chlorine gains one to reach 2, 8, 8).

This comparison also explains why noble gases sit where they do in the periodic table: they are placed in Group 0 precisely because they have a full outer shell — and that group position is then a useful visual reminder of their inertness.

Why "Group 0" and not "Group 8"?

Edexcel uses Group 0 rather than "Group 8" in the GCSE specification. The "0" reflects the fact that noble gases have zero electrons available for bonding, because their outer shell is already full. Group number at GCSE generally tells you how many outer electrons an atom has and therefore how many bonds it forms — Group 1 forms 1 bond, Group 7 forms 1 bond (gaining an electron), and Group 0 forms 0 bonds. This is a useful piece of context but the spec point itself only requires you to explain inertness via electronic configuration; you do not need to discuss the naming convention in an answer.

Worked example — explaining argon's inertness

> "Explain why argon does not react with most other elements." (2 marks)

Step 1 — write the configuration. Argon has atomic number 18, so its electronic configuration is 2, 8, 8.

Step 2 — identify the outer shell. The outer (third) shell holds 8 electrons, which is the maximum at GCSE level — so the outer shell is full.

Step 3 — link to reactivity. Because argon has a full outer shell, it has a stable electronic configuration and does not need to lose, gain or share electrons. Reactions require electron transfer or sharing, so argon does not react.

*Model 2-mark answer:** *"Argon has a full outer shell of electrons (2, 8, 8). This is a stable electronic configuration, so it does not need to gain, lose or share electrons, and therefore does not react."

Worked example — explaining helium's inertness

> "Helium has only 2 electrons. Explain why it is still classed as a noble gas." (2 marks)

Step 1 — write the configuration. Helium has atomic number 2, so its electronic configuration is just 2.

Step 2 — identify the outer shell. Both electrons are in the first shell, which can only hold a maximum of 2 electrons — so the first (and only) shell is full.

Step 3 — link to reactivity. Helium has a full outer shell (even though that shell only holds 2 electrons), so it has a stable electronic configuration and does not react.

This is worth practising because the "but helium only has 2" objection is one of the most common Foundation-tier traps in this topic — students learn "8 outer electrons = stable" and then cannot account for helium.

Using Noble Gases

Spec point 6.15 — Explain how the uses of noble gases depend on their inertness, low density and/or non-flammability. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier, Separate Chemistry and Combined Science, Paper 2.

The noble gases (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon) sit in Group 0 of the periodic table. They share a single chemical feature — a full outer electron shell — and this leads to the family property that drives almost every real-world use: they are chemically inert (extremely unreactive). On top of inertness, helium has an unusually low density and all noble gases are non-flammable. Almost every exam question on this spec point asks you to link a specific property to a specific use, so the skill is matching property to application and being able to write a one- or two-sentence explanation.

Why noble gases are inert

The noble gases all have a full outer shell of electrons (helium has 2, the others have 8). A full outer shell is an electronically stable arrangement, so a noble gas atom has no tendency to gain, lose or share electrons. Because chemical reactions involve the transfer or sharing of electrons in the outer shell, an atom that will not give up, accept or share its outer electrons effectively cannot react. That single fact — full outer shell → no electron transfer → no reaction — explains every "inertness" use you will meet in the exam.

This is what makes noble gases so useful where you need a gas that will not react with hot metals, will not support combustion, and will not contaminate a sensitive sample. The same property also means noble gases are non-flammable: burning is a chemical reaction with oxygen, and inert gases will not undergo that reaction.

The two examples you are most likely to be asked to explain are helium in balloons and argon in filament bulbs, because they each map cleanly onto two of the three named properties.

Helium in balloons and airships — worked explanation

If a question asks "Explain why helium is used to fill weather balloons rather than hydrogen" (2 marks), you need to give one mark for a property and one mark for a consequence.

  • Helium has a lower density than air, so a balloon filled with helium rises (lift).
  • Helium is non-flammable / does not catch fire, unlike hydrogen, which is highly flammable and historically caused disasters such as the Hindenburg.

Both hydrogen and helium have lower densities than air, so density alone does not distinguish them. The deciding factor is safety — non-flammability — which is why helium replaced hydrogen for filling passenger airships and weather balloons. A good answer pairs the property with what it means in practice ("won't react / won't catch fire so it is safe").

Argon in filament light bulbs — worked explanation

A traditional filament bulb contains a thin tungsten wire that becomes white-hot when current flows through it. If the bulb were filled with air, the oxygen would react with the hot tungsten, oxidising the filament and breaking it within seconds. To prevent this, the bulb is filled with argon.

  • Argon is inert / unreactive, so it does not react with the hot tungsten filament.
  • Argon is non-flammable, so it will not burn or support combustion inside the bulb.

The filament therefore lasts much longer than it would in air. You could in principle use any noble gas, but argon is chosen because it is far cheaper than neon, krypton or xenon — it makes up nearly 1% of the atmosphere and is extracted in bulk from liquefied air.

Argon as a welding shield gas

In arc welding, an electric arc melts metal so it can be joined. At those temperatures, oxygen and nitrogen from the air would react with the molten metal, weakening the weld. A stream of argon is directed over the weld area to push the air away. Because argon is inert, it does not react with the molten metal; because it is denser than air, it sits over the weld and stays there. This is another inertness-driven use.

Neon in advertising signs

A "neon" sign is a sealed glass tube containing neon gas at low pressure with electrodes at each end. When a high voltage is applied, electrons collide with neon atoms and excite their electrons; as the electrons fall back, they emit a characteristic red-orange light. The use depends on two things:

  • Inertness — the neon is not consumed by the reaction, so the sign lasts a very long time. The gas is not chemically changed by the discharge.
  • The emission spectrum of neon, which produces the distinctive colour.

Other "neon" colours (blue, green, white) are actually produced by other gases — for example, argon, krypton or xenon — or by coloured glass tubes. The same inertness argument applies to each.

Helium for cooling MRI scanners

The superconducting magnets in MRI machines must be cooled to extremely low temperatures (around 4 K) to work. Liquid helium is used because:

  • Helium has the lowest boiling point of any element (about −269 °C / 4 K), so it can reach those temperatures.
  • Helium is inert, so it does not react with the magnet, the coolant pipes or any other materials in the scanner.

This is the only common use where helium's low temperature — strictly a physical property linked to its weak interatomic forces — is the key feature. For GCSE, you would justify it as inertness plus its low boiling point.

Common exam traps

  • Saying "helium is light" or "argon is unreactive" without saying why that matters. You will be marked on the link between property and use.
  • Confusing inert with non-flammable. They are related but not identical: a gas can be non-flammable without being chemically inert (for example, CO₂ does not burn, but it can still react with metals such as magnesium).
  • Forgetting that air contains oxygen and nitrogen, both of which would react with hot metal. The reason argon is used in bulbs and welding is to exclude air, not just to "fill the space".
  • Writing that helium is used in balloons "because it floats". The marker wants the underlying property — lower density than air.

Exam technique — building an "Explain why…" answer

For a 2-mark "Explain why X is used for Y" question, build your answer in two clear parts:

  1. Name the property of the noble gas relevant to the use (inert / low density / non-flammable).
  2. Link that property to the use by stating the consequence — for example, "so it does not react with the hot filament", "so the balloon rises in air", or "so it will not catch fire".

If the question is worth 3 marks, you usually need to add a comparison ("…unlike hydrogen, which is flammable" or "…unlike air, which contains oxygen that would react"). Always make the comparison explicit rather than leaving the marker to infer it.

6.3.1.3 Trends in Group 0 F+H ▼

Trends in Group 0

Spec point 6.16: Describe the pattern in the physical properties of some noble gases and use this pattern to predict the physical properties of other noble gases. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

The noble gases sit in the right-hand column of the periodic table (Group 0, sometimes labelled Group 18). Going down the group — helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon — three physical properties show a clear, predictable pattern: relative atomic mass increases, density increases, and boiling point increases. Once you know how the trend behaves, you can read a partial data table from an exam paper and predict the value for any missing noble gas by interpolating between its neighbours. This is exactly the skill the Edexcel paper tests with this spec point.

Key Definitions

Group 0 (noble gases)The right-most group of the periodic table, containing the unreactive gases helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon
Relative atomic mass (Ar)The average mass of an atom of an element compared to one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom
DensityMass per unit volume; for gases usually quoted in g/dm³ or g/cm³ at a stated temperature and pressure

The three key trends going down Group 0

Relative atomic mass increases. Each step down the group adds a new electron shell and a much larger number of protons and neutrons. Helium has an Ar of 4, neon 20, argon 40, krypton 84, xenon 131. The values rise smoothly — every noble gas is heavier than the one above it.

Density increases. The same volume of gas (at the same temperature and pressure) contains the same number of particles, but heavier particles mean more mass packed into that volume. Helium is famously less dense than air — that is why helium balloons float. Argon is denser than air, which is why argon settles around the weld in arc-welding and protects the hot metal from oxygen. Radon, at the bottom of the group, is the densest noble gas.

Boiling point increases. All noble gases exist as single atoms (they are monatomic). The only forces between their atoms are very weak London dispersion forces (sometimes called intermolecular forces). Bigger atoms have more electrons, so these forces are stronger, so more energy is needed to pull the atoms apart into a gas. Helium boils at −269 °C — the lowest of any element — while radon boils at around −62 °C.

Three things to read off the table:

  • Every value gets bigger going down — there are no anomalies.
  • The gaps are not perfectly even, but the direction of the trend is consistent.
  • Density and boiling point both depend on the mass of the atoms, so they trend together.

Why these trends happen

Atoms get bigger. Going down the group, each element has one more occupied electron shell than the one above. Helium has electrons in shell 1 only; neon fills shells 1 and 2; argon fills shells 1, 2 and 3; and so on. More shells means a larger atomic radius and more electrons overall.

More electrons mean stronger forces between atoms. Although noble gases are unreactive (they do not form chemical bonds with each other), there are still very weak attractions between neighbouring atoms caused by tiny fluctuations in their electron clouds. The more electrons an atom has, the stronger these fluctuations and the stronger the attraction. This is why boiling point rises smoothly down the group — you have to give the atoms more energy to overcome these forces and turn the liquid into a gas.

More mass in the same space means higher density. The atoms of krypton and xenon are heavier than those of helium or neon, so a fixed volume of the heavier gas weighs more. Density is mass ÷ volume, so heavier atoms per dm³ produce a higher density value.

Predicting properties of a missing noble gas

The exam pattern is always the same: you are given a table with one row missing (or one column missing) and asked to suggest a sensible value for the missing element by looking at where it sits in the trend.

The procedure:

  1. Identify the two noble gases either side of the missing one in the table.
  2. Read off the values of the property for those two neighbours.
  3. The missing value must lie between those two — closer to whichever neighbour the missing element sits nearer in the group.
  4. State a value in that range (any value between the two neighbouring values is usually accepted).
  5. Quote the correct unit and sign (boiling points of noble gases are all negative °C).

Worked example — predicting the density of krypton (1CH0/2F 2019 Q2d pattern)

A common exam table shows density data for some of the noble gases, but not all of them. Here is a worked version using the canonical 1-mark question:

Noble gasDensity at 20 °C (g/dm³)
Helium0.17
Neon0.84
Argon1.66
Krypton?
Xenon5.49

Step 1 — Identify the neighbours. Krypton lies between argon (1.66 g/dm³) and xenon (5.49 g/dm³) in Group 0.

Step 2 — Check the trend direction. Density increases going down the group, so the krypton value must be larger than argon's and smaller than xenon's. That gives the range:

$$1.66 < \text{density of Kr} < 5.49 \text{ g/dm}^3$$

Step 3 — Interpolate. Krypton sits exactly one step below argon and one step above xenon, so a sensible estimate is roughly halfway between the two neighbours. A simple average gives:

$$\text{density of Kr} \approx \frac{1.66 + 5.49}{2} = \frac{7.15}{2} = 3.58 \text{ g/dm}^3$$

Step 4 — State the answer with units. A value of 3.58 g/dm³ (or anywhere between 1.66 and 5.49 g/dm³) is acceptable — the actual published value is around 3.49 g/dm³, which is well inside the credit range.

Common mark-scheme rule: for "suggest" questions where you are interpolating between two values, any number strictly between the two neighbouring values, with the correct unit, will score the mark. You do not need to do a precise calculation — you need to demonstrate that you understand the direction and magnitude of the trend.

Worked example — predicting the boiling point of argon

The same logic works for boiling point. Suppose the table gives you only neon (−246 °C) and krypton (−152 °C) and asks you to suggest a boiling point for argon.

Step 1 — Neighbours: neon at −246 °C, krypton at −152 °C.

Step 2 — Trend direction: boiling point rises (becomes less negative) going down the group, so argon's boiling point must lie between −246 and −152 °C.

*Step 3 — Interpolate:*

$$\text{boiling point of Ar} \approx \frac{(-246) + (-152)}{2} = \frac{-398}{2} = -199 \text{ °C}$$

Step 4 — State the answer: −199 °C is a sensible suggested value. The actual boiling point of argon is −186 °C, which is well within tolerance for a "suggest" mark.

Remember: the boiling points of noble gases are all below 0 °C — never quote a positive value, even for radon at the bottom of the group.

Extrapolating beyond the table (predicting radon or oganesson)

If the question gives you data for helium, neon, argon, krypton and xenon and asks you to predict a property of radon, you are extrapolating — going beyond the end of the data, not between two known points. The rule is the same: the trend continues, so radon will have the largest Ar, the highest density, and the highest boiling point shown. A reasonable extrapolated value is just one step further along the pattern of differences. Extrapolation is less reliable than interpolation because trends can flatten or curve at the extremes, but for noble-gas physical properties this is rarely an issue at GCSE.

Linking the trend to uses of noble gases

The physical-property trends explain why each noble gas is used the way it is (see 6.3.1.2 Using Noble Gases):

  • Helium has the lowest density — it floats balloons and airships.
  • Neon has a low boiling point and emits a bright red glow when an electric current passes through it — neon advertising signs.
  • Argon is denser than air and very unreactive — it shields hot welds and fills incandescent and energy-saving lamps so the filament does not oxidise.
  • Krypton and xenon are denser still and give brighter, whiter light in specialist lamps (xenon car headlights, xenon flash bulbs).

In every case the property being exploited (low density, high density, low boiling point, etc.) sits on one of the trends in this spec point.

Mathematical Skills — interpolating from a table

This spec point is flagged for Mathematical Skills 1d (use orders of magnitude) and 2c (extract and interpret information from charts, graphs and tables).

The general procedure for an interpolation question:

```

  1. Find the missing element in the table.
  2. Identify its two nearest neighbours with known values.
  3. Decide whether the property increases or decreases down the group.
  4. Choose a value that lies strictly between the two neighbours.
  5. Quote the value with the correct unit (and sign, for boiling points).

```

For an average-the-neighbours estimate:

$$\text{predicted value} = \frac{\text{value above} + \text{value below}}{2}$$

You can also simply pick any value in the range — exam mark schemes for "suggest" interpolation questions usually accept any number that sits between the two neighbouring entries.
