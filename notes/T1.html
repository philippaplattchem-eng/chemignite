Changing Models of the Atom

Spec 1.1: "Describe how the Dalton model of an atom has changed over time because of the discovery of subatomic particles." This is a Foundation + Higher tier spec point on Paper 1, and it is examined on both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

The Dalton model

In the early 1800s John Dalton proposed a model that pulled together the experimental chemistry of his day. The key claims were:

  • All matter is made of tiny solid spheres called atoms
  • Atoms cannot be created, destroyed, or divided into smaller pieces
  • All atoms of a given element are identical
  • Atoms of different elements are different
  • Compounds form when atoms of different elements join in fixed, simple whole-number ratios

This was a huge step forward because it explained the law of conservation of mass and the law of constant composition with one simple physical picture, and it was accepted for decades.

*Why the Dalton model could not last*

Dalton's atom was a solid ball with no internal structure. That works for "bench" chemistry — burning, dissolving, weighing, reacting. But once scientists in the late 1800s started passing electricity through gases, studying cathode rays, and firing high-energy particles at thin metal foils, they began seeing things a featureless solid sphere cannot explain.

Discovery of the electron — the plum pudding model

In 1897 J.J. Thomson, working with cathode ray tubes, showed that a beam of negatively charged particles could be pulled out of any metal. These particles were the same regardless of which metal and were nearly two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom. He had discovered the electron.

This was an immediate problem for Dalton. If atoms can give out electrons, then atoms must contain something smaller than themselves (so they are not indivisible), they must contain negative charge, and — because atoms overall are neutral — they must also contain an equal amount of positive charge.

Thomson's response was the plum pudding model: a single ball of positive charge with the tiny negative electrons stuck inside it, like currants in a pudding. The positive "pudding" exactly cancels the negative electrons, so the atom is neutral overall. The plum pudding model kept Dalton's roughly spherical atom but added an internal structure with two kinds of charge.

The nuclear model — Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden

In 1909 Rutherford's research group (Geiger and Marsden) fired alpha particles — small, fast, positive particles — at a very thin sheet of gold foil. Under the plum pudding model the positive charge is spread thinly over the whole atom, so the alpha particles should have passed almost straight through with only very small deflections.

What they actually saw:

  • Most alpha particles did pass straight through
  • A small number were deflected through large angles
  • A very small number — about one in eight thousand — bounced almost straight back

You cannot get a large-angle deflection from a thinly spread "pudding" of positive charge. To turn a fast positive particle around, the positive charge (and almost all the mass) must be packed into a tiny region.

Rutherford concluded:

  • Most of an atom is empty space (most alpha particles passed straight through)
  • The centre contains a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus holding nearly all the mass (some particles bounced back)
  • The electrons sit in the empty space around the nucleus

This is the nuclear model — the picture you draw when you sketch an atom today.

Bohr, then Chadwick — the modern model

Rutherford's nuclear model still had a problem: classical physics predicted the orbiting electrons would spiral into the nucleus and the atom would collapse. Niels Bohr solved this in 1913 by proposing that electrons can only travel in fixed energy levels (electron shells) at set distances from the nucleus. This explained the line spectra of hot gases and made the atom stable.

In 1932, James Chadwick showed there is a third subatomic particle in the nucleus — the neutron. The neutron has roughly the same mass as a proton but no charge. Adding it explained why the nucleus is heavier than the protons alone predict, and why the same element can have atoms of different masses (isotopes — same protons, different neutrons).

The result is the model used at GCSE today: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in fixed shells.

How models change in science

A key idea behind this spec point is that scientific models are not "true" or "false" — they are the best explanation of the experimental evidence available at the time. When new evidence appears that an existing model cannot explain, the model has to be revised or replaced.

The chain of evidence here is a clean example:

  1. Dalton's model fitted the chemistry of weighing and combining
  2. The discovery of the electron (Thomson, 1897) showed atoms must contain charged particles, leading to the plum pudding model
  3. The alpha-scattering experiment (Geiger and Marsden, 1909) showed positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus, leading to the nuclear model
  4. Bohr's energy-level idea (1913) explained why the nuclear atom is stable
  5. Chadwick's discovery of the neutron (1932) completed the modern picture

You may be asked to use this idea in a longer-answer style — for example, "Explain why scientists changed their model of the atom over time." A good answer states that as new experimental evidence (such as the alpha-scattering experiment) was collected, the old model could not explain the new observations, so it was replaced or refined.

The Structure of an Atom

Spec 1.2 — Describe the structure of an atom as a nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells. Foundation + Higher tier; both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0); Paper 1.

This spec point is the structural model that every other piece of atomic chemistry is built on — sub-atomic particle counting, mass number, atomic number, isotopes, electronic configurations, ionic and covalent bonding, and even Group trends in the periodic table all assume the model described in Spec 1.2. It looks deceptively simple, but examiners reward precise wording: "nucleus", "protons", "neutrons", "electrons", and "shells" all need to appear in the right places, and the words "in the centre" and "orbiting" are easy to miss under pressure.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AtomThe smallest particle of an element that still has the chemical properties of that element
NucleusThe small, dense, positively charged centre of an atom containing protons and neutrons
ProtonA positively charged sub-atomic particle found in the nucleus
NeutronA neutral (uncharged) sub-atomic particle found in the nucleus
ElectronA negatively charged sub-atomic particle that occupies shells around the nucleus
ShellA region around the nucleus at a fixed distance and energy level where electrons are found
Sub-atomic particleA particle that is smaller than an atom — protons, neutrons and electrons

The full picture

An atom is made up of a small, dense nucleus at the centre, surrounded by electrons that move in fixed energy levels called shells. The nucleus itself contains two types of sub-atomic particle: positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons. The electrons outside the nucleus are negatively charged.

Although most diagrams show the nucleus and the shells at roughly the same scale, that is misleading. The nucleus occupies only a tiny fraction of the volume of an atom. If an atom were scaled up to the size of a sports stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea in the centre — almost all of the atom is empty space, with the electrons moving in shells far from the nucleus.

This was the central insight of Rutherford's gold-foil experiment and is the model you are expected to describe and use throughout the GCSE course.

Why an atom has no overall charge

An atom is electrically neutral. The number of protons in the nucleus is always equal to the number of electrons orbiting it. Each proton carries a charge of +1 and each electron carries a charge of −1, so the positive nuclear charge is exactly cancelled by the negative charge of the electrons. Neutrons carry no charge and therefore contribute nothing to the overall electrical charge of the atom.

If the numbers of protons and electrons are not equal, the particle is no longer an atom — it is an ion. Gaining electrons gives a negative ion (anion), losing electrons gives a positive ion (cation). That distinction is important when answering exam questions: only neutral particles with equal numbers of protons and electrons count as atoms.

Why almost all the mass of an atom is in the nucleus

Protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of 1, while an electron has a relative mass of about 1/1836 — so small that on a relative scale it is treated as 0 or "negligible". Because the nucleus contains every proton and every neutron in the atom, it accounts for almost all of the atomic mass even though it occupies almost none of the atomic volume.

This is the reason that mass number is calculated by adding the number of protons and the number of neutrons together — electrons are not included because their mass barely changes the total. It also explains why a nucleus is described as "dense": a large amount of mass is squeezed into an extremely small region of space.

Why electrons are arranged in shells, not just floating around

Electrons cannot exist anywhere they like around the nucleus — they are restricted to fixed energy levels called shells. The shell closest to the nucleus is the lowest in energy and fills first. Once it is full, electrons go into the next shell out, and so on. At GCSE the shell capacities you need are:

This shell model explains why atoms react the way they do: it is the outer-shell electrons that decide an element's chemistry, not the ones buried near the nucleus.

Putting the model to work — a worked example

Question: An atom of sodium has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons. Describe its structure and explain why it has no overall charge.

*Answer:*

  • The 11 protons and 12 neutrons are found together in the nucleus in the centre of the atom.
  • The 11 electrons are arranged in shells around the nucleus (2 in the first shell, 8 in the second, 1 in the third).
  • The atom has no overall charge because the number of protons (+11 in total) is equal to the number of electrons (−11 in total), so the positive and negative charges cancel out.
  • Neutrons contribute no charge, so they do not affect this balance.

Notice the exam-style language: "found together in the nucleus", "arranged in shells around the nucleus", and "the positive and negative charges cancel out". These are the kinds of phrases that score full marks because they line up exactly with the spec wording.

Common pitfalls to avoid

  • Confusing where each particle lives. Protons and neutrons go inside the nucleus; electrons go in shells outside the nucleus. Saying "electrons are in the nucleus" or "neutrons orbit the nucleus" is a guaranteed loss of marks.
  • Confusing charge. The nucleus is positively charged because of the protons; it is not "neutral because of the neutrons". Make sure you do not let the word "neutron" pull you towards saying the whole nucleus is neutral.
  • Forgetting electrons in mass calculations. Because electrons have negligible mass, you ignore them when adding up to find mass number — but you do not ignore them when working out charge.
  • Drawing all shells the same size. Shells are at increasing distances from the nucleus and at increasing energy levels; that detail is sometimes worth a mark in higher-tier "describe" questions.

Key Takeaways
  1. An atom has a small, dense nucleus at the centre, surrounded by electrons in shells.
  2. The nucleus contains two sub-atomic particles: protons and neutrons.
  3. Protons are positively charged (+1), neutrons have no charge (0), and electrons are negatively charged (−1).
  4. Electrons sit in shells around the nucleus — not inside it.
  5. An atom is electrically neutral because the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the positive and negative charges cancel out.
  6. Almost all of the mass of an atom is in the nucleus, because protons and neutrons have a relative mass of 1 each, while electrons have negligible mass.
  7. Almost all of the volume of an atom is empty space — the nucleus is tiny compared with the atom as a whole.
  8. Electrons fill shells in a fixed order: the first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the second and third hold up to 8 each (at GCSE).
  9. When asked to describe atomic structure, name all of the components — nucleus, protons, neutrons, electrons — and say where each is found.
  10. When asked to compare today's model with an older model (e.g. Dalton's), build the answer around the Spec 1.2 components rather than just repeating the stem.
  11. Watch your charge wording: the nucleus is positively charged because of the protons, not neutral because of the neutrons.
  12. This spec point is the foundation for everything that follows in atomic structure — mass number, atomic number, isotopes, ions, electronic configuration and bonding all assume the model described here.
1.1.2.2 The Sub-atomic Particles F+H ▼

The Sub-atomic Particles

Spec 1.3 — Recall the relative charge and relative mass of a proton, a neutron and an electron. Spec 1.4 — Explain why atoms contain equal numbers of protons and electrons. Foundation + Higher tier; both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

Atoms are built from three sub-atomic particles: protons, neutrons and electrons. Two of them (protons and neutrons) sit together in a tiny central nucleus; the third (electrons) move around the nucleus in shells. The relative masses and charges of these three particles determine how every atom in the periodic table behaves — they decide what the element is, whether the atom carries an overall charge, and how it bonds to other atoms.

Because the three particles have very different masses and charges, chemists describe them on a relative scale rather than in absolute kilograms or coulombs. The actual mass of a proton is about 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and the actual charge is about 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C — numbers that are clumsy to work with at GCSE. The relative scale fixes the proton at a mass of 1 and a charge of +1, then expresses everything else in those units.

A common alternative phrasing accepted by Edexcel for the electron mass is "negligible", "very small" or "approximately 0". The full ratio is roughly 1/1836 of a proton — Foundation and Higher tier students do not need this exact figure but should know that the electron is the lightest of the three and that its mass is treated as negligible compared to that of a proton or a neutron.

*Why the proton and neutron have the same relative mass*

A proton and a neutron each have an actual mass of approximately 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. The two values are not exactly identical — a neutron is fractionally heavier — but they round to 1 on the relative mass scale. Treating them as equal is a deliberate simplification that lets us calculate mass numbers (and later relative atomic masses) very quickly: the mass of an atom is, to a very good approximation, just the count of protons plus the count of neutrons.

*Why the electron is treated as having "negligible" mass*

An electron is roughly 1836 times lighter than a proton. If you imagine a proton as having a mass of one kilogram, the electron would weigh about half a gram. Because of this, electrons contribute almost nothing to the mass of an atom — even a uranium atom, with 92 electrons, gets less than 0.03 % of its total mass from its electrons. This is why mass number (covered in 1.1.3.2) only counts protons and neutrons, not electrons.

*Why the proton and electron carry equal but opposite charges*

The proton and the electron are described as having relative charges of +1 and −1. The actual magnitudes are identical (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C); only the sign differs. This exact equality is essential — it is what makes it possible for an atom to be electrically neutral overall (see Spec 1.4 below). The neutron's relative charge of 0 reflects the fact that it carries no electric charge at all, which is why a nucleus packed with positive protons does not simply blow itself apart electrostatically — but a full explanation of nuclear stability is beyond GCSE.

Why atoms contain equal numbers of protons and electrons (Spec 1.4)

This is the "explain" half of the spec point and the part that most often appears as a 2-mark question.

A neutral atom has no overall electric charge. The only sub-atomic particles that carry a charge are the protons (+1 each) and the electrons (−1 each); neutrons contribute nothing because their charge is 0. Therefore the only way to make the total charge come out to zero is for the positive charge from the protons to be exactly cancelled by the negative charge from the electrons.

Because each proton carries +1 and each electron carries −1, the cancellation only works if there is the same number of each. Five protons (+5) need exactly five electrons (−5) to give a total of 0; six protons need six electrons; and so on for every element in the periodic table.

You can summarise the logic as:

`` Total charge of atom = (number of protons × (+1)) + (number of electrons × (−1)) + (number of neutrons × 0) = (+protons) + (−electrons) For this total to equal 0: number of protons = number of electrons ``

If an atom gains or loses electrons, the proton-to-electron count is no longer equal and the particle is no longer a neutral atom — it becomes a charged particle called an ion (covered in 1.3.1.1 and 1.3.1.2). Atoms themselves, by definition, are always neutral.

Worked example — Why a chlorine atom has no overall charge

A neutral chlorine atom contains 17 protons. Explain why the atom has no overall charge.

Step 1 — Identify the charged particles. The only particles that contribute to the overall charge are the protons and the electrons. Neutrons have a relative charge of 0 and can be ignored.

Step 2 — State the number of each. The atom has 17 protons. Because it is a neutral atom, by Spec 1.4 it must also have 17 electrons.

*Step 3 — Calculate the total charge.*

`` Total charge = (17 × (+1)) + (17 × (−1)) = (+17) + (−17) = 0 ``

Step 4 — Conclude. The total positive charge from the 17 protons is exactly cancelled by the total negative charge from the 17 electrons, so the atom has no overall charge.

A 2-mark mark scheme answer would simply be: "The number of electrons (17) is the same as the number of protons (17), so the positive and negative charges balance / cancel out."

Common confusions to avoid

Charge sign on the electron. A negative sign is required. Writing "1" instead of "−1" loses the mark, even though most students know the electron is "the negative one". Always write the sign explicitly.

Mass of the electron. Acceptable answers include 1/1836, "very small", "negligible" or "approximately 0". Writing "0" without qualification is sometimes penalised because it implies the electron has literally no mass, which is not true. Writing "1" is wrong — that is the proton's relative mass.

Confusing protons and neutrons. Both have a relative mass of 1, so the only way to tell them apart on the table is by their charges (proton +1, neutron 0). Reading the charges carefully is the safest way to spot a mix-up.

Saying "the atom has no charge because the neutrons cancel out the protons". This is wrong. Neutrons carry no charge — they cannot cancel anything out. It is the electrons that cancel the protons.

Treating "atom" and "ion" as the same thing. The proton-equals-electron rule applies only to neutral atoms. Once electrons are added or removed, you have an ion, and the rule no longer holds.

Comparison — atom vs ion (linking 1.4 forward)

This SP focuses on the atom, but it is useful to see why the rule matters by comparing it to what happens in an ion.

ParticleProtonsElectronsNet ChargeReason
Sodium atom (Na)11110Equal protons and electrons → charges balance
Sodium ion (Na⁺)1110+1One fewer electron than protons → net +1
Chloride ion (Cl⁻)1718−1One more electron than protons → net −1

The Na⁺ ion has lost an electron, and the Cl⁻ ion has gained an electron. The number of protons does not change in either case — that would change the identity of the element entirely, which is what happens in nuclear (not chemical) reactions.

1.1.3.1 The Nucleus F+H ▼

The Nucleus

Spec 1.5 — Describe the nucleus of an atom as very small compared to the overall size of the atom. Spec 1.6 — Recall that most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus. Foundation + Higher tier; both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

Every atom has a tiny central region called the nucleus that contains the protons and neutrons. The space outside the nucleus — where the electrons move in shells — is enormous compared to the nucleus itself. Even though the nucleus is so small, it carries almost all of the atom's mass because protons and neutrons are roughly 1,836 times heavier than electrons. These two ideas — that the nucleus is small but heavy — together explain almost every important property of atoms, from why solids feel solid even though atoms are mostly empty space, to why the alpha-scattering experiment of Geiger and Marsden produced its surprising results.

You need to be able to describe both facts (small size, large mass) and to use them when explaining experimental observations or interpreting diagrams of atomic structure.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
NucleusThe small, dense, positively charged centre of an atom that contains protons and neutrons
ProtonA positively charged sub-atomic particle found in the nucleus, with a relative mass of 1
NeutronAn uncharged (neutral) sub-atomic particle found in the nucleus, with a relative mass of 1
ElectronA negatively charged sub-atomic particle that moves around the nucleus in shells, with a relative mass of effectively 0 (about 1/1836 of a proton)
Atomic radiusThe distance from the centre of the nucleus to the outermost electron shell — gives the overall size of the atom
Nuclear radiusThe distance from the centre of the nucleus to its outer edge — gives the size of the nucleus alone

How small is the nucleus?

The nucleus is very small compared to the whole atom. The diameter of a typical atom is roughly 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (0.1 nanometres), while the diameter of its nucleus is roughly 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ m. That makes the atom about 10,000 times wider than its nucleus.

A common scaled analogy used in textbooks: if an atom were the size of a sports stadium, the nucleus would be a pea sitting on the centre spot. Everything else — the seats, the pitch, the stands — is the empty space where the electrons live. This scale is not just a curiosity. It tells you that atoms (and therefore all matter) are mostly empty space, and that the small size of the nucleus is one of the central facts about how atoms behave.

You can also describe it as a fraction. The nucleus is roughly 1/10,000 of the diameter of the atom. By volume the difference is even more extreme: because volume scales with the cube of length, the nucleus occupies only about (1/10,000)³ ≈ 1/10¹² of the atom's volume.

Why most of the mass is in the nucleus

Every atom is built from just three sub-atomic particles: protons, neutrons and electrons. Their relative masses are not equal:

ParticleLocationRelative massRelative charge
ProtonNucleus1+1
NeutronNucleus10
ElectronShells around the nucleusvery small (effectively 0, about 1/1836)−1

Because the relative mass of an electron is essentially zero on this scale, the electrons contribute almost nothing to the total mass of the atom. All of the heavy particles — the protons and neutrons — are packed together in the nucleus. That is why we say the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus.

For example, in a single carbon-12 atom there are 6 protons + 6 neutrons in the nucleus (total relative mass ≈ 12) and 6 electrons in the shells (total relative mass ≈ 6 × 1/1836 ≈ 0.003). More than 99.97% of the atom's mass sits in the nucleus.

Why this makes the nucleus so dense

If you concentrate almost all of an object's mass into roughly one trillionth (10⁻¹²) of its volume, the density of that small region must be huge. Nuclear matter has a density of about 10¹⁷ kg/m³ — about 100 trillion times denser than water. You will not be asked to recall this number at GCSE, but it is useful background: the nucleus is not just small, it is incredibly dense, and that density is the reason that fast-moving alpha particles can be deflected by it (see below).

Linking nucleus size and mass to alpha-scattering

The conclusion that the nucleus is small but contains nearly all of the atom's mass came directly from the alpha-scattering experiment carried out under Ernest Rutherford by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden. They fired positively charged alpha particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil and looked at how the alpha particles travelled through it.

ObservationConclusion about the nucleus
Most alpha particles passed straight through the foilMost of the atom is empty space
A small number of alpha particles were deflected through small anglesThe atom must contain something positive that repels the positive alpha particles
A very small number bounced almost straight backThe positive part of the atom (the nucleus) is small, very dense and contains almost all the mass — only something heavy and dense could turn an alpha particle around

The first observation tells you that the nucleus must be tiny — if it filled the atom, every alpha particle would hit it. The third observation tells you that the nucleus must be heavy — if the alpha particles met something light, they would knock it aside rather than bouncing back themselves. The two facts in this spec point (small + heavy) are exactly the two facts that the alpha-scattering result demands. Examiners often link 1.5/1.6 back to this experiment in stem questions or extended-answer prompts.

Worked example — estimating the scale (Maths skill 1d)

The Edexcel maths-skill list flags 1d (make estimates of the results of simple calculations) for spec 1.5. A common task is to compare the size of the nucleus to the whole atom.

Question: A hydrogen atom has an overall diameter of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Its nucleus has a diameter of about 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. Estimate how many times wider the atom is than the nucleus.

*Step 1 — Identify the two diameters.*

Atom diameter = 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m Nucleus diameter = 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m

*Step 2 — Divide one by the other.*

Ratio = (1 × 10⁻¹⁰) ÷ (1 × 10⁻¹⁵) = 10⁵ = 100,000

*Step 3 — Interpret the answer.*

The atom is about 100,000 times wider than the nucleus (an order-of-magnitude estimate). That is consistent with the textbook value of "about 10,000 times" for many atoms — the answer depends on which atom you pick, but the key point is that the atom is many thousands of times wider than the nucleus.

This kind of estimate would be marked for showing that you have used standard form correctly, divided the right way round, and reached an answer in the correct order of magnitude. Watch out for two common slips: writing 10⁻¹⁰ ÷ 10⁻¹⁵ as 10⁻²⁵ (wrong sign in the exponent rule), and dividing the smaller diameter by the larger (which gives 10⁻⁵ — an answer less than 1, which can't possibly mean "the atom is wider than the nucleus").

Common pitfalls to avoid

  • "Mass of an atom = mass of the nucleus exactly." Strictly speaking the electrons do contribute a tiny amount of mass — but for GCSE the contribution is negligible. The spec wording is "most of the mass" rather than "all of the mass". In an explain question, "almost all" or "most" is safer than "all".

  • Saying "the nucleus is in the middle" instead of "the nucleus is very small". "In the middle" is not the same fact and would not score for 1.5.
  • Forgetting electrons exist when discussing mass. Electrons are still part of the atom — they just contribute almost nothing to its mass. Saying "the atom only has protons and neutrons" is wrong.
  • Confusing relative mass and relative charge. A neutron has the same relative mass as a proton (1) but a different relative charge (0 vs +1). The mass-vs-charge confusion shows up most often in questions that combine 1.5/1.6 with the sub-atomic particles spec point.

1.1.3.2 Mass Number & Atomic Number F+H ▼

Mass Number & Atomic Number

Spec 1.7 — Recall the meaning of the term mass number of an atom.

Spec 1.8 — Describe atoms of a given element as having the same number of protons in the nucleus and that this number is unique to that element.

Tier: Foundation + Higher · Qualification: 1CH0 + 1SC0 · Paper 1.

Every atom can be uniquely described using two whole numbers: the atomic number and the mass number. Together, these two numbers tell you exactly which element the atom is and how many of each subatomic particle it contains. This is one of the most heavily examined ideas in Section 1 because almost every later topic — isotopes, electronic configuration, ionic bonding, the periodic table — depends on being able to read these numbers correctly.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Atomic number (Z)The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom
Mass number (A)The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom
ProtonA positively charged subatomic particle found in the nucleus, with a relative mass of 1
NeutronAn uncharged subatomic particle found in the nucleus, with a relative mass of 1
ElectronA negatively charged subatomic particle found in shells around the nucleus, with a relative mass of approximately 1/1836 (effectively zero in calculations)
IsotopeAtoms of the same element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons (and therefore a different mass number)
ElementA substance made of only one type of atom — all of which share the same atomic number

How atomic symbols are written

The standard nuclear symbol for an atom is written with two numbers stacked to the left of the chemical symbol:

  • The mass number (A) sits at the top left
  • The atomic number (Z) sits at the bottom left

For example, ²³Na with subscript 11 means a sodium atom with mass number 23 and atomic number 11. From this you can deduce:

  • Number of protons = atomic number = 11
  • Number of electrons (in a neutral atom) = number of protons = 11
  • Number of neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 23 − 11 = 12

The order of the numbers matters. The bottom number (atomic number) is always the smaller one because the nucleus must contain at least as many neutrons as protons in stable atoms (the only exception is hydrogen-1, where the mass number equals the atomic number because there are no neutrons).

Why the atomic number defines the element

The atomic number is the single most important property of an atom. It is what makes carbon "carbon" and gold "gold". Two atoms with the same atomic number must be the same element, even if they have different mass numbers (i.e. they are isotopes). Two atoms with different atomic numbers must be different elements.

This is why the modern periodic table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number — every element has its own unique value of Z, and the table runs from hydrogen (Z = 1) through to the heaviest known synthetic elements. If you change the number of protons, you change the element. This happens, for example, in nuclear reactions where a proton is gained or lost — but it never happens in ordinary chemical reactions, which only involve electrons.

The number of protons also fixes the overall positive charge of the nucleus. Because a neutral atom must have equal numbers of protons and electrons, the atomic number also tells you how many electrons a neutral atom has. The arrangement of those electrons in shells is what controls the element's chemistry, so once you know the atomic number you know everything you need to predict the element's bonding and reactivity.

Why the mass number is a whole number

The mass number is always a whole number because it is simply a count of particles — protons plus neutrons. You cannot have a fraction of a proton or a fraction of a neutron in a nucleus, so the mass number for any given atom is necessarily an integer.

This is a key contrast with the relative atomic mass (Aᵣ) that you see on the periodic table, which is usually not a whole number (for example, chlorine has Aᵣ = 35.5). The relative atomic mass is a weighted average across all the naturally occurring isotopes of an element, so it is not the mass number of any one atom — it is a mean. Understanding this distinction is essential before tackling isotopes and Aᵣ calculations later in the topic.

Why the mass number is (very nearly) the mass of the atom

Protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of 1. Electrons have a relative mass of about 1/1836, which is so small that it is taken to be effectively zero for GCSE calculations. The mass of an atom is therefore concentrated almost entirely in the nucleus, and the mass number gives a very good approximation of an atom's relative mass.

For example, an atom of carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, giving a mass number of 12. The 6 electrons add a negligible amount to the total mass, so the relative mass of one carbon-12 atom is taken to be 12. This is the basis of the relative atomic mass scale.

How to deduce particles from a nuclear symbol

For any neutral atom, three simple rules let you read off the particle counts:

`` Number of protons = atomic number (Z) Number of electrons = atomic number (Z) [neutral atom only] Number of neutrons = mass number (A) − atomic number (Z) ``

Worked example 1 — neutral atom

An atom of magnesium has the symbol ²⁴Mg with atomic number 12.

Step 1 — Identify the numbers. Mass number A = 24, atomic number Z = 12.

*Step 2 — Apply the rules.*

  • Protons = Z = 12
  • Electrons = Z = 12 (because it is a neutral atom)
  • Neutrons = A − Z = 24 − 12 = 12

Answer: 12 protons, 12 electrons, 12 neutrons.

Worked example 2 — neutral atom of chlorine-37

In a 1 mark Foundation question, you might be told: "Atoms of chlorine-37 have a mass number of 37. Calculate the number of neutrons in atoms of chlorine-37."

Chlorine has atomic number 17 (this is given in your periodic table — you are expected to look it up).

  • Neutrons = A − Z = 37 − 17 = 20

You only need to write the final number for 1 mark, but always show the subtraction in case you make an arithmetic slip.

How to deduce particles from an ion symbol (Higher tier focus)

When the species is an ion, the rules for protons and neutrons are unchanged — these are nuclear particles and are not affected by gaining or losing electrons. What changes is the electron count:

`` Number of electrons in a positive ion = Z − (charge size) Number of electrons in a negative ion = Z + (charge size) ``

This is the step that catches the most candidates out in exams, because it is tempting to assume the species is an atom when it is in fact an ion.

Worked example 3 — negative ion

A species is represented by ¹²⁵X²⁻, and you are told this ion has 54 electrons. Calculate the number of protons and neutrons.

Step 1 — Use the electron count and the charge to find the atomic number. A 2− ion has gained 2 electrons compared with the neutral atom. So the neutral atom has 54 − 2 = 52 electrons, which means 52 protons (and Z = 52, which is tellurium).

*Step 2 — Use the mass number to find the neutrons.*

  • Neutrons = A − Z = 125 − 52 = 73

Answer: 52 protons and 73 neutrons.

The common error here is to subtract the wrong way (adding 2 protons to the electron count instead of subtracting 2 electrons from it), or to forget that the species is an ion at all and treat 54 as the atomic number.

Worked example 4 — positive ion

An aluminium ion is represented as ²⁷Al³⁺ with atomic number 13. Find the number of each subatomic particle.

  • Protons = Z = 13
  • Neutrons = A − Z = 27 − 13 = 14
  • Electrons = Z − charge = 13 − 3 = 10 (it has lost 3 electrons to form the 3+ ion)

Relative atomic mass (Aᵣ)The average mass of the atoms of an element, taking into account the masses and abundances of all the naturally occurring isotopes, compared to 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom

Why "same element" forces "same number of protons"

The identity of an element is fixed by the number of protons in its nucleus. If you change the proton count, you change the element — carbon has 6 protons; nitrogen has 7. So any two atoms that are both carbon must both have 6 protons. They can, however, differ in the number of neutrons in the nucleus — and that is the whole idea of isotopes. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon (both have 6 protons), but carbon-12 has 6 neutrons while carbon-14 has 8 neutrons.

This is why exam mark schemes are strict about wording: an isotope definition has to mention both halves — the same number of protons and a different number of neutrons. One half on its own does not score.

Why isotopes of the same element have identical chemistry

Chemical reactions are about electrons — specifically, about the outer-shell electrons being lost, gained or shared. The number of electrons in a neutral atom equals the number of protons (the atomic number). Isotopes of the same element have the same atomic number, so they have the same number of electrons, in the same shell arrangement. Their chemistry is therefore identical — chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both react with sodium to make sodium chloride, both bleach dyes, both displace bromine from bromide solutions.

What does change between isotopes is mass. A chlorine-37 atom is heavier than a chlorine-35 atom because it has two extra neutrons in its nucleus. This shows up in physical properties — heavier isotopes tend to diffuse more slowly, have slightly higher boiling points, and so on — but not in chemical properties.

Why neutrons are the only thing that changes

Three rules together pin down what an isotope is and isn't:

  1. The proton count (atomic number) defines the element. If protons change, the element changes — so for two atoms to be isotopes of each other the proton count must stay the same.
  2. The electron count in a neutral atom equals the proton count. So electrons don't change between neutral isotopes either.
  3. That leaves neutrons as the only sub-atomic particle whose count can differ between two atoms of the same element.

This is why the mark scheme for "explain what isotopes are, in terms of subatomic particles" wants both points: same protons (electrons too — but electrons are normally implied by "same element"), different neutrons.

Reading isotope notation

You will see isotopes written in two equivalent ways. Both carry exactly the same information:

  • Hyphen notation: chlorine-35, chlorine-37, carbon-14, uranium-238. The number after the hyphen is the mass number.
  • Symbol notation: ³⁵₁₇Cl, ³⁷₁₇Cl, ¹⁴₆C, ²³⁸₉₂U. The top number is the mass number (A); the bottom number is the atomic number (Z).

If only the hyphen form is given, you have to look up the atomic number from the periodic table. For chlorine the periodic table tells you Z = 17, so chlorine-35 has 17 protons and (35 − 17) = 18 neutrons.

Worked example A — counting in a neutral atom

Question: Magnesium has the symbol ²⁵₁₂Mg. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in one neutral atom of this isotope.

*Working:*

  • Atomic number Z = 12, so protons = 12.
  • Mass number A = 25, so neutrons = A − Z = 25 − 12 = 13.
  • The atom is neutral (it carries no charge), so electrons = protons = 12.

Answer: 12 protons, 13 neutrons, 12 electrons.

This is the standard "given Z and A, count the particles" question and it appears on virtually every Foundation paper. The maths skill being tested is change the subject of an equation (Maths skill 3b — Mass number = Protons + Neutrons, rearranged to Neutrons = Mass number − Protons).

Worked example B — counting in an ion

Question: An ion has the symbol ¹²⁵₅₃X²⁻. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in this ion. Identify the element X.

*Working:*

  • Atomic number Z = 53, so protons = 53. From the periodic table, the element with 53 protons is iodine — so X = I.
  • Mass number A = 125, so neutrons = 125 − 53 = 72.
  • The 2− charge tells you the ion has gained 2 electrons compared with a neutral atom. A neutral atom of iodine has 53 electrons, so the ion has 53 + 2 = 55 electrons.

Answer: 53 protons, 72 neutrons, 55 electrons. The element is iodine.

A common slip here is to add the charge to the proton count (or subtract from it). Charges live on electrons — the proton number never changes during ionisation. If the ion is negative it has gained electrons (more electrons than protons). If the ion is positive it has lost electrons (fewer electrons than protons).

Worked example C — counting neutrons given just the isotope name

Question: Atoms of chlorine-37 have a mass number of 37. Calculate the number of neutrons in atoms of chlorine-37.

*Working:*

  • Use the periodic table to find chlorine's atomic number: Z = 17.
  • Mass number A = 37 (given by the "-37" in the name).
  • Neutrons = A − Z = 37 − 17 = 20.

Answer: 20 neutrons.

This is the most basic 1-mark version of the calculation and was the exact question set in November 2020.

Why relative atomic mass is not always a whole number

If every chlorine atom had a mass number of 35, the relative atomic mass of chlorine would be 35 — a whole number. But in the real world, chlorine is a mixture of isotopes: roughly 75% of any sample is chlorine-35 and roughly 25% is chlorine-37. The relative atomic mass is the weighted average mass of the atoms in a normal sample, and a weighted average of 35 and 37 in a 3:1 ratio gives 35.5 — not a whole number.

You can see why qualitatively without doing the calculation:

  • If chlorine were 100% chlorine-35, Aᵣ would be exactly 35.
  • If chlorine were 100% chlorine-37, Aᵣ would be exactly 37.
  • A real sample sits somewhere between those two extremes, weighted towards 35 because chlorine-35 is more common — landing at 35.5.

For Higher tier you also have to perform the actual weighted-average sum (covered in 1.1.5.1 Calculating Relative Atomic Mass). At Foundation tier you only need the qualitative explanation: relative atomic masses are non-whole numbers because elements exist as mixtures of isotopes with different masses, and Aᵣ averages over those.

The same logic explains why bromine has Aᵣ ≈ 80 (not 79 or 81) — it's a near-50:50 mixture of bromine-79 and bromine-81. Copper has Aᵣ ≈ 63.5 — a mixture of copper-63 and copper-65 with copper-63 more common. Hydrogen has Aᵣ ≈ 1.008 — almost pure hydrogen-1 with a tiny amount of hydrogen-2 (deuterium), which is why the average is just above 1.

1.1.5.1 Calculating Relative Atomic Mass H only ▼

Calculating Relative Atomic Mass

Spec point 1.12: Calculate the relative atomic mass of an element from the relative masses and abundances of its isotopes. Higher tier only on both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

This spec point is one of the most reliably-tested calculation skills on the Higher paper for Section 1. Because most elements exist as a mixture of isotopes, the value of relative atomic mass (A_r) printed on the periodic table is almost never a whole number — it is a weighted mean of the relative isotopic masses, calculated using the natural abundance of each isotope as the weighting. You need to be able to perform this calculation confidently, present the working, and round to the correct number of decimal places.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Relative atomic mass (A_r)The weighted mean mass of the atoms of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12
Relative isotopic massThe mass of a single isotope of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12 (numerically equal to the mass number for GCSE purposes)
Isotopic abundanceThe proportion of atoms of a given isotope present in a naturally-occurring sample of the element, usually expressed as a percentage (%)
Weighted meanAn average that takes into account how much each value contributes to the total — values that occur more often have a bigger effect on the average
IsotopeAtoms of the same element (same number of protons) that contain different numbers of neutrons, and therefore have different mass numbers

Why relative atomic mass is a weighted mean and not a simple arithmetic mean

A simple arithmetic mean treats every value as if it appears equally often. If you took the simple mean of lithium-6 and lithium-7, you would get (6 + 7) / 2 = 6.50. But the periodic table gives lithium an A_r of about 6.94 — much closer to 7 than to 6. The reason is that lithium-7 is far more common in nature than lithium-6: roughly 92% of lithium atoms are lithium-7, and only about 8% are lithium-6. The average mass of a lithium atom must therefore sit much closer to 7 than to 6, because nine out of ten randomly-chosen lithium atoms will be lithium-7.

A weighted mean accounts for this by multiplying each isotopic mass by its share of the total population (its abundance) before adding them together. Each isotope contributes to the final A_r in proportion to how often it actually occurs. This is why the relative atomic masses on the periodic table — chlorine 35.5, copper 63.5, bromine 79.9 — are decimal values that lie between the mass numbers of the isotopes, but skewed toward the more abundant one.

It is also worth understanding why isotopes have different masses in the first place. Isotopes of the same element have the same number of protons (same atomic number, same chemistry) but different numbers of neutrons. Each extra neutron adds approximately one unit of mass. So lithium-6 (3 protons + 3 neutrons) and lithium-7 (3 protons + 4 neutrons) behave identically in chemical reactions but differ in mass by one unit per atom. The chemistry of an element is determined by its electrons, but the average mass of its atoms depends on the mix of isotopes.

The canonical formula

`` A_r = (mass₁ × % abundance₁) + (mass₂ × % abundance₂) + … ───────────────────────────────────────────────── 100 ``

For an element with two isotopes, this simplifies to:

`` A_r = (mass₁ × % abundance₁ + mass₂ × % abundance₂) / 100 ``

The procedure is always the same:

  1. Multiply the mass number of each isotope by its percentage abundance
  2. Add the products together
  3. Divide the total by 100
  4. Round the answer to the number of decimal places (or significant figures) the question specifies — Edexcel commonly asks for 2 d.p.

If abundances are given as decimal fractions (e.g. 0.0759 and 0.9241) rather than percentages, the divisor is 1 instead of 100. Always check which form the data has been given in.

Worked example — relative atomic mass of lithium (June 2019 1CH0/2H Q6(c))

Question: A sample of lithium contains 7.59% of lithium-6 and 92.41% of lithium-7. Calculate the relative atomic mass of lithium in this sample. Give your answer to two decimal places. (4 marks)

*Step 1 — multiply each isotopic mass by its percentage abundance:*

  • Lithium-6: 6 × 7.59 = 45.54
  • Lithium-7: 7 × 92.41 = 646.87

*Step 2 — add the products:*

45.54 + 646.87 = 692.41

*Step 3 — divide by 100:*

A_r = 692.41 / 100 = 6.9241

*Step 4 — round to 2 decimal places:*

*A_r (lithium) = 6.92*

The four marks in the published mark scheme map onto: (1) correct multiplication setup, (2) correct addition of the products, (3) correct division by 100 to give the unrounded answer, (4) the answer presented to 2 decimal places. Crucially, the final mark is awarded for the correct number of decimal places of the candidate's own working — even a candidate who slipped earlier could pick up the rounding mark, which is why showing every step matters.

Worked example — silicon (Nov 2021 1CH0/1H Q9(b)(ii))

Silicon has three naturally-occurring isotopes: silicon-28, silicon-29 and silicon-30. Suppose the abundances are 92.23%, 4.68% and 3.09% respectively.

*Step 1 — multiply each mass by its % abundance:*

  • Silicon-28: 28 × 92.23 = 2582.44
  • Silicon-29: 29 × 4.68 = 135.72
  • Silicon-30: 30 × 3.09 = 92.70

*Step 2 — add the products:*

2582.44 + 135.72 + 92.70 = 2810.86

*Step 3 — divide by 100:*

A_r = 2810.86 / 100 = 28.1086

*Step 4 — round appropriately:*

To 2 d.p.: A_r (silicon) = 28.11

A common error on this question, flagged in the November 2021 examiner report, was rounding the correct answer down to a whole number (28). That destroys the second mark, because the whole point of A_r is that it is a non-integer weighted mean — if it rounds to the same value as one of the isotope masses, you have effectively thrown away the information about the other isotopes.

Estimating and sense-checking the answer (Maths skill 1d)

Before you commit to a final answer, check that it lies between the smallest and largest isotopic masses, and that it sits closer to the more abundant isotope. For lithium: the answer must be between 6 and 7, and because lithium-7 is much more abundant (92.41%), the answer must be much closer to 7 than to 6. A value of 6.92 passes both checks. A value of 6.50, 7.59 or 13 would all be obvious slips.

This estimation step is a quick, almost-free way to catch arithmetic errors and is exactly the kind of "estimate the result of a simple calculation" skill (maths skill 1d) the spec wants you to use.

1.2.1.1 Mendeleev's Arrangement F+H ▼

Mendeleev's Arrangement

Spec point 1.13 — "Describe how Mendeleev arranged the elements, known at that time, in a periodic table by using properties of these elements and their compounds." Foundation + Higher tier; assessed on both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0) Paper 1.

In 1869, Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev published the first widely accepted periodic table. Only about 60 elements were known at the time, and chemists had no concept of protons, neutrons or electronic configuration — those discoveries came decades later. Mendeleev had to work entirely from the physical and chemical properties of elements and their compounds and from the relative atomic masses that had been measured experimentally. The fact that he produced an arrangement still recognisable today — and that he correctly predicted the existence of elements that had not yet been discovered — is why his table is treated as the foundation of the modern periodic table.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Periodic tableAn arrangement of the chemical elements in rows and columns that groups together elements with similar properties
GroupA vertical column of elements in the periodic table; elements in the same group have similar chemical properties
PeriodA horizontal row of elements in the periodic table
Atomic mass (relative atomic mass)The average mass of an atom of an element compared with one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom; this was the property Mendeleev had access to and used to order his elements
Atomic number (proton number)The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom; this property was not known in Mendeleev's time, so he could not use it
PeriodicityThe repeating pattern of similar properties at regular intervals when elements are listed in order of increasing atomic mass

The distinction in the last two rows is critical for the exam. Mendeleev used atomic mass, because that was the only ordering property available to him. The modern table uses atomic number, because once protons were discovered (by Rutherford, c. 1917) it became clear that proton count is what really determines an element's chemistry. Many candidates lose marks by saying Mendeleev "arranged elements by atomic number" — that statement is wrong because atomic number was an unknown quantity in 1869.

How Mendeleev built his table

Mendeleev started by writing each known element on a card together with its measured properties — relative atomic mass, density, melting point, the formulae of its common compounds (oxides, chlorides, hydrides), and a description of its reactions. He then laid the cards out and looked for repeating patterns.

Two principles guided the layout:

  1. Order by increasing relative atomic mass. Going from left to right across a row, each element's mass was greater than the one before it.
  2. Group elements with similar properties into vertical columns. When the order by mass produced a row that became too long for the next element to fit the established pattern, Mendeleev started a new row so that elements with similar chemistry lined up underneath each other.

So lithium, sodium and potassium — all soft, low-density metals that react vigorously with water to make a hydroxide and hydrogen — ended up in the same column. Fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine — all reactive non-metals that form compounds with hydrogen and with metals in similar ratios — also ended up in the same column. The vertical columns later became known as groups, and the horizontal rows became periods.

Why Mendeleev arranged the elements by atomic mass

Atomic mass was simply the only ordering property available to him. By 1869 chemists had spent decades carefully measuring the masses of elements relative to hydrogen, and these values were the most reliable numerical fingerprints they had. Atomic number, electronic configuration and even the existence of the electron itself were unknown — the electron was not discovered until 1897 (J.J. Thomson) and the proton not until c. 1917 (Rutherford). Without those concepts, atomic mass was the natural choice.

Crucially, Mendeleev did not order strictly by mass and stop there. He used the chemical properties of the elements and their compounds as a sense-check. If a strict mass-order would have placed an element in a column where it clearly did not belong (because its reactions, its formula of oxide or its formula of chloride did not match), he treated that as evidence that something needed to be adjusted — either by leaving a gap, swapping a pair, or starting a new row earlier.

Why Mendeleev left gaps

When Mendeleev laid out his cards, the chemistry sometimes didn't line up. The next element in mass order might have very different properties from the one above it in the column. Rather than force the wrong element into a slot, Mendeleev left a gap and assumed an element with the right properties existed but had not yet been discovered.

This was a bold step. It meant the table had empty boxes, and Mendeleev was prepared to defend the gaps by predicting the properties of the missing element from the trends in the column above and below it and the rows to either side. (This predictive use of the table is the focus of the next spec point — see 1.2.1.2 Mendeleev's Predictions.)

The reason gaps work is periodicity: properties repeat at regular intervals down a column. So if you can see the property values above and below a gap, you can interpolate the missing element's properties with reasonable confidence.

Why Mendeleev swapped some pairs

A few pairs of elements would not fit neatly into the columns suggested by their atomic masses. The classic example is tellurium (Te) and iodine (I): by atomic mass, tellurium is heavier than iodine, so a strict mass-order would put iodine before tellurium. But iodine's chemistry (a reactive non-metal that forms HI, NaI, etc.) clearly matches the halogens — fluorine, chlorine, bromine — while tellurium's chemistry matches the column containing oxygen, sulfur and selenium.

Mendeleev's solution was to swap them, putting tellurium first and iodine after, even though that broke the mass order. He argued that the chemical evidence outweighed the mass measurement, and that perhaps the masses had been measured slightly inaccurately. We now know that the swap is correct because iodine has more protons (53) than tellurium (52); the mass-order anomaly arises from the unusual mix of isotopes each element has. Tellurium's most abundant isotopes are heavier than iodine's, even though tellurium has fewer protons. Mendeleev had no way of knowing this, but his willingness to trust the chemistry over the number is one of the reasons the table works.

Key features that survived into the modern table

Despite the differences above, three core ideas from Mendeleev's table are still the backbone of the modern arrangement:

  • Horizontal rows showing gradual change. Across each period, properties trend smoothly from metallic on the left to non-metallic on the right — Mendeleev recognised this trend even though he could not explain it.
  • The principle of periodicity itself — that properties repeat at regular intervals when elements are listed in order. This is what makes the periodic table predictive rather than just a list.

Mendeleev's Predictions

Spec point 1.14: Describe how Mendeleev used his table to predict the existence and properties of some elements not then discovered. Foundation and Higher tier · Paper 1.

When Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table in 1869, only about 60 elements were known. Instead of treating his table as a finished list, he treated it as a map with blank spaces, and used the patterns in the elements he could see to predict what should fill the blanks. The accuracy of those predictions — confirmed within 15 years of publication — is the single biggest reason Mendeleev's table was accepted by the scientific community.

This spec point is examined as a "Describe" question worth 2 marks. Examiners want you to explain two things: that Mendeleev left gaps for elements that had not yet been discovered, and that he used the trends shown by neighbouring elements in the same group (and the same period) to predict the properties of those missing elements before anyone had isolated them.

Key Definitions

Why leaving gaps was such a powerful idea

Most earlier scientists (such as Newlands, with his Law of Octaves) tried to fit every known element into a continuous sequence with no breaks. The problem with that approach is that, once you reach an element whose properties don't fit the next slot, the whole pattern falls apart. Newlands forced elements into positions where their chemistry didn't match the rest of the column, and his arrangement was rejected.

Mendeleev took a different decision: rather than force a poor fit, he left the slot empty. For example, when he reached the position directly below silicon in Group 4, he found that the next known element (arsenic) didn't behave like a Group 4 element — it behaved like a Group 5 element. So he put arsenic in Group 5, and left the Group 4 slot blank, predicting that an undiscovered element would one day be found to fill it.

That single decision is what makes Mendeleev's table a genuine scientific theory rather than just a catalogue. A catalogue can only describe what already exists; a theory makes testable predictions about things you have not yet observed. By leaving gaps and predicting properties for them, Mendeleev gave the scientific community something they could go and check experimentally — and if his predictions had turned out to be wrong, his table would have been discarded.

How trends in neighbouring elements let you predict a missing element

Mendeleev's prediction method relied on a simple but powerful idea: properties of the elements change in a regular, predictable way as you move across a period or down a group. If you know the properties of the elements directly above, below, left and right of a gap, you can estimate the properties of whatever element is supposed to sit in that gap by looking at the trend.

There are two complementary trends he used:

  1. Group trends (vertical): Elements in the same group share similar chemical properties because (we now know) they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell. Density, melting point and relative atomic mass all change in a regular pattern down a group. So if you know boron and aluminium and indium sit above and below a gap, you can estimate the missing element's properties by looking at how those three change as you go down.
  2. Period trends (horizontal): Properties also change steadily across a period. By comparing the missing element's left-hand and right-hand neighbours in the same row, you can refine the estimate.

In practice, Mendeleev would average — or sometimes extrapolate — the values of the neighbouring elements. This worked because the periodic patterns are smooth and regular for the elements he was working with. (We now understand that this regularity comes from the gradual filling of electron shells across a period and the addition of new shells down a group, but Mendeleev did not know this — he was simply trusting the patterns.)

How trends are used — step-by-step procedure

If a question gives you a small section of the periodic table with one element missing and asks you to predict its properties, follow this routine:

  1. Identify what surrounds the gap — the element directly above, directly below, and the elements immediately to the left and right.
  2. Read off the property values for those surrounding elements (the question will give them).
  3. Look for the trend — are the values increasing or decreasing as you go down the group? Increasing or decreasing as you go across the period?
  4. Extrapolate or interpolate — for a property like relative atomic mass, take a value that fits between the element above and the element below.
  5. State both ideas in your answer — that Mendeleev left a gap AND that he used trends in the surrounding elements to predict the properties.

Why the predictions confirmed Mendeleev's arrangement

Predictions are scientifically powerful because they can fail. If Mendeleev had been wrong about the existence of eka-aluminium, eka-boron or eka-silicon, or had been badly wrong about their properties, his table would have been quietly forgotten. Instead, every one of his major predictions was confirmed within 15 years of publication.

This matters for two reasons:

  1. Confidence in the structure of the table: Once gallium, scandium and germanium were found and matched their predictions, chemists trusted Mendeleev's overall arrangement — including the places where he had broken his own ordering rule (e.g. swapping the order of elements like tellurium and iodine when chemistry demanded it).
  2. Evidence that scientific theories make predictions: A theory that only describes what is already known is weaker than one that predicts what is not yet known. Mendeleev's predictions, and their confirmation, are a textbook example of how scientific ideas earn acceptance.

Problems with Mendeleev's Table

Spec point 1.15: "Explain that Mendeleev thought he had arranged elements in order of increasing relative atomic mass but this was not always so because of the relative abundance of isotopes of some pairs of elements in the periodic table." Examined on Foundation and Higher tier, on Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 1.

Dmitri Mendeleev published his periodic table in 1869. He arranged the elements in horizontal rows of increasing relative atomic mass, then grouped together elements that showed similar chemical behaviour — alkali metals in one column, halogens in another, and so on. The table was a triumph: it pulled the known elements into a structured pattern, predicted properties of elements that had not yet been isolated, and explained why some elements behaved as a "family". For decades it was treated as an almost finished piece of chemistry.

But on close inspection, Mendeleev's stated rule — increasing relative atomic mass left to right — quietly broke down at several specific places in the table. A handful of element pairs sat in what looked like the wrong order if you took the masses literally. For Mendeleev, this was a real puzzle. He could not explain it from first principles, because the existence of subatomic particles, and of isotopes, was not yet known. The neutron was not even discovered until 1932 — more than 60 years after his table was published.

The explanation only emerged once chemists understood isotopes and how they affect relative atomic mass. The fix was to re-order the table by atomic number (the number of protons) instead of by relative atomic mass. Once that change was made, every element fell into the chemically correct place, and the apparent swap-pair anomalies disappeared.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Relative atomic mass (Ar)The weighted mean (average) mass of the atoms of an element, taking into account the abundance of each of its isotopes, on a scale where carbon-12 has a mass of exactly 12
IsotopeAtoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers
Atomic number (Z)The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom; this defines which element the atom is and is the value used to order the modern periodic table
Mass number (A)The total number of protons plus neutrons in the nucleus of a single atom (always a whole number for one specific isotope)
Relative isotopic abundanceThe percentage of atoms of an element that are a particular isotope, as found in a natural sample
Swap-pair (or pair-reversal)An adjacent pair of elements in the modern periodic table where the lighter-Ar element is placed after the heavier-Ar element, because ordering is by atomic number rather than mass

Why Mendeleev's mass-order rule broke down

Mendeleev believed every element had one fixed atomic mass, and that these masses, lined up in order, would produce the correct chemical pattern. The problem is that relative atomic mass is not actually a property of a single atom — it is a weighted average across all the naturally occurring isotopes of that element.

For most elements, the heavier element (more protons) does also have the heavier average atomic mass, so the rules agree. But for a handful of pairs, the lighter element (fewer protons) happens to have a higher average atomic mass because its naturally occurring isotopes are, on average, unusually heavy — or because the heavier element's isotopes are unusually light. In those cases, ordering strictly by Ar puts the elements in the wrong chemical group.

Mendeleev sometimes spotted this and quietly placed an element where chemistry told him it belonged, even if mass alone said otherwise. He did this for iodine and tellurium: the chemistry of iodine clearly matched the halogens (chlorine, bromine), and tellurium clearly matched the oxygen group, so iodine had to come after tellurium even though iodine has the smaller Ar. He could justify the swap pragmatically, but he could not give a deep reason for it.

Why some Ar values are not whole numbers

A clue to the underlying problem is sitting on every periodic table you look at: relative atomic masses are usually not whole numbers. Chlorine's Ar is roughly 35.5, not 35 or 36. Copper's is 63.5. These decimals are the fingerprint of isotopes.

A pure sample of chlorine is actually a mixture of two isotopes: chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. Around three-quarters of chlorine atoms are the lighter Cl-35, and around one-quarter are the heavier Cl-37. The weighted average of those two masses is:

`` Ar(Cl) = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) / 100 = (2625 + 925) / 100 = 3550 / 100 = 35.5 ``

That 35.5 is not the mass of any single chlorine atom — no chlorine atom weighs 35.5 units. It is the average across the naturally occurring mixture, and that is the number Mendeleev would have measured experimentally.

The same logic applies to every element. If an element has just one stable isotope, its Ar is essentially a whole number. If it has two or more isotopes in different abundances, its Ar is a weighted mean that depends on which isotopes are present and in what proportion. That proportion is fixed by nature — but it has nothing to do with how many protons the element has, which is what really determines the chemistry.

So the deeper reason Mendeleev's rule failed is that he was trying to use a property of the bulk sample (Ar, which depends on isotope abundance) to predict a property of the individual atom (chemical behaviour, which depends on the number of protons and electrons). Most of the time these two ways of ordering give the same answer. Sometimes they don't.

The pair-reversal examples

The three most commonly cited swap-pairs in the modern table are argon and potassium, tellurium and iodine, and cobalt and nickel. In each case, the modern table places them in the chemically correct order — but in reverse order of relative atomic mass.

Read straight off the masses, every one of these pairs looks "out of order" — argon is heavier than potassium, tellurium is heavier than iodine, cobalt is heavier than nickel. By Mendeleev's stated rule, the lighter element should come second in each pair, and yet the chemistry clearly demands the opposite arrangement.

Argon and potassium. Argon (Ar = 39.9) is a noble gas — completely unreactive, with full outer electron shells. Potassium (Ar = 39.1) is an alkali metal — extremely reactive, reacting violently with water. They cannot possibly belong in each other's groups. The modern table places argon in Group 0 with the other noble gases and potassium in Group 1 with the other alkali metals, even though that puts the slightly lighter element second. This is correct, because argon has 18 protons and potassium has 19 — atomic number puts them in the right order.

The reason argon's Ar is unusually high is that its main natural isotope is argon-40, which makes up over 99% of natural argon. Potassium, by contrast, is dominated by potassium-39 with only a tiny fraction of heavier isotopes. So argon's average mass is dragged up and potassium's is held down.

Tellurium and iodine. Tellurium (Ar = 127.6) sits in Group 6 with oxygen, sulfur and selenium. Iodine (Ar = 126.9) sits in Group 7 with the other halogens — fluorine, chlorine, bromine. If the table were strictly mass-ordered, iodine would have to sit in Group 6 and tellurium in Group 7, which would put a non-metal halogen in a different family from the other halogens and break the periodicity entirely. Instead, the modern table uses atomic number — tellurium is element 52 and iodine is element 53 — and the chemistry works out correctly. Mendeleev himself swapped these two by hand, even though he could not justify it from his rule.

Cobalt and nickel. Cobalt (Ar = 58.9) and nickel (Ar = 58.7) are both transition metals, and the difference in mass between them is small. Cobalt has 27 protons and nickel has 28, so cobalt comes first by atomic number. Their chemical behaviour follows the atomic-number order — cobalt salts and nickel salts behave consistently with their positions in the d-block — confirming again that protons, not average mass, set the order.

This is the heart of the answer to any "Explain why…" question on this spec point: Mendeleev arranged elements by mass; the modern table arranges them by atomic number; the two orderings disagree at certain pairs because the relative atomic mass of an element depends on the abundance of its isotopes, not just on the number of protons.

Worked example: showing why argon's Ar exceeds potassium's

You can demonstrate the effect of isotope abundance with a simplified Ar calculation. Argon has three stable isotopes — Ar-36, Ar-38 and Ar-40 — but its abundance is overwhelmingly Ar-40 (around 99.6%):

`` Ar(argon) ≈ (36 × 0.34 + 38 × 0.06 + 40 × 99.60) / 100 ≈ (12.2 + 2.3 + 3984.0) / 100 ≈ 3998.5 / 100 ≈ 40.0 ``

Potassium also has three isotopes (K-39, K-40, K-41) but is dominated by K-39 (around 93.3%):

`` Ar(potassium) ≈ (39 × 93.3 + 40 × 0.01 + 41 × 6.7) / 100 ≈ (3638.7 + 0.4 + 274.7) / 100 ≈ 3913.8 / 100 ≈ 39.1 ``

So argon's average mass comes out close to 40, while potassium's comes out close to 39. Argon ends up heavier — even though it has one fewer proton — purely because its dominant isotope (Ar-40) is heavier than potassium's dominant isotope (K-39). Use of atomic number sidesteps this completely: argon (18 protons) comes before potassium (19 protons), and the chemistry then works.

You will not normally be asked to perform this calculation as part of a 1.15 question — relative atomic mass calculations sit on the Higher tier in a different spec point. But understanding the logic explains why the swap exists, which is exactly what an Explain question on 1.15 is asking for.

Always include the words isotope and atomic number in your answer.

1.2.2.1 The Modern Periodic Table F+H ▼

The Modern Periodic Table

Spec 1.16 — Explain the meaning of atomic number of an element in terms of position in the periodic table and number of protons in the nucleus.

Spec 1.17 — Describe that in the periodic table: (a) elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number, in rows called periods; (b) elements with similar properties are placed in the same vertical columns called groups.

This spec point is examinable at both Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 1, on Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0). It underpins almost every periodic-table question on the paper, so getting the language exactly right — especially the difference between group and period — is essential.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Atomic numberThe number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. Each element has its own unique atomic number.
GroupA vertical column in the periodic table. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell.
PeriodA horizontal row in the periodic table. Elements in the same period have the same number of occupied electron shells.
Periodic tableA table of all known elements arranged in order of increasing atomic number, with elements of similar properties placed in the same vertical column.
PeriodicityThe repeating pattern of physical and chemical properties seen as you move across the periodic table from one period to the next.

How the modern periodic table is arranged

In the modern periodic table, every element is placed in order of increasing atomic number. Hydrogen sits first with atomic number 1, helium next with atomic number 2, lithium with 3, and so on. Reading left-to-right along a row, the atomic number always goes up by one with each step.

The table is then split into rows and columns:

  • The horizontal rows are called periods. They are numbered 1 to 7 from top to bottom.
  • The vertical columns are called groups. The main groups are numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 0 (sometimes labelled 8). The block of elements between Group 2 and Group 3 is the transition metals — these are not given group numbers at GCSE level.

Because elements are slotted in by atomic number, every element has a unique position. There is no overlap, no gap, and no ambiguity about where an element belongs.

Why atomic number, not atomic mass, is the modern ordering principle

The original 19th-century periodic table built by Dmitri Mendeleev arranged elements in order of increasing atomic mass. This worked for most elements but produced a few problems — for example, tellurium (Te) is heavier than iodine (I), but iodine clearly behaves chemically like the other halogens (F, Cl, Br) above it, while tellurium clearly behaves like the Group 6 elements (O, S, Se) above it. To keep these two elements with their chemical "families", Mendeleev had to swap them out of mass order.

The modern arrangement removes that problem. Elements are ordered by atomic number (the proton count), and the chemical-family pattern emerges naturally without any swaps. This is because chemical behaviour is determined by electrons, and the number of electrons in a neutral atom equals the number of protons — so ordering by proton count is the same as ordering by electron count, which is what governs chemistry.

The number of protons is also fundamental in a way that mass is not: the proton number defines what element you are looking at. Carbon is carbon because it has six protons. Change the proton count and you change the element. Atomic mass, by contrast, can vary slightly between atoms of the same element because of isotopes (atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons), so it is a less reliable foundation for an ordering system.

Why elements in the same group have similar properties

Chemical behaviour is controlled almost entirely by an atom's outer electrons — the electrons in its highest-energy shell. When you write out the electronic configurations of the elements in any given group, a clear pattern appears: every element in that group has the same number of electrons in its outer shell.

For example, in Group 1:

  • Lithium (Li): 2,1
  • Sodium (Na): 2,8,1
  • Potassium (K): 2,8,8,1

Each of these alkali metals has one outer-shell electron. When they react, that single outer electron is lost, forming a 1+ ion. Because they all start with one outer electron, they all undergo the same kind of reaction — reacting vigorously with water to form an alkaline metal hydroxide and hydrogen, for instance. Their chemistry is similar because their outer-shell arrangement is similar.

The same logic applies to Group 7 (the halogens), where every element has seven outer-shell electrons and tends to gain one electron to form a 1– ion, and to Group 0 (the noble gases), where every element has a full outer shell and so is unreactive.

The group number is therefore a quick guide to the number of outer-shell electrons (for the main groups: Group 1 has 1, Group 2 has 2, Group 3 has 3 outer electrons, and so on, up to Group 7 with 7; Group 0 has a full outer shell, usually 8 electrons, except for helium which has 2).

The period number tells you how many electron shells are occupied. A Period 2 element has 2 occupied shells; a Period 3 element has 3 occupied shells. Combining the two pieces of information lets you write out an element's full electron arrangement straight from its position in the table.

Worked example — finding an element's group and period

A Foundation-tier exam stem might ask: "State in which group and in which period of the periodic table silicon is found."

Step 1 — Locate the element. Find silicon (chemical symbol Si, atomic number 14) on the periodic table provided in the data sheet.

Step 2 — Read across to find the period. Silicon sits in the third row from the top. Therefore silicon is in period 3.

Step 3 — Read up the column to find the group. Silicon sits in the same vertical column as carbon (above it) and germanium (below it). This column is group 4.

Final answer: Silicon is in group 4 and period 3.

You can sense-check this against the electronic configuration. Silicon has 14 electrons arranged 2,8,4 — three occupied shells (period 3) and four outer-shell electrons (group 4). The arrangement matches the position.

1.2.2.2 Metals and Non-Metals in the Periodic Table F+H ▼

Metals and Non-Metals in the Periodic Table

Spec point 1.18: Identify elements as metals or non-metals according to their position in the periodic table, explaining this division in terms of the atomic structures of the elements. Foundation + Higher tier; on both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science).

The single most useful piece of information the periodic table gives you at GCSE is whether an element is a metal or a non-metal. That one classification predicts the type of bonding the element forms, the kind of ions it makes, the appearance and physical properties of the element, and the acid–base behaviour of its oxide. The position of an element on the table tells you everything — but you need to know exactly where the dividing line sits and why it sits there.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
MetalAn element that loses electrons from its outer shell to form positive ions; usually shiny, malleable, and a good conductor of heat and electricity
Non-metalAn element that gains electrons (or shares them) to form negative ions or covalent bonds; typically dull, brittle when solid, and a poor conductor of heat and electricity
MetalloidAn element along the dividing "staircase" line whose properties are intermediate between metals and non-metals (e.g. silicon, germanium); not separately examined at Edexcel GCSE but useful for context
Outer (valence) shellThe highest-energy electron shell of an atom; the electrons here are the ones involved in chemical bonding
IonisationThe process of removing one or more electrons from an atom to form a positive ion; metals undergo ionisation easily, non-metals do not
Electron gainThe process of adding one or more electrons to an atom to form a negative ion; non-metals undergo electron gain easily, metals do not

Where the dividing line sits

If you look at a periodic table, the metal/non-metal boundary is drawn as a stepped or staircase line running diagonally from between boron (B) and aluminium (Al) at the top, down through the p-block, finishing between polonium (Po) and astatine (At) near the bottom.

  • Everything to the left of the staircase line is a metal.
  • Everything to the right of the staircase line is a non-metal.
  • The elements that sit immediately on or next to the line — boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony, tellurium — are sometimes called metalloids because their behaviour is intermediate. At Edexcel GCSE you are expected to classify these alongside the non-metals unless told otherwise.

This means the vast majority of the periodic table is metallic. Roughly 80% of all elements are metals — including all of Groups 1 and 2, all of the transition metals in the central block, and most of the heavier p-block elements (aluminium, tin, lead, etc.). The non-metals are concentrated in the top right corner: hydrogen (an exception, sitting in Group 1 but behaving as a non-metal at GCSE), carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, sulfur, the halogens of Group 7, and the noble gases of Group 0.

Why the divide exists — atomic structure reasoning

The metal/non-metal divide is not arbitrary. It comes directly from how many electrons an element has in its outer shell and how strongly those electrons are held by the nucleus. The two go hand in hand: position on the periodic table determines outer-shell occupancy, which determines whether the atom prefers to lose or gain electrons.

*Why metals lose electrons easily*

Metals sit on the left of the table. Their atoms have a small number of electrons in the outer shell — typically 1, 2, or 3. Two factors make these outer electrons easy to remove:

  1. Few electrons to lose. Removing 1 or 2 electrons gets the atom to a stable, full-shell electron configuration (matching the noble gas in the period above). Losing 1 or 2 electrons is energetically much cheaper than gaining 6 or 7 to reach the same goal.
  2. The outer electrons are weakly held. In a metal, the outer shell is relatively far from the positively charged nucleus and is shielded by the inner shells of electrons. The electrostatic pull from the nucleus on those outer electrons is therefore weak, so they can be lost without too much energy input.

The result: metals readily form positive ions (cations) with charges of +1, +2, or +3. Sodium loses 1 electron to form Na⁺; magnesium loses 2 to form Mg²⁺; aluminium loses 3 to form Al³⁺. This is why metals are described as electropositive.

*Why non-metals gain electrons easily*

Non-metals sit on the right of the table. Their atoms have a large number of electrons in the outer shell — typically 5, 6, or 7. The reasoning runs in the opposite direction to the metals:

  1. Few electrons to gain. A non-metal with 6 or 7 outer-shell electrons only needs to gain 1 or 2 more to reach a full outer shell (the noble gas configuration). Gaining 1 or 2 electrons is much cheaper than losing 6 or 7.
  2. The outer shell is closer to the nucleus and less shielded (compared to a metal in the same period). The nucleus pulls strongly on incoming electrons, so the atom holds onto its existing outer electrons firmly and attracts new ones.

The result: non-metals readily form negative ions (anions) with charges of −1, −2, or −3. Chlorine gains 1 electron to form Cl⁻; oxygen gains 2 to form O²⁻; nitrogen gains 3 to form N³⁻. This is why non-metals are described as electronegative.

*The Group 0 exception*

The noble gases (Group 0) are non-metals but they form no ions at all under normal conditions. Their outer shell is already full, so there is no energetic incentive to gain or lose electrons. They sit on the right of the table for that reason.

The acid–base behaviour of oxides is one of the most important diagnostic features. If you are told an oxide reacts with hydrochloric acid to form a salt and water, you can deduce the parent element is a metal. If the oxide reacts with sodium hydroxide to form a salt and water, the parent element is a non-metal.

Worked example: classifying an unknown element

A student is told that an element X has the following properties:

  • It is a shiny solid at room temperature.
  • It conducts electricity.
  • It can be hammered into a thin sheet.
  • Its oxide reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to form a salt and water.

Step 1 — interpret the physical properties. Shiny, conducts electricity, malleable: all classic metallic properties.

Step 2 — interpret the chemical behaviour. An oxide that reacts with an acid is a basic oxide. Basic oxides come from metals.

Step 3 — conclude. Element X is a metal, so it sits to the left of the staircase line on the periodic table.

A second worked example reverses the logic. An element Y has 6 electrons in its outer shell. To reach a full outer shell, it would need to either lose 6 electrons (very energetically expensive) or gain 2 electrons (much cheaper). It will therefore gain 2 electrons to form a Y²⁻ ion. An atom that gains electrons is a non-metal, so element Y sits to the right of the staircase line. (This is the reasoning that places oxygen and sulfur in Group 6 as non-metals.)

1.2.3.1 Electronic Configurations F+H ▼

Electronic Configurations

Spec 1.19 — Predict the electronic configurations of the first 20 elements in the periodic table as diagrams and in the form, for example 2.8.1.

Spec 1.20 — Explain how the electronic configuration of an element is related to its position in the periodic table.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Electron shellA region around the nucleus that holds a fixed maximum number of electrons at a particular energy level (sometimes called an "energy level" or "orbit")
Electron configurationA description of how an atom's electrons are arranged into shells, written as numbers separated by full stops (e.g. 2.8.1 for sodium)
Energy levelThe fixed amount of energy an electron in a particular shell has; shells closer to the nucleus are at lower energy levels than those further away
Dot-and-cross diagramA model that shows the electrons in each shell of an atom (or molecule) using dots and crosses arranged on concentric circles around a central nucleus

How electron shells are arranged

Every atom has a tiny, dense nucleus made of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. The electrons are not scattered randomly — they occupy fixed regions called shells (also called energy levels). The first shell sits closest to the nucleus, the second sits further out, and the third sits further out still.

Each shell can hold only a fixed number of electrons before it is full:

  • The first shell holds a maximum of 2 electrons
  • The second shell holds a maximum of 8 electrons
  • The third shell holds a maximum of 8 electrons (for the first 20 elements you study at GCSE)
  • The fourth shell begins to fill once the third has reached 8

This sequence — 2, 8, 8, 2 — covers every element from hydrogen (H, atomic number 1) through to calcium (Ca, atomic number 20), which is the full range you need to be able to predict at GCSE.

Why electrons fill shells from the inside out

Electrons carry a negative charge and are attracted to the positive protons in the nucleus. The closer a shell sits to the nucleus, the stronger that attraction and the lower the energy of an electron sitting in it. Nature always favours arrangements with the lowest possible energy, so electrons fill the first (innermost, lowest-energy) shell before any electron occupies the second shell. Only when the first shell is completely full do electrons begin to populate the second shell, and so on.

This rule is called the filling order, and it has no exceptions for the first 20 elements. It is why you never see an arrangement like 1.8.1 for sodium — putting only one electron in the first shell when a second one could fit there at lower energy would be a higher-energy, less stable arrangement.

Why each shell has a maximum capacity

The fixed shell capacities (2, then 8, then 8 for the elements you meet at GCSE) come from the underlying quantum-mechanical structure of the atom. At GCSE you do not need to know the deep reason — you only need to know the numbers and apply them — but the short version is that each shell is built from a fixed number of sub-regions (called orbitals), and each orbital can hold a maximum of two electrons. The first shell has one orbital (capacity 2), and the second and third shells (at GCSE level) effectively offer space for eight more electrons each before the next shell starts.

The practical upshot: when you are writing an electronic configuration, you keep adding electrons to the current shell until you hit its capacity, then you move outwards.

Writing electron configurations: the 2.8.1 form

The Edexcel specification asks you to write electron configurations using a numerical shorthand: the number of electrons in each shell, separated by full stops, starting from the innermost shell.

The procedure is:

  1. Find the atomic number of the element — this tells you the total number of electrons in a neutral atom (because protons = electrons in a neutral atom).
  2. Fill the first shell with up to 2 electrons.
  3. Fill the second shell with up to 8 electrons.
  4. Fill the third shell with up to 8 electrons.
  5. Place any remaining electrons in the fourth shell.

Worked example — chlorine (Cl, atomic number 17):

  • Total electrons = 17
  • Shell 1: 2 electrons (15 left to place)
  • Shell 2: 8 electrons (7 left to place)
  • Shell 3: 7 electrons (0 left to place)
  • Electronic configuration: 2.8.7

This places chlorine correctly: period 3 (three shells used) and group 7 (seven outer-shell electrons), which matches its position in the periodic table.

Dot-and-cross diagrams

A dot-and-cross diagram shows the same information visually. The nucleus sits at the centre (often labelled with the element symbol or atomic number), and the electrons in each shell are drawn as dots or crosses arranged on concentric circles, one circle per shell.

For chlorine (2.8.7) the diagram has:

  • A central nucleus labelled "Cl" (or with the proton/neutron count if asked)
  • An inner circle with 2 electrons
  • A middle circle with 8 electrons
  • An outer circle with 7 electrons

Conventions to follow when drawing:

  • Pair electrons up around the shell only after each "compass point" (top, right, bottom, left) has at least one electron, then continue pairing — though many GCSE mark schemes accept any arrangement as long as the totals are correct.
  • Show the correct total in each shell — that is what gains the mark.
  • Label the central nucleus.

A correctly drawn dot-and-cross diagram is an accepted alternative to the 2.8.7 numerical form (see Examiner Report Insights below) — but the spec's preferred shorthand is the numerical 2.8.1 form, so default to writing it that way unless the question specifically asks for a diagram.

Why period number = number of shells

Each new period (row) of the periodic table begins when the previous shell has been filled and an electron is added to a brand-new outer shell. Hydrogen and helium fill the first shell, so they sit in period 1. Lithium (2.1) is the first element to start the second shell, so it begins period 2. Sodium (2.8.1) is the first element to start the third shell, so it begins period 3. Potassium (2.8.8.1) is the first to start the fourth shell, so it begins period 4.

This is why the period number tells you the number of shells the atom uses. If you can write an element's electronic configuration, you can immediately read off its period: count the numbers (or count the full stops and add one).

Why group number = number of outer-shell electrons

The group (column) of the periodic table is set by the number of electrons in the outer shell only (the outer-shell electrons are sometimes called the valence electrons). Sodium has the configuration 2.8.1 — one electron in the outer shell — and sits in group 1. Magnesium (2.8.2) has two outer-shell electrons and sits in group 2. Chlorine (2.8.7) has seven outer-shell electrons and sits in group 7. Argon (2.8.8) has a full outer shell of eight and sits in group 0 (the noble gases).

The chemistry of an element is dominated by its outer-shell electrons, because these are the ones involved in forming bonds and reacting with other atoms. That is why all elements in the same group behave in similar ways: they have the same number of outer-shell electrons and therefore react in similar ways. Group 1 metals all lose one outer-shell electron readily; group 7 non-metals all need to gain one electron to fill their outer shell.

Worked example — deducing position from configuration (Ca)

A very common exam task — directly mirroring the November 2020 1CH0/1H Q8(a)(ii) question — is to be given an electronic configuration and asked to state where the element sits in the periodic table.

Example: An element has the electronic configuration 2.8.8.2. Explain its position in the periodic table.

Procedure:

  1. Count the shells. There are four numbers (2, 8, 8, 2), so the atom uses four shells → the element is in period 4.
  2. Read the outer-shell electrons. The last number is 2, so the atom has two outer-shell electrons → the element is in group 2.
  3. Identify the element if asked. Period 4, group 2 = calcium (Ca).

Marking points: each of the two reasoning steps (shells → period; outer-shell electrons → group) is worth one mark in a typical 2-mark "Explain how the electronic configuration is related to its position" question.

A critical wording trap is flagged in the November 2020 examiner report (see Examiner Report Insights): saying "calcium has four outer shells of electrons" is not credit-worthy. There is only one outer shell — the outermost one. The correct statement is "calcium has four shells of electrons" (which gives the period) and separately "calcium has two electrons in its outer shell" (which gives the group). Keep the two ideas distinct in your wording.

Worked example — predicting the configuration of an element from its atomic number (Cl)

Example: Predict the electronic configuration of chlorine (atomic number 17).

  1. Total electrons in a neutral chlorine atom = atomic number = 17.
  2. Fill shell 1: place 2 electrons (15 remaining).
  3. Fill shell 2: place 8 electrons (7 remaining).
  4. Fill shell 3: place the remaining 7 electrons.
  5. Configuration: 2.8.7.

Cross-check: 2 + 8 + 7 = 17 ✓. Three numbers means three shells → period 3 ✓. Final number 7 → group 7 ✓.

What this tells you: a correctly drawn dot-and-cross (electron shell) diagram is an accepted alternative to the 2.8.1-form numerical answer. Examiners credited both. However, this is a "fall-back" — the spec's preferred form is the numerical 2.8.1 notation, so default to writing it that way unless the question explicitly asks for a diagram. Drawing a diagram takes longer and gives more chances to make a numerical slip.

*1CH0/1H — November 2020 Examiner Report (Q8(a)(ii)):** *"A good proportion of learners performed well in question 8(a)(ii) with many being able to explain that calcium would be found in period 4 of the periodic table because it has four shells of electrons. In some cases, candidates lost the second mark point as they stated that calcium has four outer shells of electrons which was rejected. Where candidates did not score, it was often because they did not read the question carefully and stated what group rather than what period of the periodic table calcium is found in."

Two distinct mistakes flagged here:

  1. "Four outer shells" was rejected. An atom only has one outer shell (the outermost one). The correct phrasing is either "four shells of electrons" (which justifies the period) or "two electrons in its outer shell" (which justifies the group). Key Takeaways
  1. The first 20 elements have electrons arranged in shells with the capacity sequence 2, 8, 8, 2 — first shell holds 2, second holds 8, third holds 8, fourth begins after the third reaches 8.
  2. Electrons always fill shells from the innermost outwards, because the inner shells are at lower energy and are more strongly attracted to the nucleus.
  3. The electronic configuration is written as numbers separated by full stops (e.g. sodium = 2.8.1), starting from the innermost shell.
  4. To predict an electronic configuration: take the atomic number (= number of electrons in a neutral atom) and fill shells in order, respecting the 2-8-8-2 capacities.
  5. A dot-and-cross shell diagram showing the correct totals in each shell is an accepted alternative to the 2.8.1 numerical form, but the numerical form is the spec's preferred shorthand.
  6. The period number of an element equals the number of shells its atom uses (e.g. calcium has four shells, so it is in period 4).
  7. The group number of an element equals the number of electrons in its outer shell (e.g. calcium has 2 outer-shell electrons, so it is in group 2; chlorine has 7, so it is in group 7).
  8. Helium is in group 0 despite having a full outer shell of 2 electrons — it is grouped with the other noble gases because it behaves chemically like them.
  9. Common exam wording trap (Nov 2020 1H): saying an element has "four outer shells" is not credit-worthy — an atom has only one outer shell. Correct phrasing is "four shells" (justifies period) and "two electrons in its outer shell" (justifies group).
  10. Common exam wording trap (Nov 2020 1H): always check whether the question asks for the period or the group — a significant share of candidates lose marks by giving the wrong one.
  11. Spec 1.19 is typically tested as a 1-mark "predict / give the electronic configuration" item; spec 1.20 is typically tested as a 2-mark "explain" item linking configuration to position.
  12. Elements in the same group all have the same number of outer-shell electrons, which is why they react in similar ways — the foundation for the periodic patterns you study in Group 1, Group 7 and Group 0.
