Reactivity of Metals

Spec 4.1 — Deduce the relative reactivity of some metals, by their reactions with water, acids and salt solutions. Spec 4.3 — Explain the reactivity series in terms of reactions with water and dilute acids, and the tendency of metal atoms to form cations. Spec 4.9 — Explain how a metal's relative resistance to oxidation is related to its position in the reactivity series. Examined on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F + 1CH0/2H), both Separate (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

Key Definitions

The Reactivity Series (memorise this order)

The reactivity series is the canonical order you must learn. Carbon and hydrogen are not metals but are included as reference points — carbon because it is used to extract metals less reactive than itself, and hydrogen because acids release H⁺ ions, so any metal above hydrogen will react with a dilute acid.

Tendency to Form Cations — the Reason for the Order

A metal atom reacts by losing its outer electrons to form a positive ion (a cation). For example:

  • Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
  • Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
  • Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻

The more readily a metal loses its outer electrons, the higher up the reactivity series it sits. Potassium and sodium have a single outer electron that is far from the nucleus and weakly held, so they lose it very easily. Gold and silver hold on to their outer electrons strongly, so they do not form cations easily and are described as "unreactive". This is why the reactivity series can be thought of as an oxidation series — moving up the series means an increasing tendency to be oxidised (to lose electrons).

This same idea explains why metals are found differently in the Earth's crust. Very reactive metals (K, Na, Ca) are only ever found as compounds because they readily form cations and bond to non-metals. Unreactive metals (Au, sometimes Ag and Cu) can be found as the uncombined (native) element because they do not readily lose electrons to oxygen, water or other elements.

  • Ca (s) + 2H₂O (l) → Ca(OH)₂ (aq) + H₂ (g)
  • Mg (s) + H₂O (g) → MgO (s) + H₂ (g) (with steam)

Reactions of Metals with Dilute Acids

Any metal above hydrogen in the reactivity series reacts with a dilute acid to give a salt and hydrogen gas:

metal + acid → salt + hydrogen

The salt depends on the acid: hydrochloric acid gives a chloride, sulfuric acid gives a sulfate, nitric acid gives a nitrate.

Worked equations:

  • Mg (s) + 2HCl (aq) → MgCl₂ (aq) + H₂ (g)
  • Zn (s) + H₂SO₄ (aq) → ZnSO₄ (aq) + H₂ (g)
  • Fe (s) + 2HCl (aq) → FeCl₂ (aq) + H₂ (g)

You can rank metals from observations of these reactions by measuring how quickly bubbles are produced, how quickly the temperature rises, or how quickly the metal disappears. A metal that reacts faster and gives a bigger temperature rise is more reactive.

Displacement: Reactions of Metals with Salt Solutions

A more reactive metal will displace a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt. The more reactive metal forms a cation (it is oxidised); the less reactive metal ion in solution gains those electrons and is deposited as the metal (it is reduced).

General pattern:

metal A + salt of metal B → salt of metal A + metal B (only if A is more reactive than B)

Worked equations:

  • Zn (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → ZnSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)
  • Mg (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → MgSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)
  • Fe (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → FeSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)

Typical observations when zinc is dropped into blue copper(II) sulfate solution:

  • The blue colour fades as Cu²⁺ ions are removed from solution
  • A pink-brown copper coating forms on the surface of the zinc
  • The test tube warms up (the reaction is exothermic)

If you swap the metals — copper added to zinc sulfate solution — nothing happens. Copper is less reactive than zinc, so it cannot displace it. A "no reaction" result is just as informative as a positive result for ranking metals.

Practical: Determining a Reactivity Series by Displacement

This is the classic Edexcel practical and a frequent exam question. The method:

  1. Choose your set of metals (e.g. magnesium, zinc, iron, copper) cut into small, similar-sized pieces, and lightly sand them to remove the oxide layer.
  2. Prepare small volumes (about 5 cm³) of each salt solution in a test tube rack — typically magnesium sulfate, zinc sulfate, iron(II) sulfate, copper(II) sulfate.
  3. Add one piece of each metal to each salt solution (so you build a 4 × 4 grid of test tubes — including the "metal in its own salt solution" controls).
  4. Leave for several minutes. Record observations for every combination: colour change of the solution, coating on the metal, temperature change (a thermometer or hand-test of each tube), bubbles, or "no change".
  5. Tabulate the results. A reaction (✓) means the metal in the row is more reactive than the metal whose salt is in the column. "No reaction" (✗) means the row metal is less reactive.
  6. Order the metals from the row that reacts with the most salts (most reactive) down to the row that reacts with none (least reactive).

Sample results table for Mg, Zn, Fe, Cu:

Metal addedMgSO₄ (aq)ZnSO₄ (aq)FeSO₄ (aq)CuSO₄ (aq)Reactions seen
Mg—✓ (grey coat)✓ (grey coat, warms)✓ (pink coat, blue fades, warms)3
Zn✗—✓ (grey coat)✓ (pink coat, blue fades)2
Fe✗✗—✓ (pink coat, blue fades)1
Cu✗✗✗—0

Order from most to least reactive: Mg > Zn > Fe > Cu, matching the established series.

To make this a fair test: same surface area / mass of metal, same volume and concentration of salt solution, same temperature, same time of observation, and remove the surface oxide layer before adding the metals.

Resistance to Oxidation and Position in the Series (Spec 4.9)

Oxidation in this topic means a metal atom losing electrons — usually to oxygen in the air, forming a metal oxide on the surface. The more reactive a metal is (the higher up the series), the more readily it is oxidised — so the less resistant it is to oxidation.

  • Potassium, sodium: Tarnish (lose their shiny appearance) within seconds when freshly cut and exposed to air. Stored under oil in the lab to keep oxygen and water away.
  • Calcium, magnesium: Tarnish over hours/days, forming a dull oxide layer.
  • Aluminium: Reacts rapidly with oxygen but forms a thin, tough, transparent Al₂O₃ layer that sticks to the surface and stops further reaction — so aluminium looks unreactive even though it is moderately reactive.
  • Iron: Rusts in damp air (iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide). The rust flakes off, exposing fresh metal, so the rusting continues — this is why iron is so vulnerable to oxidation.
  • Copper: Slowly tarnishes to a dull brown CuO; over decades develops a green carbonate/sulfate patina. Used widely in plumbing and electrical wiring because it resists corrosion well enough for everyday use.
  • Silver: Resists oxidation in air but slowly tarnishes black with sulfur compounds (Ag₂S).
  • Gold: Does not tarnish or react with oxygen at all under normal conditions — this is why gold jewellery and gold coins keep their shine over thousands of years.

So when you are asked why gold, silver and copper are used in jewellery, electrical contacts or coins, the chemistry answer is: they are low in the reactivity series, so they have a high resistance to oxidation and do not corrode in air.

Key Takeaways
  1. The reactivity series in order is: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, (hydrogen), copper, silver, gold — memorise this order, including the positions of carbon and hydrogen.
  2. The more reactive a metal, the more readily its atoms lose their outer electrons to form positive ions (cations); the reactivity series is effectively an oxidation series.
  3. Metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. K, Na and Ca react vigorously with cold water; Mg reacts very slowly with cold water but rapidly with steam; Al, Zn, Fe react only with steam; Cu, Ag, Au do not react.
  4. Metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen, but only for metals above hydrogen. K, Na and Ca are far too reactive to test with acid safely in a school lab; Mg, Al, Zn and Fe react at decreasing rates; Cu, Ag and Au do not react.
  5. In a displacement reaction, a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt, e.g. Zn (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → ZnSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s).
  6. A reactivity series can be deduced by mixing each metal with each salt solution, recording observations (colour change, metal coating, temperature change, bubbles), and ranking each metal by the number of salts it can react with.
  7. When describing this practical in an exam, name the metals, name the salt solutions, give the observations, and explain how the observations let you rank the metals — vague answers without observations are penalised heavily (1CH0/1H Nov 2021).
  8. The further up the series a metal is, the less resistant it is to oxidation — it tarnishes, corrodes or burns in air more readily.
  9. Aluminium appears unreactive because of a thin protective Al₂O₃ layer on its surface; underneath, the metal is genuinely reactive.
  10. Gold, silver and copper resist oxidation well, which is why they are used in jewellery, ornaments, coins, electrical wiring and water pipes.
  11. The correct exam reason for copper not reacting with dilute acid is that copper is less reactive than hydrogen, not that copper is a transition metal or has the wrong electron configuration (1CH0/1F June 2019).
  12. Very reactive metals (K, Na) are only ever found as compounds in the Earth's crust; unreactive metals (Au, often Ag and Cu) can be found as the uncombined element.
4.1.2.1 Displacement Reactions & Redox H only ▼

Displacement Reactions & Redox

Syllabus 4.2: Explain displacement reactions as redox reactions, in terms of gain or loss of electrons. Higher tier only — Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 1 (1CH0/1H).

A displacement reaction happens when a more reactive metal pushes a less reactive metal out of one of its compounds — for example, zinc reacting with copper(II) sulfate. At Foundation tier you only need to predict whether a reaction occurs and describe what you'd see. At Higher tier the spec point goes further: you have to explain the same reaction as a redox reaction, in terms of which species loses electrons and which species gains electrons. That electron-transfer language is exactly what examiners reward — and exactly where many candidates lose marks.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Displacement reactionA reaction in which a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in one of its compounds
Redox reactionA reaction in which oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously
Oxidation (Higher tier)Loss of electrons by a species
Reduction (Higher tier)Gain of electrons by a species
Oxidising agentThe species that gains electrons (so it gets reduced) and causes another species to be oxidised
Reducing agentThe species that loses electrons (so it gets oxidised) and causes another species to be reduced
Spectator ionAn ion present in solution that takes no part in the chemical change — it appears unchanged on both sides of the equation
Half-equationAn equation showing only the species being oxidised or only the species being reduced, with electrons (e⁻) included to balance the charge
Ionic equationA balanced equation written using only the ions and atoms that actually change during the reaction (spectator ions are removed)

Why displacement reactions are redox reactions

A metal will displace another metal from a solution of its salt only if it is higher in the reactivity series. Linking to 4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals — the more reactive metal is the one that loses electrons more readily, so it can hand electrons over to the ions of the less reactive metal sitting in solution.

That electron handover is the heart of why displacement is redox. One species loses electrons (it is oxidised). Another species gains those same electrons (it is reduced). Both halves happen in the same beaker at the same time, so the overall reaction is a redox reaction by definition.

At Foundation tier students sometimes try to explain redox in terms of "gain or loss of oxygen". That definition is fine for combustion-style reactions but is not sufficient at Higher tier — and it definitely doesn't work for a zinc-and-copper-sulfate displacement, where no oxygen changes hands at all. At Higher tier the only acceptable explanation is in terms of electron transfer.

Worked example 1: zinc + copper(II) sulfate

When a piece of grey zinc metal is dropped into pale-blue copper(II) sulfate solution, the following reaction occurs:

Zn (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → ZnSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)

Step 1 — identify the spectator ion. Write out the full reaction with all the ions shown:

Zn (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) + Cu (s)

The sulfate ion SO₄²⁻ appears unchanged on both sides — same charge, same formula, still dissolved in water. It is a spectator ion and takes no part in the chemistry. (Examiners specifically flag candidates who claim SO₄²⁻ gains or loses electrons — see Examiner Report Insights below.)

Step 2 — write the ionic equation. Cross out the spectator ion from both sides:

Zn (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + Cu (s)

Step 3 — split into half-equations. Each half-equation shows only one species and balances the charges by adding electrons:

  • Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ — zinc loses two electrons
  • Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu — copper(II) ions gain two electrons

Step 4 — apply OILRIG. The mnemonic OILRIG sums up the definitions:

> Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons), Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

  • Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → loss of electrons → oxidation → so Zn is oxidised.
  • Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu → gain of electrons → reduction → so Cu²⁺ is reduced.

*Step 5 — name the agents.*

  • Zn loses electrons (it is oxidised), and in doing so it hands those electrons to Cu²⁺, causing Cu²⁺ to be reduced. So Zn is the reducing agent.
  • Cu²⁺ gains electrons (it is reduced), and in doing so it accepts electrons from Zn, causing Zn to be oxidised. So Cu²⁺ is the oxidising agent.

Notice the slightly counter-intuitive pairing: the species that gets oxidised is the reducing agent, and the species that gets reduced is the oxidising agent. The "agent" is named for what it does to the other species, not to itself.

*What you would actually observe in the beaker:*

  • The blue colour of the solution fades to colourless (Cu²⁺ ions, which are blue in solution, are being removed as they get reduced to copper metal; Zn²⁺ ions are colourless).
  • The grey/silver zinc metal becomes coated with a brown/pink layer of copper.
  • A slight temperature rise may be felt — displacement reactions are exothermic.
  • There is no gas evolved and no bubbles or fizzing — this is a metal-metal displacement, not an acid-metal reaction.

The "no bubbles" point is worth holding on to: examiners explicitly flagged candidates who wrongly wrote about bubbles or fizzing here (see Examiner Report Insights).

Worked example 2: magnesium + iron(II) nitrate

Magnesium is more reactive than iron, so magnesium displaces iron from a solution of one of its salts:

Mg (s) + Fe(NO₃)₂ (aq) → Mg(NO₃)₂ (aq) + Fe (s)

The nitrate ion NO₃⁻ is the spectator (it is unchanged on both sides). Removing it gives the ionic equation:

Mg (s) + Fe²⁺ (aq) → Mg²⁺ (aq) + Fe (s)

Half-equations:

  • Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ — Mg is oxidised (loses electrons); Mg is the reducing agent.
  • Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Fe — Fe²⁺ is reduced (gains electrons); Fe²⁺ is the oxidising agent.

You would see the silvery magnesium ribbon become coated with a dark-grey layer of iron, and any pale-green colour of the Fe²⁺ solution fade.

Worked example 3: iron nail in copper(II) sulfate solution

A classic school experiment: place a clean iron nail into blue copper(II) sulfate solution and leave it for a few minutes. Iron is more reactive than copper, so:

Fe (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → FeSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)

Ionic equation (remove the spectator SO₄²⁻):

Fe (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) → Fe²⁺ (aq) + Cu (s)

Half-equations:

  • Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ — iron is oxidised; iron is the reducing agent.
  • Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu — copper(II) ions are reduced; Cu²⁺ is the oxidising agent.

Observations: the blue solution fades and goes pale green (Fe²⁺ ions are pale green in solution), and the iron nail becomes coated with a brown/pink layer of copper. Again, no gas is given off.

Writing a redox explanation under exam conditions

If you're asked to explain a displacement reaction in terms of electron transfer, a reliable structure is:

  1. State which species is oxidised and write its half-equation (or just say "X loses n electrons to form X^n+").
  2. State which species is reduced and write its half-equation (or "Y^n+ gains n electrons to form Y").
  3. Use the word oxidation for the loss step and reduction for the gain step explicitly — don't just describe electron movement and hope examiners infer it.
  4. Optionally, name the oxidising agent and reducing agent if the question asks.

Avoid these traps:

  • Don't talk about oxygen gain or loss — this won't score at Higher tier.
  • Don't claim the spectator ion (e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻) is gaining or losing electrons. It isn't doing anything.
  • Don't muddle "oxidising agent" with "is oxidised". The species that is oxidised is the reducing agent; the species that is reduced is the oxidising agent.
  • Don't describe bubbles or gas for a metal-metal salt displacement.

4.1.3.1 Sources of Metals F+H ▼

Sources of Metals

Syllabus 4.4: Recall that (a) most metals are extracted from ores found in the Earth's crust, and (b) unreactive metals are found in the Earth's crust as the uncombined elements. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 1.

This spec point is the gateway to all of section 4.1.3 on extracting metals. Before you can talk about a blast furnace, electrolysis, or reduction by carbon, you need to be clear on what state the metal is in when we dig it out of the ground. For almost every metal, the answer is "as a compound" — and the rest of the topic is the story of how we get the metal back out of that compound. For a small group of very unreactive metals, the answer is "as the metal itself" — and that fact is a direct consequence of where they sit in the reactivity series.

Where metals come from

Metals are one of the Earth's finite resources. They are found in the Earth's crust — the thin outer layer of solid rock — and they exist there in two different forms depending on how reactive the metal is.

1. As compounds inside ores (the vast majority of metals). Most metals are reactive enough that, over the billions of years since the Earth formed, they have reacted with non-metals in the environment — chiefly oxygen, but also sulfur, carbon (in carbonates) and chlorine. What we mine is therefore not the pure metal but a metal compound, mixed in with unwanted rock. A rock is only called an ore if it contains enough of the metal compound to make extraction profitable — that economic threshold is part of the definition.

2. As the uncombined element (a small group of unreactive metals). A few metals are so unreactive that they have not reacted with oxygen, water, or anything else in the environment, even over geological time. These metals are therefore found in the crust as the pure element itself — sometimes as visible nuggets or veins running through other rock. They are described as native metals or as occurring as the uncombined element.

Why most metals are found as compounds

The driving force is reactivity. Metals on the more reactive end of the reactivity series — covered in 4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals — readily lose electrons to form positive ions (see 1.3.1.1 Ions). In the presence of oxygen, water, and other reactive species over geological timescales, these metals form stable ionic compounds such as oxides, sulfides, and carbonates. Once that compound has formed, it is thermodynamically favourable and the metal stays locked up in that compound until energy is supplied to extract it.

That is why iron, aluminium, zinc, copper (mostly), magnesium, sodium, and so on are all found as compounds. They are reactive enough that the elemental form would not survive exposure to air or water for very long, let alone for hundreds of millions of years.

Why unreactive metals are found uncombined

Gold, silver, platinum and (to a large extent) copper sit at the bottom of the reactivity series. Because they are so unreactive:

  • They do not react with oxygen in the air, so they don't form oxides on the surface of the Earth.
  • They do not react with water, so rainwater and groundwater don't convert them into ionic compounds.
  • They do not react readily with acids or with non-metals such as sulfur or chlorine under everyday environmental conditions.

The result is that even after billions of years exposed to the Earth's atmosphere and water cycle, these metals remain as the pure element. A nugget of gold pulled out of a riverbed today is the same gold that was there when the rock around it was first formed. This is exactly why gold and silver have been used by humans for thousands of years — they were available in their metallic form long before any extraction technology existed.

Copper sits a little higher in the reactivity series than gold, silver and platinum. Most copper is now extracted from ores such as copper sulfide and copper(II) carbonate, but some copper is still found native as the uncombined element, particularly in regions where the mineralogy is right. So if you are asked to list metals found as uncombined elements, copper is acceptable but the safest answers are gold, silver and platinum.

Why "ore" needs the economic definition

A common trap is to define an ore simply as "a rock containing a metal". That definition is too loose — almost every rock contains some metal atoms somewhere. The Edexcel-friendly definition adds the economic test: an ore must contain enough of the metal (or metal compound) to make it worth extracting. That word "worthwhile" carries the meaning that mining, transporting, and processing the rock has to be financially justified by the value of the metal recovered.

The same rock can stop being an ore if the price of the metal falls, or if a cheaper deposit is found elsewhere, or if mining costs rise. Conversely, a low-grade deposit that was once uneconomic can become an ore if the market price climbs high enough.

4.1.3.2 Extracting Metals F+H ▼

Extracting Metals

Syllabus 4.5–4.7: oxidation is gain of oxygen, reduction is loss of oxygen; extraction of metals involves reduction of ores; the method (heating with carbon or electrolysis) is chosen by the metal's position in the reactivity series and the cost of the process — illustrated by iron and aluminium. Foundation + Higher tier. **Knowledge of the blast furnace is not required.**

Most metals are found in the Earth's crust combined with other elements — usually as oxides or as compounds that can be converted to oxides by roasting. To get the useful metal element on its own, the metal compound has to be broken down by a chemical process. That process is always a reduction, and the choice of which reduction method to use is decided by two things: the reactivity of the metal and the cost of the extraction.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
OreA naturally occurring rock that contains enough of a metal (or a metal compound) to make it economic to extract

Oxidation and reduction in terms of oxygen

At GCSE, oxidation and reduction are defined in terms of oxygen:

  • Oxidation = gain of oxygen
  • Reduction = loss of oxygen

A simple memory aid is OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain — but at this stage the "loss/gain" refers to oxygen. (The electron-transfer definition is reserved for Higher tier work on redox and electrolysis — see 4.1.2.1 Displacement Reactions & Redox.)

In any reaction where one substance is reduced, another must be oxidised: the oxygen has to go somewhere. So when a metal oxide is reduced to the metal, whatever takes the oxygen is itself oxidised. That substance is called the reducing agent.

Example — extracting copper from copper(II) oxide using carbon:

2 CuO + C → 2 Cu + CO₂

  • CuO has lost oxygen → copper is reduced
  • C has gained oxygen → carbon is oxidised
  • Carbon is the reducing agent

This template — metal oxide loses oxygen, carbon takes it — is the heart of every "heating with carbon" extraction in this spec point.

Why all metal extraction is a reduction

A metal in an ore is usually present as a positively charged ion (e.g. Fe³⁺ in iron oxide, Al³⁺ in aluminium oxide, Cu²⁺ in copper oxide). To turn it into the neutral metal element, electrons have to be added back to those ions. Whether that is done chemically (with carbon) or electrically (in electrolysis), the change at the metal is the same: the ion gains electrons and loses oxygen. Every metal extraction is therefore a reduction, and you should be ready to state this in one sentence in an exam.

This is why spec point 4.6 is phrased as: "the extraction of metals involves reduction of ores". Whether carbon does the reducing or electricity does the reducing, the metal compound → metal element step is reduction.

The reactivity series and the carbon–hydrogen rule

The reactivity series for the metals you need to recognise at GCSE, with carbon and hydrogen placed in for reference, is:

*K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > (C) > Zn > Fe > Sn > Pb > (H) > Cu > Ag > Au*

The position of carbon is the decision line for extraction:

  • Metals less reactive than carbon (Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, Cu) can be extracted by heating their ores with carbon — carbon is reactive enough to "pull" the oxygen off the metal oxide
  • Metals more reactive than carbon (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) cannot be extracted with carbon — they hold onto their oxygen too strongly — so they must be extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound
  • Metals below hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Au) are unreactive enough that some are found native (especially gold), but they can still be extracted by heating with carbon if they occur as a compound

You should be able to predict which method a metal needs simply by checking where it sits relative to carbon in the series.

Method (a): Heating with carbon — including iron

This is the cheaper of the two methods because carbon (in the form of coke) is inexpensive and a furnace is much less energy-hungry than electrolysis. It is used for metals below carbon in the reactivity series.

The general process is:

metal oxide + carbon → metal + carbon dioxide (or carbon monoxide)

Worked equations for the metals named in the spec:

  • Zinc: 2 ZnO + C → 2 Zn + CO₂
  • Iron: Fe₂O₃ + 3 C → 2 Fe + 3 CO₂ (or, with carbon monoxide as the reducer in the blast furnace: Fe₂O₃ + 3 CO → 2 Fe + 3 CO₂ — but knowledge of the blast furnace is not required, so you will not be asked for furnace details, gas flows, slag formation, or limestone's role)
  • Tin: SnO₂ + C → Sn + CO₂
  • Lead: 2 PbO + C → 2 Pb + CO₂
  • Copper: 2 CuO + C → 2 Cu + CO₂

In each case, label the redox:

  • The metal oxide is reduced (loses oxygen)
  • Carbon is oxidised (gains oxygen)
  • Carbon is the reducing agent

The big advantages of this method are that the reactants are cheap (carbon and the ore) and the equipment is comparatively simple — just heat. The disadvantage is that it does not work for metals more reactive than carbon, because carbon cannot remove their oxygen.

Method (b): Electrolysis of the molten compound — including aluminium

Metals above carbon in the reactivity series (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) bind their oxygen too tightly for carbon to remove it. Instead, the ore (or a purified compound made from it) is melted and an electric current is passed through it. At the negative electrode (cathode), the positively charged metal ions gain electrons to become neutral metal atoms — this is the reduction step. See 3.2.3.1 Electrolysis of Molten Compounds and 3.2.4.1 Electrolysis & Redox for the full electrode story.

The named example in this spec is aluminium:

*2 Al₂O₃ → 4 Al + 3 O₂*

Aluminium is extracted from purified aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃), which is obtained from the ore bauxite. Pure Al₂O₃ has a very high melting point (about 2050 °C), so to save energy it is dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆), which lowers the operating temperature to around 950 °C. The electrolyte is then a molten mixture of aluminium oxide in cryolite. The carbon-lined cell is the cathode, and carbon blocks are used as the anodes.

At each electrode:

  • Cathode (–): Al³⁺ + 3 e⁻ → Al (Al³⁺ is reduced to Al)
  • Anode (+): 2 O²⁻ → O₂ + 4 e⁻ (O²⁻ is oxidised to O₂)

The oxygen produced reacts with the hot carbon anodes to make CO₂, so the anodes are gradually burnt away and have to be replaced — a continuing running cost.

The reason this method is expensive is essentially energy: melting the electrolyte and driving a huge electrolytic current both consume large amounts of electrical energy, which is costly. That cost is the trade-off you pay to extract a reactive metal.

Why cost matters (linking method to economics)

Spec point 4.7 specifically asks you to link the method to two factors: position in the reactivity series and cost. The reactivity decides whether carbon is chemically capable of doing the reduction; cost decides which available method is the most economic. The cheapest viable method is always preferred, which is why iron is reduced with carbon (cheap and works) and aluminium is electrolysed (the only method that works, even though it is much more expensive).

An exam answer that explains why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis rather than with carbon should include both ideas:

  1. Aluminium is more reactive than carbon, so carbon cannot remove the oxygen from aluminium oxide
  2. Therefore the more expensive electrolysis method is the only viable route

Worked example — percentage of metal in an ore (Math skill 1c)

A common calculation in this topic uses percentages. Ores are usually only a small percentage metal by mass, with the rest being silicate rock, sand, or other compounds.

*Formula:*

`` % metal by mass = (mass of metal / mass of ore) × 100 ``

*Worked example (based on the NLM-cited 1CH0/1F November 2020 Q6(d) tariff of 2 marks):*

> A sample of copper ore has a mass of 5000 kg. It contains 42.5 kg of copper. Calculate the percentage of copper in the ore.

*Procedure:*

  1. Identify the mass of metal: 42.5 kg of copper
  2. Identify the total mass of ore: 5000 kg
  3. Divide and multiply by 100: (42.5 ÷ 5000) × 100
  4. Calculate: = 0.0085 × 100 = 0.85 %

So the ore is 0.85 % copper by mass. Even at this low percentage, the ore is economic to extract because copper is valuable enough and the deposit is large enough.

Exam tip: always check the units match (kg with kg, or g with g) before dividing. If the question gives one mass in tonnes and the other in kilograms, convert one of them first. The answer should be given as a percentage with no unit, to an appropriate number of significant figures (usually 2 or 3).

Bioleaching & Phytomining

Spec 4.8: Evaluate alternative biological methods of metal extraction (bacterial and phytoextraction). **Higher tier only**, examined on Paper 1 (1CH0/1H) for Separate Chemistry and Combined Science (1SC0).

The world's richest, easiest-to-mine metal ores are running out. Most remaining deposits are low-grade — they contain such a small percentage of metal compound that smelting them in a blast furnace or large electrolysis cell would burn more fuel and produce more carbon dioxide than the metal is worth. Biological extraction uses living organisms — bacteria or plants — to concentrate the metal first, so that only a small, energy-cheap recovery step is needed at the end. The two methods you need to evaluate are bioleaching and phytoextraction (also called phytomining).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Low-grade oreA rock or soil containing only a small percentage of a metal compound, too low for traditional mining and smelting to be economic
BioleachingA method of extracting metals from low-grade ores using bacteria, which produce a solution (leachate) containing dissolved metal compounds
LeachateThe solution containing dissolved metal compounds that is produced when bacteria break down a low-grade ore
PhytoextractionA method of extracting metals from low-grade soil using hyperaccumulator plants, which absorb metal compounds through their roots and concentrate them in their shoots and leaves
Hyperaccumulator plantA plant species that can absorb and tolerate very high concentrations of a particular metal compound in its tissues
PhytominingAnother name for phytoextraction, especially when carried out commercially to recover a metal
PhytoremediationThe use of plants to remove pollutants (including toxic metals) from contaminated land

How bioleaching works

Bioleaching exploits naturally occurring bacteria — Thiobacillus species are a classic example — that obtain their energy by oxidising sulfur or sulfide compounds. When these bacteria are introduced to a heap of low-grade ore containing copper sulfide minerals, they break the ore down and release copper ions into solution.

The general sequence is:

  1. A low-grade ore (e.g. copper sulfide rock that contains only ~0.5% copper) is crushed and heaped onto a leaching pad.
  2. A bacterial solution is sprayed onto the heap. The bacteria oxidise the sulfide minerals and the metal ions dissolve into the water trickling through the rock.
  3. The solution that drains out at the bottom — the leachate — contains dissolved copper compounds.
  4. The copper is then recovered from the leachate, usually by displacement with scrap iron or by electrolysis.

The displacement step works because iron is more reactive than copper (see 4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals). Adding scrap iron to a copper(II) sulfate solution gives:

Fe (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → FeSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)

Solid copper precipitates out and can be filtered off. Electrolysis is an alternative — and is also the route used to purify the copper to the very high purity needed for electrical wiring.

Why the energy demand is lower. Traditional copper extraction involves digging an open-pit mine, transporting huge volumes of waste rock, smelting the ore at high temperature, and finally purifying by electrolysis. Bioleaching skips the digging, transport and smelting steps, so the only significant energy cost is the final displacement or electrolysis stage — and because the leachate already contains a concentrated copper compound, less electricity is needed than for purifying smelted blister copper.

How phytoextraction works

Phytoextraction (phytomining) is the plant-based equivalent. Hyperaccumulator plants are sown on land that contains low concentrations of a metal compound — either a natural low-grade deposit or contaminated industrial land. As the plants grow they absorb metal ions through their roots along with water, and the metal compounds become concentrated in the shoots and leaves.

The general sequence is:

  1. Hyperaccumulator plants are grown on low-grade soil or on the spoil heaps left over from a previous mine.
  2. The plants take up metal compounds via their roots and store them in their above-ground tissues over a growing season.
  3. The plants are harvested and burnt. The resulting ash contains a much higher percentage of metal compounds than the soil did originally.
  4. The metal is recovered from the ash by displacement (e.g. with scrap iron) or by electrolysis — the same final-step chemistry as bioleaching.

Why the ash is so much richer than the soil. When the plant burns, the carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in the plant tissue are converted to carbon dioxide and water vapour, which escape to the atmosphere. The non-volatile metal compounds are left behind in the ash, so they end up as a much higher proportion of what remains. Even a soil that contained only fractions of a percent of nickel can give an ash that is over 10% nickel by mass — see the worked example below.

Phytoremediation. Because hyperaccumulator plants pull metals out of soil, the technique can also be used simply to clean up contaminated land — for example, sites polluted by industrial waste or by historic mining. Whether or not the metal is recovered afterwards, the soil ends up cleaner. This is called phytoremediation.

Advantages and disadvantages — written out for "evaluate" answers

*Bioleaching — advantages:*

  • Works on low-grade ores that are uneconomic to mine traditionally
  • No large open-pit mine needed, so less damage to the landscape
  • Lower energy demand than smelting, so lower carbon dioxide emissions
  • Less air pollution because there is no smelting step releasing sulfur dioxide

*Bioleaching — disadvantages:*

  • Slow — yields build up over months or years, not weeks
  • Produces toxic leachate solutions that can pollute water sources if not contained
  • Bacteria need specific conditions (temperature, pH, oxygen) and the process can fail if conditions change
  • The leachate still has to be processed by displacement or electrolysis, so some energy and equipment are still needed

*Phytoextraction — advantages:*

  • Can recover metals from soil that is far too low-grade for traditional mining
  • Reduces damage to the landscape compared with open-pit mining
  • Lower energy demand and lower carbon dioxide emissions than smelting
  • Can be used to clean up land already polluted with toxic metals (phytoremediation)

*Phytoextraction — disadvantages:*

  • Very slow — multiple growing seasons are needed before a useful amount of metal is harvested
  • Yield per hectare per year is low compared with traditional mining
  • Depends on climate and season — a poor growing year reduces output
  • Land used for phytoextraction cannot be used for food crops at the same time

Worked example — percentage by mass of metal in plant ash

This is the most commonly examined calculation on the SP. It uses Maths Skill 1c (use ratios, fractions and percentages).

A common scenario: hyperaccumulator plants are grown on low-grade soil, harvested and burnt. The ash is weighed and analysed for its metal content. You are asked for the percentage by mass of the metal in the ash.

*Formula:*

`` % by mass of metal = (mass of metal in ash ÷ total mass of ash) × 100 ``

Worked example. 1.00 kg of ash from burnt hyperaccumulator plants contains 142.0 g of nickel. Calculate the percentage by mass of nickel in the ash.

Step 1 — convert both masses to the same unit.

1.00 kg = 1000 g

Step 2 — substitute into the formula.

% nickel = (142.0 ÷ 1000) × 100

Step 3 — calculate.

% nickel = 0.142 × 100 = 14.2%

So almost one-seventh of the ash is nickel by mass — a much higher proportion than the soil the plants grew on, which is the whole point of phytoextraction. Compare this to a typical commercial copper ore, which might contain only 0.5–1% copper.

Higher tier extension. Once you have the percentage, you can also be asked for the mass of metal you could obtain from a stated mass of ash, or for the mass of ash that contains a stated mass of metal. Both are simple rearrangements:

`` mass of metal = mass of ash × (% metal ÷ 100) mass of ash = mass of metal ÷ (% metal ÷ 100) ``

4.1.5.1 Recycling Metals F+H ▼

Recycling Metals

Spec 4.10: Evaluate the advantages of recycling metals, including economic implications and how recycling can preserve both the environment and the supply of valuable raw materials. Foundation + Higher tier · Paper 1.

Metals such as iron, aluminium and copper are extracted from ores dug out of the ground. These ores are a finite resource — once a deposit is mined out, it cannot be replaced on any human timescale. Recycling avoids the need to mine and extract fresh metal each time a product reaches the end of its useful life, because the metal in a drinks can, a car body or an electrical wire can simply be melted down and re-cast into something new. The chemistry of the metal does not change, so a recycled aluminium atom is identical in every way to one freshly extracted from bauxite.

This spec point is assessed as an evaluate statement (4.10). At GCSE that almost always appears as a short-answer "give two advantages of recycling…" question, and the most important examiner message — covered in detail below — is that vague answers do not score. You must give qualified advantages that link a cause to a specific consequence.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
RecyclingThe process of collecting used materials and reprocessing them into new products instead of disposing of them
Finite resourceA resource that exists in a fixed quantity on Earth and cannot be replaced once used up (e.g. metal ores, fossil fuels)
OreA rock that contains enough of a metal or metal compound to make extracting the metal economically worthwhile
Primary extractionObtaining metal directly from its ore by mining and chemical processing (e.g. blast furnace, electrolysis)
LandfillA site where solid waste is buried in the ground; metals do not biodegrade and can remain for centuries
Economic implicationsThe effects of a process on costs, jobs, profits and prices

Why we recycle metals — the chemistry behind each advantage

The spec asks you to evaluate, which means weighing benefits against drawbacks. The benefits divide naturally into three groups: preserving raw materials, protecting the environment, and economic implications.

*1. Conserving finite metal ores*

Metal ores are non-renewable — they formed over millions of years through geological processes, and the higher-grade deposits are being depleted faster than any new ones can form. Every tonne of aluminium recovered from old drinks cans is a tonne that does not need to be extracted from bauxite. This matters most for metals where world reserves are already limited, such as copper and tin. Recycling extends the time that economically workable ore deposits will last, which preserves the supply of valuable raw materials for future generations — this is the wording the spec uses.

*2. Less waste sent to landfill*

Metals do not rot or decompose in a meaningful timescale, so any metal thrown away as general waste sits in landfill more or less indefinitely. Landfill sites take up land that could be used for farming, building or wildlife, and the leachate (liquid that drains out of the bottom of a landfill) can contaminate soil and groundwater. Recycling diverts the metal away from landfill, so less waste metal goes to landfill — this exact phrasing is one of the few that is specifically flagged in examiner feedback as scoring a mark (see the Examiner Report Insights below).

*3. Less energy used in production*

Extracting a metal from its ore usually requires high temperatures and/or electrolysis, both of which use enormous amounts of energy. Recycling a metal only needs enough energy to melt it, which is much less than the energy needed to break the metal–oxygen bonds in an ore and reduce the metal.

The clearest example is aluminium. Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide, which requires temperatures of around 950 °C and a very large electric current — the bauxite has to be purified, dissolved in molten cryolite, and then electrolysed. Recycling aluminium only requires melting the scrap (around 660 °C, no electrolysis). The result is that recycling aluminium uses roughly 5% of the energy needed to extract it from bauxite — a 95% energy saving for the same amount of metal. Less energy use means less fossil fuel burned at power stations, which links directly to advantages 4 and 5.

*4. Less mining and quarrying*

Primary extraction starts with mining or quarrying. This has visible local impacts: large open-cast mines destroy the natural landscape, remove habitats for wildlife, generate dust and noise pollution, and leave behind spoil heaps and tailings ponds. Recycling reduces the demand for fresh ore, which in turn reduces the rate at which new mines need to be opened. Less mining means less landscape damage and less habitat destruction.

*5. Lower CO₂ and other emissions*

Because recycling uses less energy, fewer fossil fuels need to be burned to generate that energy. Burning less fossil fuel reduces the release of carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas linked to climate change) and sulfur dioxide (which causes acid rain). For iron and steel, primary production in a blast furnace also produces large amounts of CO₂ directly from the reaction of carbon with iron oxide; recycling skips that reaction entirely, so the CO₂ saving is even larger than the energy saving alone would suggest.

*6. Economic implications*

The spec specifically mentions economic implications, so a strong answer should include at least one cost or jobs point:

  • Recycling is often cheaper than mining and extracting fresh metal, because so much less energy is used and the raw material (scrap) is already concentrated metal rather than dilute ore.
  • The recycling industry creates jobs in collection, sorting and reprocessing.
  • It reduces a country's dependence on imported ore — countries with no domestic copper or aluminium ore can still produce metal by recycling.

Disadvantages and challenges of recycling

The command word is evaluate, so a top answer mentions drawbacks too:

  • Collection and sorting are expensive. Scrap has to be gathered from millions of households or scrapyards, transported, and then separated by metal type. Sorting steel from aluminium from copper takes equipment and time.
  • Contamination lowers the quality of recycled metal. If different metals get mixed together — for example, copper wires left attached to scrap steel — the recycled batch may not meet the specifications needed for high-grade applications, so it can only be used for products with looser tolerances.
  • The process still uses some energy. Melting and reprocessing scrap is not free of CO₂ emissions; it is just much less than primary extraction.
  • It depends on public participation. If consumers do not separate metal from general waste, much of it ends up in landfill anyway. Recycling rates therefore vary by country and by metal.

Worked example — aluminium recycling (4-mark answer)

Question style: "Evaluate the advantages of recycling aluminium drinks cans rather than extracting aluminium from its ore." (4 marks)

A model 4-mark answer:

> Recycling aluminium uses far less energy than extracting it from bauxite, because the recycled aluminium only needs to be melted rather than electrolysed (1). This means fewer fossil fuels are burned at power stations, so less carbon dioxide is released, reducing the contribution to climate change (1). Bauxite is a finite resource, so recycling preserves the supply of aluminium ore for future use and reduces the amount of mining needed, which protects landscapes and wildlife habitats (1). Recycling is also often cheaper than primary extraction, although collecting and sorting the used cans does add a cost (1).

Notice how every advantage is qualified — none of the points are left as bare phrases like "better for the environment" or "saves energy". Each one identifies the advantage and explains why it is an advantage.

4.1.6.1 Life Cycle Assessment F+H ▼

Life Cycle Assessment

Spec 4.11: Describe that a life time assessment for a product involves consideration of the effect on the environment of obtaining the raw materials, manufacturing the product, using the product and disposing of the product when it is no longer useful. Foundation + Higher tier; 1CH0 + 1SC0; Paper 1.

A Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a structured way of working out the total environmental impact of a product across its entire life — from the moment raw materials are pulled out of the ground to the moment the product is thrown away, burned, or recycled. The point of an LCA is that no single stage tells the whole story. A product that looks "green" while you use it (an electric car, say) might be much more polluting to manufacture. A product that is cheap and disposable might cost very little energy to make but generate huge amounts of waste when discarded. LCAs force you to look at the whole picture.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)An analysis of the total environmental impact of a product across its full life, covering raw materials, manufacture, use, and disposal
Raw materialsThe natural substances obtained from the Earth (ores, crude oil, timber, water) that are processed into a product
ManufacturingThe processes that turn raw materials into a finished product, including refining, shaping, and assembling
DisposalWhat happens to a product at the end of its useful life — landfill, incineration, or recycling
Environmental impactThe effect of human activity on the natural world, including energy use, emissions, water use, pollution, and habitat loss

The four stages of an LCA

An LCA always considers the same four stages, in the same order. You need to know all four and be able to identify what kind of environmental effects each one produces.

*Stage 1 — Obtaining the raw materials*

Every product starts with raw materials extracted from the Earth. For a metal product this means mining ore; for a plastic product, drilling crude oil; for a paper product, harvesting timber. The environmental effects at this stage include:

  • Energy used to mine, drill, transport, and crush the raw materials, much of it from burning fossil fuels
  • Habitat destruction when forests are cleared or open-pit mines are dug
  • Landscape damage and visual pollution (quarries, spoil heaps, deforested land)
  • Pollution of land and water from leaching of metal ions, oil spills, or run-off from mining sites
  • Depletion of finite resources — once an ore body or oil field is gone, it is gone

*Stage 2 — Manufacturing the product*

The raw materials are then refined and processed into the finished item. For a metal, this might be smelting and rolling; for a plastic, cracking crude oil fractions and polymerising them; for a paper bag, pulping wood, bleaching, and pressing. The environmental effects at this stage include:

  • Energy use, again mostly from burning fossil fuels, which releases carbon dioxide (CO₂) and contributes to climate change
  • Water consumption — pulp mills and metal refining use very large volumes of water
  • Emissions to air, such as CO₂, sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), and volatile organic compounds
  • Waste byproducts, including slag from blast furnaces, sludge from refining, and offcuts during assembly
  • Use of additional materials — solvents, dyes, catalysts, and packaging all carry their own footprints

*Stage 3 — Using the product*

This is the only stage that depends on how the product is actually used, and for how long. A product used once and thrown away has almost no "use phase" impact, while a product used for years (a car, a fridge, a kettle) can rack up a far bigger total than its manufacture. The environmental effects at this stage include:

  • Energy consumption during use — petrol burned by a car, electricity drawn by a fridge, gas burned by a boiler
  • Emissions during use — exhaust gases from vehicles, CO₂ from heating
  • Maintenance and repair, which require extra raw materials and energy
  • Longevity — a product that lasts twice as long spreads its raw-material and manufacturing footprint over twice as much use

For some products this stage dominates the LCA. For others (e.g. a single-use plastic bag) it is almost zero.

*Stage 4 — Disposal at the end of life*

When the product is no longer useful, it has to go somewhere. The three main options each have different environmental effects:

  • Landfill — takes up land, can leach pollutants into groundwater, releases methane (CH₄) from rotting waste; non-biodegradable plastics persist for hundreds of years
  • Incineration — reduces volume and can recover energy, but produces CO₂ and may release toxic gases if not properly controlled
  • Recycling — saves raw materials and usually saves energy, but the collection, sorting, and reprocessing themselves require energy and produce some emissions

The disposal stage feeds back into stage 1 for the next product: a recycled aluminium can saves about 95% of the energy needed to extract aluminium from bauxite.

Why all four stages matter together

A common mistake is to judge a product on just one stage. The whole point of an LCA is that you have to consider the total environmental impact across all four stages — because improvements in one stage can be wiped out by worse impacts in another.

The plastic shopping bag vs cotton tote bag vs paper bag comparison illustrates this clearly:

StagePlastic shopping bagPaper bagCotton tote bag
Raw materialsCrude oil (finite)Timber (renewable, but habitat loss from forestry)Cotton plants (large land use, pesticides, irrigation)
ManufacturingLow energy per bag; small amount of plasticHigher energy than plastic; uses lots of water and bleachVery high energy; very high water use (~2,700 L per bag)
UseOften single-use; very light to transportUsually single-use; tears when wetReusable hundreds of times; durable
DisposalPersists in landfill or environment for hundreds of yearsBiodegradable; recyclableBiodegradable; recyclable; reused

A plastic bag looks worst on disposal but actually has the smallest raw-material and manufacturing footprint per bag. A cotton tote looks "green" but only beats a plastic bag if you reuse it many hundreds of times. The "best" choice is not obvious until you put all four stages side by side.

The petrol car vs electric car comparison flips the picture in a different way:

StagePetrol carElectric car

ManufacturingStandard car-plant emissions and energy useHigher energy use because of battery manufacture

DisposalMost metals recyclable; engine oil and tyres are pollutantsMost metals recyclable; battery recycling is developing but complex

An electric car has higher raw-material and manufacturing impacts than a petrol car, mainly because of the battery. But its use stage is far lower. Over the full life of the vehicle, the use stage typically dominates, so an electric car comes out ahead — but only after you have driven it enough miles to "pay back" the higher manufacturing footprint. None of that is visible if you only look at one stage.

LCA vs carbon footprint

Students sometimes confuse LCA with carbon footprint. They are related but not the same.

A carbon footprint is one slice of an LCA. An LCA includes the carbon footprint and everything else.

Worked example — comparing two drinks bottles

Imagine you are comparing a single-use plastic water bottle with a reusable aluminium water bottle. Work through the four stages:

  1. Raw materials. The plastic bottle uses crude oil — a finite fossil resource. The aluminium bottle uses bauxite ore, which requires open-pit mining and lots of electricity to extract by electrolysis. Aluminium extraction has a much higher raw-material energy cost per bottle.
  2. Manufacturing. Moulding a thin plastic bottle uses very little energy. Shaping an aluminium bottle uses more energy because aluminium has to be melted and worked. Manufacturing impact is higher for the aluminium bottle.
  3. Use. The plastic bottle is used once. The aluminium bottle can be refilled hundreds of times. If you refill it 100 times, the per-use impact of every earlier stage is divided by 100.
  4. Disposal. A used plastic bottle may end up in landfill, incinerated, or recycled — only some are actually recycled. An aluminium bottle, when it is finally thrown away, is almost always recycled, and aluminium recycling saves about 95% of the original extraction energy.

The aluminium bottle has higher impacts in stages 1 and 2, but a much smaller impact per use because of stage 3 (reuse) and stage 4 (highly recyclable). Looking at any one stage in isolation would give the wrong answer.

4.1.6.2 Evaluating LCA F+H ▼

Evaluating LCA

Spec 4.12: Evaluate data from a life cycle assessment of a product. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry and Combined Science, Paper 1.

A life cycle assessment (LCA) produces a table of numbers describing the environmental impact of a product across its four stages: raw materials, manufacture, use, and disposal. The skill examined in this spec point is reading those numbers and reaching a justified conclusion — not just describing what an LCA is. For the description of the stages themselves, see 4.1.6.1 Life Cycle Assessment.

In Foundation papers, evaluation questions almost always present you with a data table comparing two or three products. Your job is to compare the totals, identify which stage dominates, weigh up trade-offs, and quote specific numbers from the data to back up your conclusion.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition

Carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e)A common unit used in LCAs to combine the effect of all greenhouse gases into a single number, measured in kg or g

What the data table usually looks like

A typical LCA evaluation question gives you a table with products in columns and stages in rows (or vice versa). The numbers are usually one of:

  • kg CO₂ equivalent (greenhouse gas emissions)
  • MJ of energy used or required
  • litres of water consumed or polluted
  • kg of solid waste produced
  • g of air pollutants (e.g. SO₂, NO_x)

Here is a worked example comparing a single-use paper bag with a single-use plastic (polythene) bag:

LCA stagePaper bag (kg CO₂e)Plastic bag (kg CO₂e)
Raw materials (timber / crude oil)0.080.02
Manufacture0.120.04
Use (transport to shop)0.010.01
Disposal (landfill / recycling)0.020.05
Total0.230.12

A naïve answer would be "paper is better because it is natural" or "plastic is better because it has a lower total". Neither shows evaluation. A good evaluation does all of the following.

How to evaluate the data — a five-step method

*Step 1 — Compare totals across the whole life cycle.*

Add the four stages for each product and compare. Quote the totals.

From the table above: paper = 0.23 kg CO₂e, plastic = 0.12 kg CO₂e. Plastic has a smaller total carbon footprint, about half that of paper.

*Step 2 — Identify which stage dominates for each product.*

For each product, find the single biggest number. That tells you where the product does most of its environmental damage.

  • For the paper bag, manufacture (0.12) dominates — paper-making uses a lot of energy to pulp wood and dry sheets.
  • For the plastic bag, disposal (0.05) dominates — plastic does not break down in landfill and can pollute water and harm wildlife.

*Step 3 — Look for trade-offs.*

A trade-off is when one product wins at one stage but loses at another. These are the most marks in an evaluation question, because they show you have read the whole table rather than picking one number.

  • Paper has a lower disposal impact (0.02 vs 0.05) because it is biodegradable and easier to recycle.
  • Plastic has a lower raw-materials and manufacture impact (0.06 vs 0.20) because making polythene uses less energy per bag than making paper.
  • "Use" is identical (0.01 for both), so it is not a deciding factor.

*Step 4 — Use specific numbers, not generalisations.*

Every evaluation point should be backed by a number from the table. "Paper has higher manufacturing emissions" is weaker than "paper has higher manufacturing emissions of 0.12 kg CO₂e compared with 0.04 kg CO₂e for plastic — three times as much".

*Step 5 — Reach a conclusion based on the question's criteria.*

The conclusion depends on what the question asks. If the question asks "which has the lower overall carbon footprint?" the answer is plastic (0.12 < 0.23). If the question asks "which is better for waste reduction?" the answer is paper (0.02 < 0.05). Read the question carefully and answer the specific question asked.

Worked example — petrol car vs electric car

Some LCA questions cover a longer time period (such as the lifetime of a car) and use larger units. Here is a comparison over 200,000 km of driving:

LCA stagePetrol car (tonnes CO₂e)Electric car (tonnes CO₂e)
Raw materials (steel, battery metals)612
Manufacture46
Use (fuel / electricity over 200 000 km)3610
Disposal12
Total4730

*Evaluation:*

  • Over the full lifetime, the electric car produces 30 tonnes CO₂e compared with 47 tonnes CO₂e for the petrol car — a saving of 17 tonnes, or roughly 36% less.
  • The electric car has a higher raw-materials impact (12 vs 6 tonnes) because lithium, cobalt, and nickel for the battery have to be mined and refined.
  • The electric car also has a higher manufacture and disposal impact for the same battery-related reason.
  • The decisive stage is use: the petrol car emits 36 tonnes during use compared with only 10 tonnes for the electric car. This single stage outweighs the higher raw-materials and disposal impacts of the electric car.
  • Conclusion: Over a full 200,000 km lifetime, the electric car has the lower total carbon footprint, but most of the saving comes from the use stage. If the car is driven for fewer kilometres than that, the petrol car can come out ahead because the electric car never "pays back" its higher manufacturing emissions.

This is a typical exam answer: numbers quoted, dominant stage named, trade-off recognised, conclusion linked to the question's criteria.

Exam-answer template

When you write an LCA evaluation in an exam, work through this template:

  1. State the totals. "Product A has a total of X, product B has a total of Y."
  2. Name the dominant stage for each. "For A, manufacture (X) is the biggest stage; for B, use (Y) is the biggest."
  3. Identify any trade-offs. "A is better at one stage (with numbers); B is better at another stage (with numbers)."
  4. Give a justified conclusion. "Overall, [product] has the lower environmental impact, mainly because of [specific stage] where it saves [number]."

Do quote figures from the table for every claim. Do not say "X is better for the environment" without backing. Do not invent extra data the question did not give you. Do not ignore stages — examiners want to see you have read the whole table.

Limitations of LCA data

A good evaluation also recognises that LCA numbers are not perfect:

  • Different LCA studies use different assumptions, so two LCAs of the "same" product can produce different totals.
  • The "use" stage depends heavily on how the consumer behaves (how often a bag is re-used, how the car is driven, whether the bottle is recycled).
  • Some impacts are easier to measure than others — energy in MJ is straightforward, but the impact on wildlife or human health is harder to put a single number to.

You only need to mention limitations if the question specifically asks you to evaluate the reliability of the data, not just compare products.

Mathematical Skills — extracting data from tables

Maths skills 2c and 4a: extract and interpret information from charts, graphs, and tables. LCA evaluation is one of the most common places this maths skill is examined in Chemistry.

The procedure is:

  1. Read the row and column headings carefully. Make sure you know which number belongs to which product and which stage.
  2. Check the units. kg CO₂e is not the same as tonnes CO₂e — confusing these by a factor of 1,000 is an easy mistake.
  3. Add up the totals for each product to get a like-for-like comparison.
  4. Find the largest number in each column to identify the dominant stage for that product.
  5. Compare the two products stage by stage to spot trade-offs.

*Worked maths example — reading the paper-vs-plastic table:*

  • Total for paper: 0.08 + 0.12 + 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.23 kg CO₂e
  • Total for plastic: 0.02 + 0.04 + 0.01 + 0.05 = 0.12 kg CO₂e
  • Difference: 0.23 − 0.12 = 0.11 kg CO₂e in favour of plastic.
  • As a percentage saving: (0.11 ÷ 0.23) × 100 = 48% lower for plastic.

Quoting a percentage difference or a multiple ("three times as much", "about half") often picks up an extra mark because it shows you have processed the data rather than just copied it out.

Reversible Reactions

Spec 4.13: Recall that chemical reactions are reversible, the use of the symbol ⇌ in equations and that the direction of some reversible reactions can be altered by changing the reaction conditions.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Most of the reactions you have met so far have been treated as one-way changes — reactants turn into products and that is the end of the story. A combustion reaction, for example, is effectively irreversible: once methane has burned to carbon dioxide and water, you cannot easily push those products back to methane and oxygen under normal laboratory conditions. A reversible reaction is different. The products can react together to re-form the original reactants, and the same physical setup can drive the change either forwards or backwards depending on the conditions you apply.

This idea is the conceptual gateway to everything that follows in this section, including dynamic equilibrium and the industrial reactions you will study in 4.2.2.1 The Haber Process.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Reversible reactionA reaction in which the products can react together to re-form the original reactants
Forward reactionThe reaction that converts reactants (left of the equation) into products (right of the equation)
Reverse reactionThe reaction that converts products back into the original reactants
⇌ symbolThe half-arrow double-headed symbol used in chemical equations to show that a reaction is reversible
Reaction conditionsThe temperature, pressure, and concentration (and sometimes the presence or absence of water or other species) that determine which direction a reversible reaction is driven in

The ⇌ Symbol — and the Word Trap

A reversible reaction is written using the ⇌ symbol in place of the single arrow (→). The two half-arrows pointing in opposite directions are a visual reminder that both the forward and reverse reactions are happening, or are at least possible.

A general equation for a reversible reaction looks like this:

`` A + B ⇌ C + D ``

Reading left to right is the forward reaction (A and B combining to make C and D). Reading right to left is the reverse reaction (C and D recombining to give A and B back).

*The word trap — "reversible", "equilibrium", and the wrong answer "reversed"*

When an exam question shows the ⇌ symbol and asks what type of reaction it represents, the required word is reversible. "Equilibrium" is also accepted, because a reversible reaction in a closed system reaches a state of equilibrium (see 4.2.1.2 Dynamic Equilibrium). What is not accepted is the word "reversed" — it is too vague and does not name the type of reaction. If you describe a reversible reaction as a "reversed reaction", you will lose the mark.

This is a common error flagged in examiner reports — see the Examiner Report Insights below.

How Reaction Conditions Change the Direction

For some reversible reactions, you can change which direction is favoured by altering the conditions. Two classic class demonstrations illustrate this for Foundation and Higher tier candidates.

*Worked example 1 — Hydrated copper(II) sulfate*

Hydrated copper(II) sulfate is a blue crystalline solid containing water molecules built into its lattice. When you heat it strongly in a test tube, the water is driven off and you are left with a white powder — anhydrous copper(II) sulfate. Add a few drops of water to the white powder and the blue colour returns, often with enough warmth that you can feel the test tube heating up slightly.

The reversible reaction is:

`` CuSO₄·5H₂O (s) ⇌ CuSO₄ (s) + 5H₂O (l) (blue) (white) ``

  • Forward reaction (left to right): dehydration. You drive it by heating — the heat supplies the energy needed to break the bonds holding the water of crystallisation inside the lattice.
  • Reverse reaction (right to left): rehydration. You drive it by adding water — the water re-enters the lattice and the blue colour returns. The reverse reaction is exothermic — energy is released as new bonds form between the water molecules and the copper(II) sulfate.

Because the forward reaction is endothermic and the reverse is exothermic, the two directions have opposite energy profiles — see the section below on energy in reversible reactions.

This experiment is a frequent exam target — see the Examiner Report Insights below for a worked-through trap.

*Worked example 2 — Heating ammonium chloride*

Ammonium chloride is a white solid. When you heat it strongly in a test tube, you can see what looks like the solid disappearing from the bottom of the tube and a white solid re-forming further up the tube where the glass is cooler. What is happening is:

`` NH₄Cl (s) ⇌ NH₃ (g) + HCl (g) (white) (colourless gases) ``

  • Forward reaction: thermal decomposition. Heat splits the solid ammonium chloride into two colourless gases — ammonia and hydrogen chloride.
  • Reverse reaction: recombination. As the gases drift up the test tube into the cooler region, they react together again to re-form solid ammonium chloride, which deposits on the glass.

This is a striking visual demonstration because the same substance appears to be moving up the tube — when in fact it is being decomposed at the hot bottom and re-formed at the cool top.

Energy in Reversible Reactions

A key idea that students often miss the first time round: the forward and reverse reactions of a reversible reaction have opposite energy profiles.

If the forward reaction is exothermic (releases energy, e.g. X kJ), then the reverse reaction is endothermic by the same amount (absorbs X kJ). Energy is conserved — the energy released when the products form is exactly the energy needed to break them apart again.

Forward ReactionReverse Reaction
Exothermic (releases X kJ)Endothermic (absorbs X kJ)
Endothermic (absorbs X kJ)Exothermic (releases X kJ)

*Applied to hydrated copper(II) sulfate:*

  • Forward (CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ + 5H₂O): endothermic — you have to supply heat to drive it
  • Reverse (CuSO₄ + 5H₂O → CuSO₄·5H₂O): exothermic — you can feel the test tube warm up when you add the water

This "equal and opposite" relationship is something you should be able to state cleanly. If an exam question tells you the forward reaction releases 50 kJ, the reverse reaction absorbs 50 kJ — same magnitude, opposite sign.

How Reaction Conditions Alter the Direction — the General Principle

You do not need to know dynamic equilibrium ideas for this spec point — that is the job of 4.2.1.2 Dynamic Equilibrium. For 4.2.1.1, you need to be able to state and recognise that:

  • A reversible reaction can be driven in either direction
  • The conditions that matter most at GCSE are temperature (heating to drive one direction, cooling to drive the other), and the addition or removal of one of the reactants or products (e.g. adding water to anhydrous CuSO₄)
  • Different conditions favour different directions — this is why industrial processes (such as the Haber process for ammonia, see 4.2.2.1 The Haber Process) carefully control temperature, pressure and concentration

The deeper "why" of how each condition shifts the position is dealt with in 4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium under Higher-tier content.

4.2.1.2 Dynamic Equilibrium F+H ▼

Dynamic Equilibrium

Spec 4.14: Explain what is meant by dynamic equilibrium — Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry + Combined Science, Paper 1.

A reversible reaction (covered in 4.2.1.1 Reversible Reactions) is one that can go in both directions and is shown with the ⇌ symbol. Dynamic equilibrium is the special state that a reversible reaction settles into when it is left alone in a closed container for long enough. The word "dynamic" tells you that things are still happening at the molecular level, even though, from the outside, the reaction mixture looks as though nothing is changing.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Reversible reactionA reaction in which the products can react together to re-form the original reactants; shown with the ⇌ symbol
Dynamic equilibriumThe state reached in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, so the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant
Closed systemA reaction vessel from which no reactants or products can escape, and to which nothing is added or removed
Forward reactionThe reaction going left-to-right as written (reactants → products)
Reverse reactionThe reaction going right-to-left as written (products → reactants)
Position of equilibriumA description of how much reactant compared with product is present at equilibrium (covered in detail in 4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium)

The two conditions for dynamic equilibrium

For a reversible reaction to be at dynamic equilibrium, two conditions must be met at the same time:

  1. The rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of the reverse reaction. Reactants are turning into products at exactly the same rate as products are turning back into reactants.
  2. The system must be closed — no reactants or products can enter or leave the reaction container.

If either condition is missing, the system is not at dynamic equilibrium. For example, if the reaction is open to the air and a gaseous product can escape, the reverse reaction cannot keep up with the forward reaction, and equilibrium can never be established.

*Why a closed system matters*

In a reversible reaction, you need both directions to be possible. If a product escapes (for example, a gas bubbling away), it cannot collide with anything to re-form the reactants. The reverse reaction effectively stops, the forward reaction keeps going until the reactants are used up, and the reaction behaves as if it were one-way. This is why thermal decomposition of hydrated copper(II) sulfate is reversible only when the water vapour driven off is kept in contact with the anhydrous solid — in an open boiling tube, the steam escapes and the reverse reaction does not occur.

What "dynamic" really means

The single biggest misconception students have about equilibrium is that the reaction has stopped. It has not. The forward and reverse reactions are both still happening — molecules are still colliding, reacting, breaking apart and re-forming bonds. The reason there is no overall (net) change in concentration is that for every reactant molecule converting into product, somewhere else in the flask a product molecule is converting back into reactant at the same instant. The two processes are perfectly balanced.

This is what the word dynamic captures: there is constant molecular-level activity, even though the macroscopic properties (colour, pressure, concentration) appear constant.

*Why concentrations stay constant — but are not necessarily equal*

A second very common error is to say that "the concentrations of reactants and products are equal at equilibrium". That is wrong. At equilibrium:

  • The concentration of each substance stays constant (it doesn't change over time).
  • The concentrations of reactants and products are usually not equal to each other. One side is often present in much greater amounts than the other.

Whether there is mostly reactant, mostly product, or a roughly even mixture is described by the position of equilibrium (see 4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium). What is identical at equilibrium is not the amounts of substance but the rates of the two opposing reactions.

The swimming-pool analogy

A useful way to picture dynamic equilibrium is a swimming pool with water flowing in from a tap at one end and out through a drain at the other. If the inflow rate equals the outflow rate:

  • The water level in the pool stays constant (this represents the constant concentrations of reactants and products).
  • Water is still moving in and out continuously (this represents the forward and reverse reactions still happening).

If you took a snapshot, you would see a still water surface — but turn the tap or the drain off and you would quickly see that water was moving all along. That is exactly what is happening in a chemical system at dynamic equilibrium. It is not static. It only looks still.

The same picture works for a busy escalator: people stepping on at the bottom at the same rate as people stepping off at the top means the number of people on the escalator stays constant, even though every single person is moving.

Worked example — the Haber process

The classic industrial example is the synthesis of ammonia, covered in more detail in 4.2.2.1 The Haber Process:

`` N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) ``

When nitrogen and hydrogen are mixed in a sealed container at the right temperature and pressure, the following happens over time:

  1. At the start, only the forward reaction can happen because no NH₃ exists yet. N₂ and H₂ molecules collide and form NH₃. The concentrations of N₂ and H₂ fall, and the concentration of NH₃ rises.
  2. As NH₃ builds up, the reverse reaction becomes possible: NH₃ molecules can collide and break apart back into N₂ and H₂. The reverse rate starts low but rises as more NH₃ is present.
  3. Meanwhile, as N₂ and H₂ are used up, the forward rate falls.
  4. Eventually, the forward rate falls to meet the rising reverse rate. Once they are equal, dynamic equilibrium is reached.

At equilibrium in this sealed container:

  • [N₂], [H₂] and [NH₃] are all constant.
  • They are not equal — under typical Haber conditions only a small fraction of the gas mixture is ammonia, so [NH₃] is much lower than [N₂] and [H₂] (the position of equilibrium lies towards the reactants).
  • N₂ and H₂ molecules are still combining to form NH₃ every moment, and NH₃ molecules are still decomposing back into N₂ and H₂. The two rates are simply identical.

*Why the closed container is essential here*

If the reaction vessel were open, ammonia would escape (it is a gas) and the reverse reaction would never get going properly — the system would never reach equilibrium. In the real industrial Haber process the vessel is sealed during the reaction; ammonia is later separated out by cooling it to a liquid and a fresh batch of N₂/H₂ is fed in.

A simple lab example — hydrated and anhydrous copper(II) sulfate

The colour change between blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate and white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate is reversible:

`` CuSO₄·5H₂O (s) ⇌ CuSO₄ (s) + 5H₂O (l) (blue) (white) ``

If the water vapour is trapped (closed system), the forward and reverse reactions can both occur and an equilibrium can be set up. If the boiling tube is left open and the steam escapes, only the forward reaction proceeds and the white solid remains white — this is not an equilibrium, simply a one-way thermal decomposition under those conditions. This is a useful way to test whether you really understand the closed-system condition.

Common student errors

Three errors come up again and again in answers to "Explain what is meant by dynamic equilibrium":

  • Writing that "the reaction stops". This loses the mark — dynamic equilibrium is not the same as the reaction finishing. Both the forward and reverse reactions are still happening.
  • Writing that "the concentrations are equal". The concentrations are constant, not equal. Mixing up these two words is the single most common reason for losing the mark on this spec point.
  • Forgetting to mention the closed system. Without this condition, the system can never reach equilibrium because one or more substances would be lost to the surroundings.

There is also a more subtle error flagged by examiners: applying equilibrium ideas to questions that are really asking about something else. The June 2019 1CH0/1H examiner report noted a candidate who had been asked to design a laboratory experiment to show that a reaction (the hydration/dehydration of copper(II) sulfate) is reversible, but instead wrote about equilibria. Reserve the equilibrium language for questions that explicitly ask you to explain dynamic equilibrium.

Key Takeaways
  1. Dynamic equilibrium is the state reached in a reversible reaction (in a closed system) when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.
  2. At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant — but they are usually not equal to each other.
  3. "Dynamic" means the forward and reverse reactions are still happening at the molecular level — the reaction has not stopped.
  4. Equilibrium can only be reached in a closed system where nothing can enter or leave; if a product escapes, the reverse reaction cannot occur and no equilibrium is set up.
  5. The two essential marking points on "explain what is meant by dynamic equilibrium" are: (i) equal forward and reverse rates, and (ii) closed system / constant concentrations of reactants and products.
  6. The swimming-pool analogy: water flowing in at the same rate as water flowing out keeps the level constant even though water is still moving — that is exactly what is happening in a chemical system at equilibrium.
  7. In the Haber process, N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, at equilibrium the concentrations of N₂, H₂ and NH₃ are constant, but ammonia molecules are still being formed and decomposed continuously at equal rates.
  8. Two very common errors to avoid: writing that "the reaction stops" (it does not — it is dynamic), and writing that "the concentrations are equal" (they are constant, not equal).
  9. The June 2019 1CH0/1H examiner report warned candidates not to apply equilibrium principles when a question asks them to describe a laboratory experiment showing reversibility — that needs practical detail, not equilibrium theory.

  12. Reversible reactions are introduced in 4.2.1.1 Reversible Reactions; dynamic equilibrium is the natural follow-up because it describes what eventually happens inside any closed reversible system left long enough.
4.2.2.1 The Haber Process F+H ▼

The Haber Process

Spec 4.15: Describe the formation of ammonia as a reversible reaction between nitrogen (extracted from the air) and hydrogen (obtained from natural gas) and that it can reach a dynamic equilibrium.

Spec 4.16: Recall the conditions for the Haber process as: a temperature 450 °C, b pressure 200 atmospheres, c iron catalyst.

The Haber process is the industrial method for making ammonia (NH₃) from nitrogen and hydrogen. Ammonia is one of the most important industrial chemicals on the planet — more than 80% of global ammonia output goes into making fertilisers, which feed roughly half the world's population. The same gas is also used to make nitric acid, explosives, dyes, plastics, and household cleaners. Understanding the Haber process means understanding three things at once: the balanced equation, the raw materials, and the specific compromise conditions that make the process commercially viable.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Haber processThe industrial reaction between nitrogen and hydrogen that produces ammonia, carried out at high temperature and high pressure over an iron catalyst
AmmoniaA compound with the formula NH₃, made of one nitrogen atom bonded to three hydrogen atoms
Reversible reactionA reaction in which the products can react together to re-form the reactants; shown with the ⇌ symbol
Dynamic equilibriumThe point in a reversible reaction (in a closed system) at which the forward and backward reactions occur at the same rate, so the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant
CatalystA substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being used up; in the Haber process the catalyst is iron
YieldThe amount of product actually produced compared to the maximum amount theoretically possible

The Balanced Equation — Learn This Exactly

The balanced equation for the Haber process is:

*N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g)*

Three features of this equation are non-negotiable and frequently lose marks:

  1. The formula of ammonia is NH₃ — single N, three H atoms. Examiners have flagged NH₂ and NH₄ as the two most common wrong answers (see Examiner Report Insights below). NH₄ is the ammonium ion, not ammonia.
  2. The arrow is reversible (⇌), not a single arrow (→). The Haber process never goes to completion; some nitrogen and hydrogen always remain unreacted.
  3. State symbols must be (g) for all three species. Although ammonia is condensed to a liquid later (during separation), inside the reactor everything is a gas.

The equation is also where most students lose easy mark-scheme points on balancing: there are 3 mol of hydrogen on the left and 2 mol of ammonia on the right. The total moles of gas drop from 4 to 2 across the reaction — a detail that becomes important when explaining why high pressure is used.

The Raw Materials — Where N₂ and H₂ Come From

The two reactants are extracted from cheap, abundant sources:

ReactantSourceWhy this source
Nitrogen (N₂)The airAir is about 78% nitrogen by volume, so it is essentially free; nitrogen is obtained by fractional distillation of liquid air
Hydrogen (H₂)Natural gas (mainly methane, CH₄)Methane is reacted with steam in a process called steam reforming to produce hydrogen

This is a frequent recall question on the Foundation paper: candidates are simply asked to state the source of one or both reactants. "From the air" for nitrogen and "from natural gas" (or "from methane") for hydrogen are the credit-worthy answers.

Why These Conditions Were Chosen — The Compromise

The conditions used in the Haber process are not the conditions that give the highest yield. They are the conditions that give the best compromise between yield, rate, cost, and safety. Understanding each compromise is essential because Foundation papers frequently ask candidates to state a condition and give a reason, while Higher papers push into the full Le Chatelier reasoning (covered in 4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium).

*Why 450 °C and not a lower temperature*

The forward reaction (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃) is exothermic. In principle, a lower temperature would push the equilibrium to the right and give a higher yield of ammonia. However, a low temperature also means a very slow reaction rate, because particles have less kinetic energy and fewer successful collisions per second. If the reaction is too slow, the plant cannot produce enough ammonia per hour to be commercially worthwhile — even if the yield per pass is high.

A higher temperature speeds the reaction up but reduces yield (because the position of equilibrium shifts back towards reactants in an exothermic reaction). 450 °C is the compromise: hot enough to give a reasonable rate, cool enough to keep the yield acceptable.

*Why 200 atm and not a higher pressure*

Look at the moles of gas on each side of the equation:

  • Left-hand side: 1 mol N₂ + 3 mol H₂ = 4 mol of gas
  • Right-hand side: 2 mol NH₃ = 2 mol of gas

There are fewer gas molecules on the products side. Increasing the pressure pushes the equilibrium towards the side with fewer gas molecules — i.e. towards more ammonia. So high pressure is good for yield.

So why not use even higher pressure (e.g. 1000 atm)? Two reasons:

  1. Cost — pressure vessels strong enough to withstand very high pressures are extremely expensive to build, run, and maintain.
  2. Safety — very high pressures of hydrogen create a serious explosion risk. Hydrogen is flammable across a wide concentration range, and leaks become more likely (and more dangerous) the higher the pressure.

200 atm is the compromise: high enough to give a useful yield, low enough that the cost of the equipment and the safety risk remain manageable.

*Why an iron catalyst is used*

Even at 450 °C, the reaction between N₂ and H₂ is slow because the N≡N triple bond in nitrogen is very strong and difficult to break. The iron catalyst provides a surface on which the bond-breaking happens more easily, lowering the activation energy.

Two key points students must remember:

  1. The catalyst speeds up the rate at which equilibrium is reached — it does not change the position of equilibrium.
  2. The catalyst is not used up in the reaction — it does not appear in the balanced equation.

So an iron catalyst means the plant reaches its (compromise) yield faster, allowing more batches of ammonia to be produced per day at the same conditions. Iron is also cheap and abundant, which matters when an industrial plant uses tonnes of it.

The Industrial Flow — What Actually Happens in the Plant

The Haber process is a continuous (not batch) industrial process. The same gases are recycled around the system over and over again.

  1. Feedstock preparation — nitrogen is obtained from the air (by fractional distillation of liquid air); hydrogen is obtained from natural gas (mainly methane) by reacting CH₄ with steam.
  2. Mixing and compression — the two gases are mixed in a 1:3 mole ratio (matching the equation: N₂ + 3H₂) and compressed to 200 atm.
  3. Heating — the compressed mixture is heated to 450 °C.
  4. Catalytic conversion — the hot, pressurised mixture is passed over beds of iron catalyst inside the reactor. Some of the N₂ and H₂ react to form NH₃, but the reaction does not go to completion — equilibrium is reached, with all three gases present.
  5. Cooling and condensation — the product mixture is cooled. Ammonia liquefies at a higher temperature than N₂ or H₂, so the NH₃ condenses to a liquid and is tapped off. N₂ and H₂ remain as gases.
  6. Recycling — the unreacted N₂ and H₂ are fed back into the reactor with fresh feedstock. This is why the relatively low yield per pass (typically only around 10–15%) is acceptable — nothing is wasted because the unreacted gas is reused.

The combination of high pressure, high temperature, the catalyst, and continuous recycling is what makes the plant commercially viable. No single trick on its own is enough.

Uses of Ammonia — Why the Haber Process Matters

The reason the Haber process is so important industrially is the sheer scale of ammonia demand:

The Position of Equilibrium

Syllabus 4.17 — Predict how the position of a dynamic equilibrium is affected by changes in: (a) temperature, (b) pressure, (c) concentration. **Higher tier only.**

A reversible reaction at dynamic equilibrium has equal forward and reverse rates, so the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant. The position of equilibrium tells you which side those constant concentrations sit on — closer to the reactants (left), or closer to the products (right). When something about the conditions changes, the position can shift, and a higher-tier student needs to be able to predict the direction of the shift and explain why.

The reasoning tool you'll use throughout is Le Chatelier's principle. Although the Edexcel specification text does not name it, every shift you predict for this SP is an application of it.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Dynamic equilibriumThe state of a reversible reaction in a closed system where the forward and reverse reactions are occurring at the same rate, so the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant
Position of equilibriumA description of the relative amounts of reactants and products present in the equilibrium mixture; a shift "to the right" means more products, a shift "to the left" means more reactants
Le Chatelier's principleIf a closed system at dynamic equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose that change
YieldThe amount of product formed; shifting the position to the right increases the yield of products
Closed systemA reaction system in which matter cannot enter or leave, so all species remain in contact and equilibrium can be established

Le Chatelier's principle — the single rule that explains everything

Read the principle carefully: the system opposes the change. That single phrase generates every prediction in this SP.

  • If you add heat, the system "removes" heat — so the equilibrium shifts in the direction that absorbs heat (the endothermic direction).
  • If you compress the system (increase pressure), the system "reduces volume" — so it shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas.
  • If you add a reactant, the system "consumes" the added reactant — so it shifts toward products.

You don't need to memorise a table of outcomes. If you can identify (i) which direction is exothermic, (ii) which side has more moles of gas, and (iii) what was added or removed, the prediction follows automatically.

Effect of changing each condition — the master grid

The table below shows what happens for each of the three variables on the specification. The reasoning column is the part that earns the explanation marks.

VariableChangeDirection of shiftReasoning (Le Chatelier)
TemperatureIncrease TShifts toward the endothermic directionExtra heat is supplied; the system opposes this by absorbing heat, which is what the endothermic direction does
TemperatureDecrease TShifts toward the exothermic directionHeat is removed; the system opposes this by releasing heat, which is what the exothermic direction does
Pressure (gases only)Increase PShifts toward the side with fewer moles of gasCompression raises pressure; the system opposes this by reducing the total number of gas particles, lowering pressure
Pressure (gases only)Decrease PShifts toward the side with more moles of gasExpansion lowers pressure; the system opposes this by increasing the number of gas particles
Concentration of a reactantIncreaseShifts toward productsThe added reactant is consumed by the forward reaction
Concentration of a reactantDecreaseShifts toward reactantsThe forward reaction slows because there is less reactant; the reverse reaction tops it back up
Concentration of a productIncreaseShifts toward reactantsThe added product is consumed by the reverse reaction

A few important caveats:

  • Pressure only matters if the number of moles of gas is different on the two sides. If a reaction has the same number of gas moles on each side (e.g. H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI, which has 2 mol gas on each side), changing the pressure does not shift the position. The system has no "fewer-moles side" to move to.
  • Solids and liquids are ignored when counting moles of gas for the pressure rule. Only count gaseous species.
  • A catalyst does not shift the position of equilibrium. It speeds up both the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster — but the final equilibrium mixture is identical. This is a common student trap; if a question asks about a catalyst and the position, the answer is "no change to position; faster rate of attainment of equilibrium".

Worked example 1 — the Haber Process

The Haber Process is the classic industrial application:

N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) ΔH = exothermic (forward)

Counting moles of gas: 4 mol of gas on the left (1 N₂ + 3 H₂), 2 mol of gas on the right (2 NH₃). The right-hand side has fewer moles of gas.

*Predicting the effect of higher pressure:*

Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium to the side with fewer moles of gas — that's the right. So higher pressure increases the yield of ammonia.

*Predicting the effect of lower temperature:*

The forward reaction is exothermic, so decreasing temperature favours the exothermic direction — that's the forward. So lower temperature increases the yield of ammonia.

So in theory we'd run the Haber Process at very high pressure and very low temperature. In practice, the operating conditions used industrially are around 200 atm and 450 °C. Why the compromise?

  • Very high pressures (e.g. 1000+ atm) need extremely thick, expensive reaction vessels and are dangerous. 200 atm is a cost-effective middle ground.
  • Very low temperatures give a higher equilibrium yield, but the reaction rate is too slow to be economically useful — equilibrium would take too long to reach. 450 °C sacrifices some equilibrium yield for a workable rate of reaction.

This idea — that the theoretical prediction and the industrially chosen conditions are different, because rate of attainment of equilibrium matters too — is essential at higher tier. Examiners reward candidates who address both the position of equilibrium and the rate of attainment of equilibrium when explaining industrial conditions.

Worked example 2 — methane-steam reforming

This reaction makes hydrogen for industrial use:

CH₄ (g) + H₂O (g) ⇌ CO (g) + 3H₂ (g) ΔH = endothermic (forward)

Counting moles of gas: 2 mol of gas on the left (1 CH₄ + 1 H₂O), 4 mol of gas on the right (1 CO + 3 H₂). The left-hand side has fewer moles of gas.

Notice that the stoichiometry is the opposite way round to the Haber Process. Get the counting right before predicting anything.

*Predicting the effect of higher temperature:*

The forward reaction is endothermic. Increasing temperature favours the endothermic direction — that's the forward. So higher temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right and increases the yield of hydrogen.

*Predicting the effect of lower pressure:*

Decreasing pressure shifts the equilibrium to the side with more moles of gas — that's the right (4 mol). So lower pressure also increases the yield of hydrogen.

This is the exact opposite of the Haber Process for both variables. The reasoning is identical (Le Chatelier), but because the thermodynamics and stoichiometry are different, the predictions flip. Always work from the equation given in the question — never reflexively apply Haber-Process conclusions to a different equilibrium.

Mathematical Skills

The mathematical skill examined here is Math Skill 1c — using ratios, fractions and percentages, applied to gas-mole reasoning for pressure shifts. The procedure is short but must be reliable under exam conditions:

`` Step 1: Identify all gaseous species on each side of the balanced equation Step 2: Add the stoichiometric coefficients (moles) of the gases on each side Step 3: Compare: which side has the smaller mole count? Step 4: Apply the rule: - Increase P → shift to side with fewer moles of gas - Decrease P → shift to side with more moles of gas Step 5: State which species' yield therefore increases or decreases ``

Worked through for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃:

  • Step 1: All four species are gases.
  • Step 2: Reactants = 1 + 3 = 4 mol gas. Products = 2 mol gas.
  • Step 3: Products side has fewer moles of gas.
  • Step 4: Increase P → shift to products side.
  • Step 5: Yield of NH₃ increases.

Worked through for CH₄ + H₂O ⇌ CO + 3H₂:

  • Step 1: All four species are gases.
  • Step 2: Reactants = 1 + 1 = 2 mol gas. Products = 1 + 3 = 4 mol gas.
  • Step 3: Reactants side has fewer moles of gas.
  • Step 4: Increase P → shift to reactants side. Decrease P → shift to products side.
  • Step 5: Lower pressure → yield of H₂ increases.

Key Takeaways

  2. Temperature increase favours the endothermic direction; temperature decrease favours the exothermic direction.
