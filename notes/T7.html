Determining the Rate of a Reaction

Spec point 7.2: Suggest practical methods for determining the rate of a given reaction. Examined on both Foundation and Higher tier, Paper 2 (1CH0/2 and 1SC0/2).

The rate of a reaction tells you how quickly reactants are being used up or how quickly products are being formed. To measure rate you must choose a property that changes during the reaction — something you can detect, time, and convert into a quantity. The "right" method depends entirely on what the reaction actually does: does it make a gas, does the solution change colour, does a solid appear, does mass drop because gas escapes? You pick the method that matches the observable change.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Rate of reactionThe change in amount (or concentration) of reactant or product per unit time, usually in g/s, cm³/s, or mol/dm³/s
Mean (average) rateTotal change in quantity divided by total time taken; gives one rate value across an interval
Initial rateThe rate at the very start of the reaction (steepest part of the graph), before any reactant has been used up
ReactantA substance that is used up during the reaction; its amount decreases over time
ProductA substance that is formed during the reaction; its amount increases over time
Gas syringeA graduated, low-friction syringe used to collect and measure the volume of gas produced
Inverted measuring cylinderA water-filled measuring cylinder upturned over water, used to collect gas by water displacement
ColorimetryA technique that measures how much light a coloured solution absorbs, used to follow colour change over time

Why rate must be defined per unit time

A reaction does not have a single "speed" the way a moving car does. Some reactions are fast at the start and slow down as reactants are used up; others are slow at the start and accelerate (autocatalysis). Defining rate as change in quantity ÷ time lets you give a numerical answer for any chosen time interval. If you pick the whole reaction, you get the mean rate. If you pick a tangent at t = 0, you get the initial rate. The unit always carries "per second" (or per minute) because rate is a "how much per how long" quantity.

Choosing the right method — match the method to the observable

Every rate experiment starts with the same question: what changes that you can measure? The four common observable changes in GCSE reactions are:

  1. A gas is produced (volume increases, or mass of the flask decreases).
  2. A precipitate forms (the solution turns cloudy).
  3. A coloured species appears or disappears (the colour changes).
  4. The pH or conductivity of the solution changes (less common at GCSE).

Each observable maps to a specific apparatus.

Gas collection — why a gas syringe is usually preferred

A gas syringe is the most accurate routine method for collecting gas at GCSE. The syringe barrel is graduated (commonly to 100 cm³, with 1 cm³ divisions), it has low friction so the plunger moves freely, and it works regardless of whether the gas is soluble in water. Collecting gas over water in an inverted measuring cylinder works well for gases like hydrogen and oxygen that are essentially insoluble, but it fails for carbon dioxide because CO₂ dissolves measurably in water, giving an underestimate of the true volume.

A common reason students lose marks in gas-collection questions is missing one critical piece of apparatus from the diagram. Without a bung sealing the reaction flask, the gas escapes into the air before it ever reaches the delivery tube and syringe. Without a delivery tube, there is nothing connecting the flask to the collection apparatus. Without the trough of water (in the water-displacement version), the inverted cylinder has nothing to displace. A correctly drawn apparatus must form an unbroken sealed pathway from where the gas is generated to where it is collected.

Mass loss method — why you need cotton wool

If the gas produced is heavier than air (carbon dioxide is the classic example, as in the reaction of marble chips with hydrochloric acid), you can leave the flask sitting on a top-pan balance and simply record the mass at regular time intervals. The mass falls as gas escapes. A loose cotton wool plug is placed in the neck of the flask to stop liquid splashing out (which would also lower the mass and ruin the measurement) while still allowing gas to leave. The cotton wool does not seal the gas in — that would defeat the entire point.

You cannot use the mass-loss method for hydrogen because hydrogen is so much lighter than air that the mass change is too small to detect reliably on a school balance (the resolution is typically only 0.01 g).

Disappearing cross — why this only works for precipitate reactions

The classic disappearing-cross experiment involves sodium thiosulfate reacting with hydrochloric acid:

Na₂S₂O₃ (aq) + 2HCl (aq) → 2NaCl (aq) + S (s) + SO₂ (g) + H₂O (l)

A pale yellow precipitate of sulfur slowly clouds the solution. The conical flask sits on top of a piece of paper with a cross drawn on it; you start a stopwatch when the reactants mix and stop it when you can no longer see the cross when looking down through the solution. The time taken for the cross to disappear is inversely proportional to the rate — a shorter time means a faster reaction, so rate ∝ 1/time.

This method is cheap and accessible but it has a serious limitation: judging exactly when the cross has "disappeared" is subjective and depends on the observer's eyesight, the lighting in the room, and the angle of viewing. Two students often record different times for the same reaction. Colorimetry, where a machine measures absorbance, removes this subjectivity but is rarely available in school labs.

Calculating rate from your measurements

Once you have collected data, calculating rate uses one simple formula:

`` Rate = change in quantity ÷ time ``

For a gas-collection experiment:

`` Rate of reaction = volume of gas produced (cm³) ÷ time (s) ``

For a mass-loss experiment:

`` Rate of reaction = mass lost (g) ÷ time (s) ``

Worked example — calculating mean rate

A student reacts 5.0 g of magnesium ribbon with excess hydrochloric acid. The reaction is complete after 40 seconds and 120 cm³ of hydrogen gas has been collected in a gas syringe.

Step 1: Identify the change in quantity. Volume of gas produced = 120 cm³. Step 2: Identify the total time. Time taken = 40 s. Step 3: Apply the rate formula.

Rate = 120 cm³ ÷ 40 s = 3.0 cm³/s

If the question gave time in minutes you would convert to seconds first (1 min = 60 s). Always quote the unit of the answer — examiners expect cm³/s, g/s, or mol/dm³/s and will not award the unit mark for a bare number.

Worked example — comparing two methods

A student wants to investigate the reaction of calcium carbonate (marble chips) with dilute hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃ (s) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl₂ (aq) + H₂O (l) + CO₂ (g).

Method A — gas syringe. Fit the conical flask with a bung and delivery tube leading to a 100 cm³ gas syringe. Add the marble chips to the acid, immediately replace the bung, and start the stopwatch. Record the syringe reading every 10 seconds.

Method B — mass loss. Place the conical flask (with a loose cotton wool plug) on a top-pan balance. Add the acid, then drop in the marble chips and start the stopwatch. Record the mass every 10 seconds.

Both methods work for this reaction because CO₂ is produced. Gas-over-water would not work well because CO₂ is moderately soluble in water — the volume collected would be lower than the true volume generated.

Mathematical Skills

The mathematical skills tested under this spec point are 1a (arithmetic computation) and 1c (ratio). They appear when:

  • Converting units of time (minutes → seconds) before substituting into the rate formula.
  • Dividing a change in volume or mass by a time interval to get a rate.
  • Comparing two rates as a ratio (e.g. "the rate at 40 °C is twice the rate at 20 °C").

The standard formula:

`` Rate = change in quantity ÷ time ``

Procedure:

  1. Identify the quantity that has changed (volume of gas, mass of flask, concentration).
  2. Convert all values into consistent units (cm³ and s, or g and s).
  3. Divide the change in quantity by the time taken.
  4. State the answer with the correct unit (cm³/s, g/s, etc.) to the appropriate number of significant figures.

Typical tariff for a rate calculation: 1–3 marks.

Key Takeaways
  1. Rate of reaction is the change in amount (or concentration) of a reactant or product per unit time, calculated as rate = change in quantity ÷ time.
  2. Choose the measurement method to match the observable change: gas produced, precipitate formed, colour change, or mass loss.
  3. Gas can be collected over water (using an inverted measuring cylinder) for insoluble gases like H₂ and O₂, but not for CO₂ because CO₂ dissolves in water.
  4. A gas syringe is the most accurate routine method for collecting gas: it works for soluble and insoluble gases, has low friction, and has finer graduations than a measuring cylinder.
  5. The mass-loss method works for reactions that produce a heavy gas (typically CO₂). A loose cotton wool plug stops splashing while still letting gas escape; you cannot use this method for H₂ because the mass change is too small to detect on a school balance.
  6. The disappearing-cross method works for precipitate reactions (e.g. Na₂S₂O₃ + HCl); rate is inversely proportional to the time taken for the cross to disappear, so rate ∝ 1/time.
  7. Apparatus diagrams must show a sealed pathway from reaction flask to collection vessel — missing the bung is a very common mark-losing error.
  8. Always convert time to seconds before substituting into the rate formula, and always quote the unit (cm³/s, g/s, or mol/dm³/s).
  9. Mean rate is calculated over a whole interval; initial rate is the steepest gradient at the start of the reaction.
  10. Colorimetry can be used to follow reactions where a coloured species appears or disappears, removing the subjectivity of "by eye" judgements.
  11. Common Paper 2 tasks are: complete the apparatus diagram (2 marks), name a better piece of apparatus (1–2 marks), devise a plan (4 marks).
  12. When devising a plan, include the apparatus, what you measure, how often you measure it, the variable you change, the variables you control, and how you process the data into a rate.
7.1.1.2 Core Practical: Investigating Rate of Reaction F+H ▼

Core Practical: Investigating Rate of Reaction

Spec point 7.1 — Investigate the effects of changing the conditions of a reaction on the rates of chemical reactions by (a) measuring the production of a gas in the reaction between hydrochloric acid and marble chips, and (b) observing a colour change in the reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid. Foundation + Higher tier, Paper 2 only.

This core practical sits at the heart of the Rates of Reaction topic. You need to know both methods in detail — the apparatus, the variables, the measurements you take, the graphs you draw, and the safety considerations — because Paper 2 questions routinely ask you to complete a diagram, name a better piece of apparatus, or devise a plan based on these set-ups.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Rate of reactionThe change in amount of reactant or product per unit time, usually measured in cm³/s, g/s, or mol/dm³/s
Independent variableThe variable you deliberately change (e.g. concentration, temperature, surface area)
Dependent variableThe variable you measure (volume of gas collected, or time for the cross to disappear)
Control variableA variable you keep constant so the test is fair (e.g. volume of acid, total mass of marble chips)
Surface area to volume ratioThe amount of solid surface exposed to a reactant per unit volume; higher for smaller pieces or powder

Why these two methods?

A reaction's rate can only be measured if something observable changes as it proceeds. Edexcel chose these two reactions because each offers a different, easily monitored change:

  • Method (a) — calcium carbonate (marble chips) reacts with hydrochloric acid to produce carbon dioxide gas, a soluble product that bubbles off and can be collected and measured.
  • Method (b) — sodium thiosulfate reacts with hydrochloric acid to produce a fine suspension of sulfur, which gradually makes the solution cloudy (opaque).

Together, the two methods let you investigate different conditions: surface area and concentration affect the marble chip reaction most cleanly, while temperature is best investigated with the thiosulfate "disappearing cross" set-up.

---

Method (a) — Hydrochloric acid + marble chips (gas collection)

*Reaction:*

CaCO₃ (s) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl₂ (aq) + H₂O (l) + CO₂ (g)

*Apparatus*

  • Conical flask fitted with a bung and delivery tube (the bung is essential — without it, the gas escapes and is not collected)
  • Gas collection method — either a gas syringe, or an inverted measuring cylinder over water in a trough
  • Hydrochloric acid (e.g. 1.0 mol/dm³)
  • Marble chips (calcium carbonate) — small/large chips or powder
  • Balance, stopwatch, measuring cylinder (for the acid)

*Method*

  1. Measure out a fixed volume of hydrochloric acid (e.g. 50 cm³) using a measuring cylinder and pour into the conical flask.
  2. Weigh out a fixed mass of marble chips (e.g. 5.0 g).
  3. Add the marble chips to the flask, immediately replace the bung, and start the stopwatch.
  4. Record the volume of gas collected every 30 seconds (or every 10 seconds for a faster reaction) until no more gas is produced.
  5. Repeat with a changed variable — for example, a different concentration of acid, a different surface area (small chips vs powder), or a different temperature.
  6. Plot volume of gas (y-axis) against time (x-axis) and compare the gradients.

*Expected results*

  • The curve rises steeply at the start (highest rate) and gradually levels off as reactants are used up.
  • Higher concentration of acid → steeper initial gradient, same final volume (if acid is the limiting reactant, increasing concentration gives more gas; if marble is limiting, same final volume).
  • Smaller chips / powder → steeper initial gradient, same final volume of gas (same mass reacts).
  • Higher temperature → steeper initial gradient, same final volume.

The role of the gradient. The gradient of a tangent to the curve gives the rate of reaction at that moment. The initial gradient (drawn at t = 0) is the most useful comparison because at later times the reactants are partly used up and rate naturally falls. See 7.1.3.1 Rate Graphs for the tangent technique.

---

Method (b) — Sodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid (disappearing cross)

*Reaction:*

Na₂S₂O₃ (aq) + 2HCl (aq) → 2NaCl (aq) + SO₂ (aq) + S (s) + H₂O (l)

The sulfur formed is insoluble and produces a pale yellow precipitate that gradually makes the mixture opaque.

*Apparatus*

  • Conical flask
  • A piece of paper with a black cross drawn on it, placed under the flask
  • Measuring cylinders for sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid
  • Thermometer
  • Stopwatch
  • Water bath (for varying temperature)

*Method*

  1. Measure 50 cm³ of sodium thiosulfate solution into the conical flask using a measuring cylinder.
  2. Place the flask on top of the paper marked with a cross.
  3. Measure out 10 cm³ of hydrochloric acid.
  4. Add the acid to the flask, start the stopwatch immediately, and swirl gently.
  5. Look down through the top of the flask. Stop the stopwatch when the cross is no longer visible.
  6. Record the time taken.
  7. Repeat at different temperatures (e.g. 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 °C) using a water bath to warm the thiosulfate solution before adding the acid. Keep all volumes and concentrations the same.
  8. Plot a graph of 1/time (a measure of rate) against temperature.

*Expected results*

  • The higher the temperature, the shorter the time for the cross to disappear — so 1/time is larger and rate is higher.
  • Typically the rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise.
  • A graph of 1/time vs temperature gives a steep upward curve.

Why 1/time? Time itself is inversely proportional to rate — a faster reaction takes less time. Plotting 1/time gives a quantity that is directly proportional to rate, which makes patterns easier to see and gradients meaningful.

Safety

  • Hydrochloric acid — irritant; wear eye protection. Wipe up spills with damp cloth.
  • Sulfur dioxide gas produced in method (b) is toxic and a respiratory irritant. Carry out the experiment in a well-ventilated room; people with asthma should not stand close.
  • Hot water bath / hot thiosulfate — risk of scalds; do not exceed 60 °C as decomposition of thiosulfate accelerates and sulfur dioxide release becomes noticeable.
  • Marble chips are mild irritants — wash hands after handling.

Sources of error and improvements

*Method (a)*

  • Gas escapes before the bung is in place — improvement: have the bung ready in one hand and replace it the instant the chips are added, or use a side-arm flask.
  • Gas dissolves in water if using the inverted-cylinder-over-water method (CO₂ is slightly soluble) — improvement: use a gas syringe instead, which avoids contact with water.
  • Measuring cylinders are imprecise — improvement: use a gas syringe (typical resolution 1 cm³) for more accurate gas volume readings.
  • Marble chips are irregular in shape and surface area — improvement: use the same total mass of chips of the same size grade, or pre-weigh chips of similar visual size.

*Method (b)*

  • The endpoint is subjective — improvement: use a light sensor or data logger to record when light transmission drops below a set value.
  • Temperature drops during the reaction — improvement: take an average of the temperature at the start and end of the run, or keep the flask in the water bath while the reaction proceeds.

7.1.2.1 Collision Theory F+H ▼

Collision Theory

Spec point 7.3: Explain how reactions occur when particles collide and that rates of reaction are increased when the frequency and/or energy of collisions is increased. Foundation + Higher tier, examined on Paper 2 for both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

Collision theory is the underlying model that explains every rate-of-reaction observation you meet at GCSE. Once you can use it fluently, the four factors that change rate (temperature, concentration, surface area, pressure) all collapse into the same two ideas: how often particles meet, and how hard they hit when they do.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Collision theoryThe idea that chemical reactions only occur when reactant particles collide with each other with sufficient energy and the correct orientation
Successful collisionA collision between reactant particles that results in a reaction (also called an effective collision)
Activation energy (Eₐ)The minimum amount of energy that colliding particles must have in order to react
Frequency of collisionsThe number of collisions per unit time between reactant particles
Energy of collisionsThe kinetic energy that the colliding particles bring into the collision
Rate of reactionThe change in the amount of a reactant or product per unit time

Why reactions need collisions in the first place

For two reactant particles to form new products, the existing bonds between their atoms have to break and new bonds have to form. Bonds are electrostatic interactions between charged particles — they cannot rearrange by themselves while the reactants are sitting at opposite ends of a beaker. The two particles must physically come into contact, i.e. they must collide.

This is why every rate question is, at heart, a question about particle movement. If reactant particles cannot reach each other, no reaction occurs. If they meet each other but bounce off without breaking any bonds, no reaction occurs either. A reaction only happens when a collision is successful.

The two conditions for a successful collision

A collision only counts as successful — meaning it actually produces a reaction — when both of the following are true:

  1. The particles collide with sufficient energy, i.e. their combined kinetic energy is greater than or equal to the activation energy (Eₐ) of the reaction.
  2. The particles collide with the correct orientation, so that the bonds that need to break are in the right places relative to each other.

If either condition fails, the particles bounce apart unchanged and no products are formed. Most collisions in a typical reaction mixture are unsuccessful because the particles either do not have enough energy or hit each other at the wrong angle.

Activation energy and why it matters

The activation energy is the minimum energy a colliding pair of particles must possess for the existing bonds to start breaking. You can think of it as an "energy barrier" sitting between the reactants and the products: particles that arrive at the collision with less energy than Eₐ rebound off this barrier, while particles with more energy than Eₐ climb over it and go on to form products.

Different reactions have different activation energies, which is one reason why some reactions are naturally fast at room temperature (a low Eₐ) while others appear not to happen at all unless you supply a lot of heat (a high Eₐ). For the same reaction, however, Eₐ is fixed — you cannot change it by stirring harder or using a different beaker. What you can change is the fraction of collisions that meet or exceed it, and that is where the four rate-changing factors come in.

Frequency of collisions vs energy of collisions — the distinction examiners care about

This is the single most important distinction in the topic, and it is where most marks are lost.

  • Frequency of collisions answers the question "how often do particles meet?" If you double the number of reactant particles in the same volume, they meet twice as often. If you grind a solid into a powder, the surface particles meet acid particles more often. Frequency is about contact rate.
  • Energy of collisions answers the question "when particles do meet, how hard do they hit?" This depends on how fast the particles are moving — i.e. their kinetic energy. The hotter the mixture, the faster the particles move on average, and the larger the fraction of collisions that arrive with energy greater than or equal to Eₐ.

Increasing either quantity increases the rate of reaction. The spec text specifically says rate increases "when the frequency and/or energy of collisions is increased" — both routes work, and some changes (notably temperature) affect both at once.

Factor changedFrequency of collisionsEnergy of collisions
Increase temperatureIncreases (faster-moving particles meet more often)Increases (more particles ≥ Eₐ)
Increase concentration of solutionIncreases (more particles per unit volume)Unchanged
Increase pressure of gasIncreases (more particles per unit volume)Unchanged
Decrease particle size / increase surface areaIncreases (more reactant particles exposed)Unchanged
Add a catalystUnchangedEffectively more collisions ≥ Eₐ (because Eₐ is lowered)

Notice that three of the four factors in 7.1.2.2 (concentration, pressure, surface area) work by changing only the frequency. Temperature is the special one that changes both.

How the model explains everyday rate observations

Why a reaction slows down as it proceeds. As reactants are used up, fewer reactant particles remain in the same volume. The frequency of reactant–reactant collisions therefore falls, and so does the rate. By the time one reactant has run out, the frequency of successful collisions drops to zero and the reaction stops. This is the standard explanation for the curve flattening on a mass-loss or gas-volume graph (see 7.1.3.1 Rate Graphs).

Why powdered solids react faster than lumps. Reaction can only occur where acid particles meet the surface of the solid. A powder has a far greater total surface area than a single lump of the same mass, so more solid particles are exposed at any one time and the frequency of acid–solid collisions per second increases. The energy of each collision is the same — what has changed is how often successful contact occurs.

Why concentrated acid reacts faster than dilute acid. In a more concentrated solution there are more acid particles per unit volume. Each magnesium (or marble chip, or zinc) particle is therefore struck by acid particles more often per second. Again the energy per collision has not changed — the frequency has.

Why warming the reaction up has the biggest effect. Raising the temperature gives every particle more kinetic energy. They move faster, so they meet each other more often (frequency rises) and a greater proportion of those meetings exceed Eₐ (energy rises). Both effects act in the same direction, which is why a 10 °C rise can roughly double the rate of many reactions.

Why the spec doesn't ask you to draw an energy distribution

At A Level, students see a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution and shade in the fraction of particles above Eₐ. At GCSE, you do not have to draw or label that distribution. What you do need is the conceptual idea behind it: at any temperature, particles do not all share the same kinetic energy — some have far less, some have far more, and only those above Eₐ are capable of reacting. Heating the mixture shifts more particles into the "above Eₐ" group, increasing the rate.

Worked example — applying collision theory to a 6-mark question

A student adds dilute hydrochloric acid to two beakers of calcium carbonate. Beaker A contains one large lump; Beaker B contains the same mass of small chips. Beaker B is then repeated using a more concentrated acid. Explain, in terms of collision theory, what happens to the rate.

A model 6-mark answer threads collision theory through both variables:

  1. Reactions occur only when acid particles collide with calcium carbonate particles.
  2. Small chips have a larger total surface area than one large lump (same mass).
  3. So more calcium carbonate particles are exposed to the acid at any one time.
  4. The frequency of collisions between acid particles and the surface increases.
  5. Increasing the acid concentration puts more acid particles in the same volume of solution.
  6. So acid particles collide with the calcium carbonate more frequently per second.
  7. Both changes increase the frequency of successful collisions, so the rate increases.

Notice the answer never claims that the energy of the individual collisions has changed — neither surface area nor concentration affects that. The frequency-vs-energy distinction is the marking pivot.

Explaining Rates

Spec point 7.4: explain the effects on rates of reaction of changes in temperature, concentration, surface area to volume ratio of a solid and pressure (on reactions involving gases) in terms of frequency and/or energy of collisions between particles. Foundation + Higher tier · Paper 2.

This spec point applies collision theory to four named variables. The whole game is linking the change in conditions to what happens to the particles, and then to the rate of reaction. Examiners reward answers that explicitly mention particles, frequency of collisions and (for temperature) energy of collisions. They penalise vague answers that simply restate "the rate goes up" or list factors without explaining them.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Rate of reactionThe change in amount of reactant used up or product formed per unit time
Collision frequencyThe number of collisions between reacting particles per unit time
Activation energy (Eₐ)The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a collision to be successful and lead to a reaction
Successful collisionA collision in which the particles have at least the activation energy and the correct orientation, so that a reaction takes place
Surface area to volume ratioThe total exposed surface area of a solid divided by its volume — a measure of how much of the solid is accessible to reacting particles

The two collision-theory levers

Every rate-changing factor in this spec point works through one or both of the following mechanisms:

  1. Frequency of collisions — how often particles meet. Increasing the number of particles per unit volume, or exposing more surface, makes collisions more frequent.
  2. Energy of collisions — what fraction of those collisions are successful. Only collisions with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy lead to a reaction. Increasing temperature raises the average kinetic energy and so increases the proportion of collisions that succeed.

Three of the four named variables (concentration, pressure, surface area to volume ratio) act only on frequency. Temperature acts on both, and at GCSE level the energy effect is the dominant one. Getting this distinction right is the single biggest scoring opportunity in the whole subtopic.

Temperature

Increasing the temperature of a reaction mixture has two effects on the particles:

  • The particles gain kinetic energy, so they move faster. They therefore collide more frequently.
  • More importantly, a greater proportion of the particles now have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy. A greater proportion of collisions are therefore successful.

At GCSE level, the energy effect is the more important one — a relatively small temperature increase causes a relatively small increase in collision frequency, but a much larger increase in the proportion of particles exceeding Eₐ. As a rough rule of thumb, a 10 °C rise often roughly doubles the rate of many reactions, and this is mainly because of the energy effect, not the frequency effect.

A model answer at Higher tier looks like:

> When the temperature is increased, the particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. This increases the frequency of collisions between particles, and a greater proportion of collisions have energy greater than or equal to the activation energy. More collisions are therefore successful per unit time, so the rate of reaction increases.

Concentration (of solutions)

If the concentration of a solution is increased, there are more particles of solute per unit volume. Particles are packed more closely together, so they collide with reactant particles more frequently. The frequency of successful collisions per unit time therefore increases, and the rate of reaction increases.

Concentration does not change the energy of the particles. The proportion of collisions that are successful is unchanged. The effect comes entirely from collision frequency.

A model answer looks like:

> Increasing the concentration of the acid means there are more acid particles in the same volume of solution. The acid particles collide with the magnesium more frequently, so the frequency of successful collisions per unit time increases and the rate of reaction increases.

Pressure (gases only)

Increasing the pressure of a gas (at constant temperature) squeezes the same number of particles into a smaller volume — or, equivalently, puts more particles into the same volume. The logic is identical to concentration: more particles per unit volume means a higher frequency of collisions and so a higher rate of reaction.

Pressure changes only matter for reactions involving gases. For reactions between solids and liquids, or solutions, changing the pressure has essentially no effect, because liquids and solids are not significantly compressible.

Surface area to volume ratio (solids)

When a solid reactant is broken into smaller pieces, the total surface area exposed to the other reactant increases, but the total volume (and therefore the total amount of substance) stays the same. The surface area to volume ratio increases.

Reactions involving a solid happen only at its surface, where reactant particles in solution or gas can meet solid particles. With a higher surface area to volume ratio, more solid particles are exposed, so collisions between the two reactants happen more frequently. The rate of reaction therefore increases.

Like concentration and pressure, this is a pure frequency effect — the energy of the colliding particles is unchanged.

FactorEffect on frequency of collisionsEffect on energy of collisionsNet effect on rate

Increase pressure (gas)IncreaseNo changeIncrease

Worked example — surface area to volume ratio of cubes

A common Foundation-tier maths skill is to quantify why smaller lumps react faster by comparing the surface area to volume ratios of cubes. This is the integrated mathematical skill for this spec point (criteria 1c, 1d, 5c — calculate surface areas and volumes of cubes).

Consider 1 cm³ of a solid reactant present as one large cube, vs the same total volume divided into smaller cubes.

*Case 1: one cube of side 1 cm*

`` Surface area = 6 × (1 cm × 1 cm) = 6 cm² Volume = 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm = 1 cm³ SA : V ratio = 6 : 1 = 6 cm⁻¹ ``

*Case 2: split the same volume into 8 cubes of side 0.5 cm*

Each small cube:

`` Surface area (per cube) = 6 × (0.5 × 0.5) = 6 × 0.25 = 1.5 cm² Volume (per cube) = 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125 cm³ ``

Total for 8 cubes:

`` Total surface area = 8 × 1.5 = 12 cm² Total volume = 8 × 0.125 = 1 cm³ SA : V ratio = 12 : 1 = 12 cm⁻¹ ``

*Case 3: split the same volume into 1000 cubes of side 0.1 cm*

`` Surface area (per cube) = 6 × (0.1 × 0.1) = 0.06 cm² Volume (per cube) = 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.001 cm³ Total surface area = 1000 × 0.06 = 60 cm² Total volume = 1000 × 0.001 = 1 cm³ SA : V ratio = 60 : 1 = 60 cm⁻¹ ``

Same total volume of reactant — but the SA : V ratio has gone from 6 to 12 to 60 cm⁻¹. Because there are now far more particles of solid exposed at the surface, the frequency of collisions with the other reactant is far higher, and so the rate of reaction is far higher. This is exactly the kind of cube-calculation candidates were expected to handle in 1CH0/2F June 2023 Q4b.

Rate Graphs

Spec point 7.5 — Interpret graphs of mass, volume or concentration of reactant or product against time. Examined at both Foundation and Higher tier, on Paper 2 of both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science).

A rate graph is a plot of how much reactant has been used up, or how much product has been made, against time. Almost every rate-of-reaction question on Paper 2 either gives you one or asks you to draw, sketch, or read from one. Being fluent at reading the shape, the gradient, and the plateau is what separates a 2-mark answer from a full-mark answer.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Rate of reactionThe change in amount of reactant or product per unit time
Mean rateThe average rate over a stated interval, calculated as change in quantity ÷ change in time
Instantaneous rateThe rate at one specific moment in time, found from the gradient of a tangent to the curve at that point
TangentA straight line that touches a curve at exactly one point without crossing it
GradientThe slope of a line, calculated as Δy ÷ Δx
PlateauThe flat horizontal section at the end of a rate graph, showing that the reaction has stopped
Limiting reactantThe reactant that gets used up first; it determines the final amount of product formed

What the shape of a rate curve tells you

A typical rate graph plotting product formed against time has three distinct regions, and you must be able to describe each one:

  1. Steep section at the start. Both reactants are at their highest concentrations, so collisions are most frequent. The curve rises sharply because product is being formed quickly.
  2. Curving section in the middle. As reactants get used up, their concentrations fall, collisions become less frequent, and the gradient decreases. The curve bends.
  3. Plateau (horizontal flat section). The reaction has finished. The limiting reactant has been completely used up, so no more product can form. The line is flat, not still creeping upwards.

A rate graph plotting mass of reactant (or mass of flask) against time is simply the mirror image — it starts steep going downwards, curves, then flattens.

*Why the curve levels off*

The plateau is reached because the limiting reactant has run out. Once the limiting reactant is gone, no further product can be made, so the quantity on the y-axis stays constant. The height of the plateau is determined by the starting amount of limiting reactant — not by how fast the reaction was. This is the single most important idea behind sketching comparison curves, and it is the one most often dropped by candidates (see Examiner Report Insights below).

*Why the curve is steepest at the start*

At time zero, reactant concentration is at its maximum. From collision theory, rate depends on collision frequency, which depends on concentration. Maximum concentration → maximum collision frequency → maximum rate → steepest gradient. As the reaction proceeds, reactants are consumed, concentrations fall, and the gradient falls with them.

Comparing rate curves

A very common exam task is to sketch a second curve on the same axes — either for a faster reaction (catalyst added, higher temperature, higher concentration, smaller particle size) or for a reaction with a different amount of reactant.

ChangeEffect on initial gradientEffect on plateau heightEffect on time to finish
Higher temperature (same amounts)SteeperSameShorter
Higher concentration of one reactant (still limiting)SteeperHigher (more reactant available)May be shorter or similar
Catalyst added (same amounts)SteeperSameShorter
Powder instead of lumps (same mass)SteeperSameShorter

More reactant (same conditions)Same initial gradientHigherLonger

The two trickiest cases to draw correctly:

  • Same amount of reactant, faster conditions → the new curve is steeper at the start but must level off at the same height as the original. Both reactions form the same total amount of product because the same amount of limiting reactant is used.
  • Less reactant, same conditions → the new curve has roughly the same initial gradient (collision frequency is similar at the start) but levels off earlier and at a lower plateau.

A student collects hydrogen gas from the reaction between magnesium and dilute hydrochloric acid. The volume of gas in the syringe is recorded every 30 seconds:

Worked example 2 — Instantaneous rate using a tangent

The same student is asked to find the rate of reaction at exactly 45 seconds, using a tangent drawn on the same graph.

Step 1 — Draw the tangent. Place a ruler so it touches the curve at exactly one point (at t = 45 s) without crossing it. Make the line long — extend it well beyond the point of contact so you can read coordinates accurately.

Step 2 — Pick two points on the tangent that are easy to read. Choose points near the ends of the line, not next to each other, to reduce reading error. For this curve, suppose the tangent passes through (10 s, 8 cm³) and (80 s, 36 cm³).

*Step 3 — Calculate the gradient.*

`` rate = Δy ÷ Δx rate = (36 − 8) cm³ ÷ (80 − 10) s rate = 28 cm³ ÷ 70 s rate = 0.40 cm³/s ``

The instantaneous rate at 45 s is 0.40 cm³/s.

Notice that the instantaneous rate at 45 s (0.40 cm³/s) is similar to but distinct from the mean rate over the first 90 s (0.42 cm³/s). The mean rate averages out a steep early section and a shallower later section. The instantaneous rate captures only the gradient at one chosen moment — and gives a more honest answer to "how fast is the reaction going right now?".

Common errors when working with rate graphs

  • Levelling off at the wrong height. When sketching a comparison curve for the same amount of reactant under faster conditions, students draw the new curve flattening off above or below the original. It must flatten off at the same height — same reactant in, same product out.
  • Not levelling off at all. Some students extend their drawn curve upwards as a straight line, which would imply the reaction never finishes.
  • Tangent drawn through the curve instead of touching it. A tangent must touch the curve at one point only. A chord (a line that crosses the curve at two points) gives the mean rate between those points, not the instantaneous rate at one point.
  • Tangent too short. A 2 cm tangent makes Δy and Δx tiny and amplifies reading errors. Draw the tangent long.
  • Reading off coordinates that aren't on the tangent. Once the tangent is drawn, only points on the tangent can be used to find its gradient — not points on the curve.
  • Wrong units. The units of rate are always (y-axis units) ÷ (x-axis units). For example, cm³/s, cm³/min, g/s, or mol/dm³/s. Mixing time units (e.g. dividing a minute-scale reading by a second-scale interval) is a common slip.

  3. The curve levels off (plateaus) when the limiting reactant has been completely used up; the height of the plateau depends on the starting amount of that reactant.
  4. Mean rate between two times = (change in quantity) ÷ (change in time), with units of (y-axis units)/(x-axis units).
  5. Instantaneous rate at a single moment = gradient of a tangent drawn at that point on the curve.
  6. A tangent must touch the curve at one point only — never cross it, and never be too short to read accurately.
  7. When sketching a faster reaction with the same amount of reactant, draw a steeper curve that levels off at the same plateau height as the original.
  8. When sketching a reaction with less reactant, draw a curve with a similar starting gradient that levels off earlier and lower.
  9. The 2019 June Foundation report flagged that many candidates lose marks by failing to level off, or levelling off at the wrong height, when sketching comparison curves.
  10. Units of rate always come from the axes: cm³/s, cm³/min, g/s, or mol/dm³/s. Check the axis labels every time.
  11. Typical exam tariff is 2–3 marks: one for reading the graph, one for the calculation, one for the final answer with units.
  12. Rate-graph questions appear on Paper 2 of both 1CH0 (Separate) and 1SC0 (Combined), and very often follow on from core-practical data.
7.1.4.1 Catalysts F+H ▼

Catalysts

Spec reference: 7.6 — Describe a catalyst as a substance that speeds up the rate of a reaction without altering the products of the reaction, being itself unchanged chemically and in mass at the end of the reaction. 7.7 — Explain how the addition of a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction in terms of activation energy.

Tier and qualification: Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 2.

A catalyst is one of the most useful tools in chemistry — and one of the most commonly misunderstood at GCSE. It changes how fast a reaction goes, but does not change what the reaction produces or how much product is made. Getting the definition exactly right, and being able to explain the activation-energy effect properly, is essential for full marks at both tiers.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CatalystA substance that speeds up the rate of a reaction without altering the products of the reaction, being itself unchanged chemically and in mass at the end of the reaction
Activation energy (Eₐ)The minimum amount of energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur
Rate of reactionHow quickly a reactant is used up or a product is formed in a given time
Successful collisionA collision between particles that has enough energy (≥ Eₐ) and the correct orientation to produce a chemical change
EnzymeA biological catalyst, usually a protein, that speeds up reactions in living organisms

Building the full GCSE definition

The spec wording for 7.6 has four separate ideas built into it. Examiners frequently penalise answers that only mention one or two of them, so it helps to learn the definition as four chunks:

  1. A catalyst speeds up the rate of a reaction.
  2. It does not change the products of the reaction.
  3. It is unchanged chemically at the end.
  4. It is unchanged in mass at the end.

A common Foundation-tier mistake is to write "a catalyst speeds up a reaction" and stop there. That earns the first mark only. Higher-mark answers always include the "not used up / unchanged in mass" idea, because that is what distinguishes a catalyst from a reactant.

A catalyst is not a reactant. It does not appear in the overall balanced equation — instead it is often written above the reaction arrow:

N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) →[Fe] 2NH₃ (g)

This shows that iron speeds up the Haber process but is not consumed in it.

Why a catalyst does not change the products

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. The reactants are still the same reactants; the products are still the same products; only the route between them changes. Because the starting materials and the ending materials are identical to the uncatalysed reaction, the yield (how much product you can make from a given amount of reactant) is unchanged. A catalyst lets you reach equilibrium or completion faster, but does not let you make more product than the chemistry allows.

This is a frequent source of confusion. A catalyst:

  • Does change how fast the reaction happens
  • Does not change how much product is made
  • Does not change what the products are
  • Does not appear in the overall equation
  • Does not get used up

Explaining catalysts in terms of activation energy (spec 7.7)

This is the part of the spec point that carries the most explanation marks. To answer "Explain how a catalyst increases the rate of reaction" properly, you need to link together collision theory and the energy diagram.

The chain of reasoning is:

  1. For a reaction to happen, particles must collide.
  2. Only collisions with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy result in a reaction — these are successful collisions.
  3. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
  4. Because the activation energy is lower, a greater proportion of the colliding particles now have enough energy to react.
  5. The frequency of successful collisions per second increases.
  6. Therefore the rate of reaction increases.

Notice that a catalyst does not make the particles move faster (that would require raising the temperature). It does not increase the total number of collisions either. What it does is increase the fraction of collisions that are successful, by lowering the energy barrier.

A really useful exam phrasing is: "The catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of colliding particles have enough energy to react, increasing the rate."

Energy profile diagram

If you sketch a reaction profile (energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis), the catalysed pathway is drawn as a second curve sitting below the original one, between the same reactant level and the same product level.

Key features of a correct sketch:

  • Same start point (reactants) and same end point (products) for both curves
  • A "hump" representing the activation energy, with the catalysed hump lower than the uncatalysed one
  • The overall energy change (ΔH) of the reaction is the same in both cases — the difference is only the height of the hump, not where the start and end sit
  • Label the activation energy of the uncatalysed reaction (Eₐ) and the lower activation energy of the catalysed reaction (Eₐ catalysed)

The fact that ΔH is unchanged tells you that the catalyst does not change the energetics of the reaction overall — only the route taken to get there.

Enzymes — biological catalysts

Enzymes are catalysts produced by living organisms. They are usually proteins, and they speed up specific reactions inside cells. The everyday example used most often at GCSE is yeast, which contains enzymes that catalyse the fermentation of glucose to ethanol and carbon dioxide:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

Enzymes still obey the catalyst definition: they speed the reaction up, they don't change the products, and they are not used up.

Worked example — devising an experiment to show a catalyst's mass is unchanged

A typical 3-mark practical-design question asks you to devise a simple experiment to find out what happens to the mass of a solid catalyst during a reaction. A common context is manganese(IV) oxide (MnO₂) catalysing the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide:

2H₂O₂ (aq) → 2H₂O (l) + O₂ (g)

A method that scores all 3 marks:

  1. Weigh a sample of the solid catalyst on a balance — record the initial mass.
  2. Add the catalyst to the reaction mixture and allow the reaction to go to completion (e.g. until no more oxygen gas is produced).
  3. Filter the mixture to recover the catalyst, then wash it with distilled water and dry it in a low-temperature oven.
  4. Reweigh the dried catalyst on the same balance — compare the final mass with the initial mass.

If the catalyst is genuinely unchanged in mass, the two readings should be the same (within experimental uncertainty).

The marks usually break down as:

  • Mark 1: Weigh the catalyst before the reaction
  • Mark 2: Recover the catalyst at the end — filter, wash and dry (this is the step most candidates miss)
  • Mark 3: Reweigh and compare

7.1.5.1 Enzymes F+H ▼

Enzymes

Spec point 7.8 (F+H, 1CH0 + 1SC0): Recall that enzymes are biological catalysts and that enzymes are used in the production of alcoholic drinks. Examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.

This spec point sits at the join between two areas of the course. The chemistry it tests is rates of reaction — what a catalyst is and why catalysts speed reactions up — but the example you're expected to know is fermentation, which links forward into the organic chemistry of alcohols (Topic 9). Examiners regularly use a fermentation question to probe whether you understand the catalyst role of enzymes, not just whether you can recall the equation for fermentation.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CatalystA substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up, and without changing the products of the reaction
EnzymeA biological catalyst — a protein molecule, made by living cells, that speeds up a specific reaction
Activation energyThe minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur
FermentationThe reaction in which yeast converts a sugar (most commonly glucose) into ethanol and carbon dioxide
DenaturationThe permanent change in shape of an enzyme (or other protein) caused by heat or extreme pH, which stops the enzyme from working

Why enzymes count as catalysts

An enzyme satisfies the full catalyst definition:

  1. It increases the rate of a specific reaction by providing a route with a lower activation energy.
  2. It is not used up during the reaction — the enzyme molecules at the end of the reaction are the same as those at the start (in principle; in practice, they wear out over time, but they are not chemically consumed by the reaction they catalyse).
  3. It does not change the products of the reaction.

The "biological" label simply tells you that enzymes are made by living organisms (yeast, bacteria, plants, animals) and that they are made of protein. From a chemistry-exam point of view, anything that is true of catalysts is true of enzymes — including the fact that the enthalpy change of the reaction (ΔH) is unchanged when an enzyme is present, because the enzyme only alters the activation energy.

The fermentation example

The Edexcel specification names alcoholic drinks specifically as the worked example you must know. The chemistry is:

C₆H₁₂O₆ (aq) → 2C₂H₅OH (aq) + 2CO₂ (g)

In words: glucose is broken down by enzymes in yeast to give ethanol and carbon dioxide.

The reaction is carried out:

  • in an aqueous solution (sugar dissolved in water)
  • in the absence of oxygen (anaerobic conditions) — if oxygen is present, the yeast respires aerobically instead, producing CO₂ and water rather than ethanol
  • at a warm temperature, typically around 30–40 °C
  • using yeast, which provides the enzymes

The key point for this spec point is that the catalyst in fermentation is an enzyme produced by yeast — sometimes called zymase in older textbooks. The yeast cell is the source; the enzyme molecule is the catalyst. A common mark-losing answer says "the yeast catalyses the reaction." That is loose — what the examiner wants is "the yeast contains enzymes which catalyse the reaction" or equivalent.

Why the temperature matters — the most-examined idea

Fermentation has to be run inside a fairly narrow temperature window. Understanding why is the single highest-yielding piece of chemistry on this spec point because it lets you answer the standard "explain the choice of temperature" question.

*Why temperature is raised above room temperature*

Higher temperature means the particles move faster and collide more often, and a greater proportion of collisions have energy above the activation energy. This is the standard collision-theory rate argument — the warmer it is, the faster the reaction. So a fermentation carried out at, say, 5 °C would be very slow and uneconomical.

*Why temperature is not raised much higher than about 40 °C*

Enzymes are proteins. A protein's catalytic activity depends on its three-dimensional shape — specifically the shape of its active site, where the substrate (here, glucose) binds. If the temperature rises too high, the bonds holding the protein in its folded shape break, the active site loses its shape, and the substrate can no longer fit. The enzyme is then said to be denatured.

Denaturation is permanent — cooling the mixture back down does not restore activity. Once an enzyme has been heat-denatured, it stops working entirely.

So the fermentation temperature is a compromise: warm enough to give a reasonable rate, but not so warm that the enzymes are destroyed. Around 30–40 °C is the typical sweet spot.

*Why the temperature is not lowered to room temperature or below*

Below about 15 °C the enzyme is still intact but the reaction is too slow to be commercially useful. The molecules have less kinetic energy, fewer collisions occur per second, and a smaller fraction of those collisions reach the activation energy.

Both still satisfy the basic catalyst definition: speed up the reaction, not used up, no change to products.

Worked example — comparing two temperature choices

A student is asked: "Fermentation is carried out at 30 °C rather than 80 °C. Explain why." This is a 2-mark question — one mark for the rate point and one for the enzyme/denaturing point.

A model answer:

> At 30 °C the enzymes in the yeast are active and catalyse the conversion of glucose to ethanol. At 80 °C the enzymes would be denatured, so the active site changes shape and the reaction cannot take place. 30 °C is also warm enough for the reaction to proceed at a reasonable rate.

The two scoring ideas are:

  1. Enzymes are denatured at 80 °C (high temperature destroys the enzyme).
  2. The temperature must come from the yeast — i.e. you must tie the enzymes to the yeast, not just say "it's too hot." (See examiner-report insight below.)

A common partial-credit answer is "80 °C is too hot for the enzymes." That gets the denaturing idea but does not link it to yeast as the source, which is what the mark scheme is looking for on a 2-mark item.

Why a catalyst speeds reactions up — the underlying mechanism

Even though you don't need an enzyme-specific mechanism, knowing the generic catalyst mechanism makes "explain" questions much easier:

  • A reaction only happens when reactant particles collide with at least the activation energy.
  • A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
  • Because the activation energy is lower, a greater proportion of the colliding particles now have enough energy to react, so the rate goes up.
  • For an enzyme, the substrate binds to a specific site (the active site) on the enzyme; the geometry of binding is what lowers the activation energy.

The shape of the active site is what makes enzymes specific — only the right substrate fits — and it is also what makes them vulnerable to heat: lose the shape and you lose the catalysis.

7.2.1.1 Heat Energy & Temperature changes F+H ▼

Heat Energy & Temperature changes

Spec point 7.9: changes in heat energy accompany salts dissolving in water, neutralisation, displacement and precipitation reactions; temperature changes measured in solution reflect those heat changes. Foundation + Higher tier · Paper 2 · 1CH0 + 1SC0.

Whenever a chemical change happens in aqueous solution, the bonds between particles are broken and new ones are made. Bond breaking absorbs energy from the surroundings; bond making releases energy back to the surroundings. The difference between these two energy flows shows up as a change in the temperature of the solution — a thermometer dipped into the reaction mixture is, in effect, measuring the heat energy change of the reaction itself.

This spec point is the gateway to the rest of Topic 7.2. Before you can label a reaction as exothermic or endothermic, before you can do any calorimetry calculation, and before you can draw a reaction profile, you need to be confident that a temperature change in solution = a heat energy change in the reaction. That is the single idea this spec point is built around.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Heat energy changeThe energy transferred to or from the reaction mixture when a chemical reaction occurs
Temperature change (ΔT)The difference between the final and initial temperature of the solution, measured in °C
ExothermicA reaction that releases heat energy to the surroundings, causing the temperature of the solution to rise
EndothermicA reaction that absorbs heat energy from the surroundings, causing the temperature of the solution to fall
Surroundings (in solution)Everything around the reacting particles — in practice, the water of the solution itself
DissolvingThe process by which solute particles separate and mix uniformly with water molecules
NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base (or alkali) to produce a salt and water
DisplacementA more reactive metal pushing a less reactive metal out of its compound in solution
PrecipitationThe formation of an insoluble solid when two soluble solutions are mixed

Why a temperature change tells you about heat energy

The water in a solution acts as a built-in calorimeter. Water has a high specific heat capacity (4.18 J/g/°C), so it absorbs or supplies large amounts of heat without changing temperature too quickly. When a reaction in that water releases heat, the water's particles gain kinetic energy and the thermometer reads a higher temperature. When a reaction in that water absorbs heat, the water's particles lose kinetic energy and the thermometer reads a lower temperature.

So the direction of the temperature change tells you the direction of the heat flow:

  • Temperature rises → heat has been released by the reaction → exothermic
  • Temperature falls → heat has been absorbed by the reaction → endothermic

This is the conceptual link that examiners test most often, and it is the link that students most often get the wrong way round. The temperature of the solution rises because the reaction has given energy out, not because the reaction has taken energy in. (See Examiner Report Insights below.)

The four reaction types named in the spec

Edexcel names four specific situations in which you can observe a heat change by measuring a temperature change. You should be able to give an example of each.

Reaction typeTypical exampleUsual direction of heat change
Salts dissolving in waterAmmonium nitrate dissolving in water (endothermic — temperature falls); calcium chloride dissolving in water (exothermic — temperature rises)Can be either direction depending on the salt
NeutralisationHydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + waterAlmost always exothermic
DisplacementZinc added to copper(II) sulfate solution → zinc sulfate + copperAlmost always exothermic
PrecipitationSilver nitrate + sodium chloride → silver chloride (s) + sodium nitrateAlmost always exothermic

*Salts dissolving — why both directions are possible*

Dissolving has two stages: breaking the ionic lattice (endothermic — energy in) and hydrating the separated ions (exothermic — energy out). Whether the overall temperature change is positive or negative depends on which of those two stages releases or absorbs more energy. Ammonium nitrate is the classic endothermic example used in chemical cold packs; anhydrous calcium chloride is the classic exothermic example used in self-heating cans.

*Neutralisation — why the temperature rises*

The defining step of any strong-acid + strong-alkali neutralisation is H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l). Forming the O–H bonds in water releases more energy than was needed to separate the ions, so the surrounding solution warms up.

*Displacement — why the temperature rises*

A more reactive metal loses electrons more easily than the less reactive metal it displaces. The electron transfer ends up forming the more stable arrangement, releasing energy as heat. Zinc displacing copper from copper(II) sulfate is the standard schools example and gives a clearly measurable rise.

*Precipitation — why the temperature rises*

When two solutions are mixed and an insoluble solid forms, the ions in the precipitate are held in a more stable, lower-energy lattice than they were as free hydrated ions in solution. Forming that lattice releases energy to the surrounding water.

Calculating a temperature change

The arithmetic here is deliberately simple, but the sign convention is the part that trips students up.

`` ΔT = T(final) − T(initial) ``

  • If ΔT is positive (final temperature higher than initial), the reaction is exothermic
  • If ΔT is negative (final temperature lower than initial), the reaction is endothermic

*Worked example 1 — exothermic neutralisation*

25.0 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid is added to 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution in a polystyrene cup. The initial temperature of each solution was 19.0 °C. The maximum temperature after mixing was 25.5 °C.

Step 1: identify the initial and final temperatures.

  • T(initial) = 19.0 °C
  • T(final) = 25.5 °C

Step 2: calculate ΔT.

  • ΔT = 25.5 − 19.0 = +6.5 °C

Step 3: interpret.

  • Temperature rose, so the reaction released heat to the surroundings → exothermic.

*Worked example 2 — endothermic dissolving*

5.00 g of ammonium nitrate is added to 50.0 cm³ of water at 21.0 °C. The lowest temperature recorded after stirring was 14.0 °C.

Step 1: identify temperatures.

  • T(initial) = 21.0 °C
  • T(final) = 14.0 °C

Step 2: ΔT = 14.0 − 21.0 = −7.0 °C

Step 3: interpret.

  • Temperature fell, so the dissolving process absorbed heat from the surroundings → endothermic.

Common student mistakes to avoid

  • Saying that an exothermic reaction "takes in" energy. It releases it. The temperature rise is evidence of energy leaving the reaction and going into the surroundings.
  • Saying that the temperature of the reaction falls in an endothermic process. It is the temperature of the surrounding solution that falls, because heat has been drawn out of it into the reacting system.
  • Forgetting the sign of ΔT. Write the subtraction the right way round: T(final) − T(initial), not the other way.
  • Calling dissolving "always exothermic" or "always endothermic". It depends on the salt.

Mathematical Skills

This spec point sits in maths skills band 1a / 1c — arithmetic computation. You need to be able to:

`` ΔT = T(final) − T(initial) ``

Procedure:

  1. Read the starting (initial) temperature of the solution from the table or thermometer scale.
  2. Read the final temperature reached after the reaction.
  3. Subtract: ΔT = T(final) − T(initial).
  4. State whether ΔT is positive (temperature rose, exothermic) or negative (temperature fell, endothermic).
  5. If asked to compare several salts/reactions, compare the size (magnitude) of ΔT to find which released or absorbed the most heat energy.

No specific heat capacity calculations are required for this spec point — those come in 7.2.1.3 Calorimetry.

7.2.1.2 Exothermic Reactions F+H ▼

Exothermic Reactions

Spec point 7.10: Describe an exothermic change or reaction as one in which heat energy is given out. Examined on Foundation and Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Exothermic reactionA reaction in which heat energy is given out (transferred from the chemicals to the surroundings)
SurroundingsEverything around the reacting chemicals — usually the solvent (water), the reaction vessel and the air
Temperature change (ΔT)The difference between the final and starting temperature of the reaction mixture, in °C
SystemThe reacting chemicals themselves (the reactants turning into products)
Heat transferThe movement of thermal energy from a hotter region to a cooler region

What "exothermic" actually means

The word exo- comes from the Greek for "outside" and -thermic relates to heat. So an exothermic reaction is one in which heat moves out of the reaction mixture and into the surroundings. The chemicals release energy that they previously stored in their bonds, and that energy spreads into the water, glassware and air around them.

The most reliable everyday clue that a reaction is exothermic is that the surroundings get hotter. If you carry out the reaction in solution and stick a thermometer into the beaker, the temperature reading rises — because the energy released by the reaction is warming the solvent.

This is the single most important idea to lock in. Heat energy is given out, which means the temperature of the surroundings (the solution you are measuring) goes up. Students very often try to reason the other way around — assuming that "energy goes in" causes a temperature rise — and this is exactly the misconception that examiners pick out year after year.

Why a temperature rise tells you a reaction is exothermic

A thermometer measures the temperature of whatever it is dipped into. In a school lab, you nearly always dip the thermometer into the solution in the beaker — the surroundings of the reacting particles. So:

  • If the reaction gives out heat, that heat warms the solution, and the thermometer reading goes up.
  • If the reaction takes in heat, it pulls that heat from the solution, and the thermometer reading goes down.

So a temperature rise in the reaction mixture means the chemicals must have released energy — i.e. the reaction is exothermic. A temperature fall means the chemicals must have absorbed energy from the surroundings — i.e. the reaction is endothermic.

A neat way to phrase the logic in an explain question is:

"The temperature of the solution increased, which shows that heat energy was transferred from the reaction to the surroundings, so the reaction is exothermic."

Three things are doing the work in that sentence: the observation (temperature increased), the direction of heat transfer (from reaction to surroundings), and the classification (exothermic). Examiners want all three.

Common examples of exothermic reactions

Several reactions you meet across the GCSE course are exothermic. Knowing a few concrete examples is useful for explain and describe questions:

Combustion is the biggest and most familiar exothermic process — every time fuel burns it gives out heat (and usually light). Neutralisation of an acid by an alkali is the classic school example, because the temperature rise is easy to measure with a polystyrene cup and a thermometer.

Everyday uses of exothermic reactions

Exothermic reactions are useful precisely because they give out heat. Some everyday applications you should be able to name:

  • Hand warmers — disposable hand warmers use the slow oxidation of iron filings: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. The reaction releases heat over several hours, warming your hands.
  • Self-heating cans — drinks cans with a separate compartment containing calcium oxide and water. When mixed, they react exothermically to warm the drink: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂.
  • Combustion of fuels — burning natural gas, petrol or diesel releases heat that is used for cooking, heating buildings and powering engines.
  • Respiration — the controlled exothermic oxidation of glucose in your cells provides the energy your body needs to stay warm and move.

Reading the energy story: where does the heat come from?

At GCSE you do not need to discuss bond energies in detail for spec point 7.10, but it helps to know the underlying picture so explain answers feel less like memorised slogans:

  • Reactant molecules contain chemical energy stored in their bonds.
  • During an exothermic reaction, the products end up with less stored chemical energy than the reactants had.
  • The difference is released as heat (and sometimes light), warming the surroundings.

So an exothermic reaction transfers energy from chemical energy to heat energy, moving it out of the system and into the surroundings. The total energy of the universe is unchanged — energy is simply being redistributed.

Measuring temperature change (linking to the practical)

In the core practical on temperature changes, you mix the two reactants in a polystyrene cup (cheap, lightweight, good insulator) and record temperatures with a thermometer:

  1. Measure the starting temperature of the solution (T₁).
  2. Add the second reactant and stir.
  3. Record the maximum temperature reached (T₂) for an exothermic reaction.
  4. Calculate temperature change: ΔT = T₂ − T₁.

A positive ΔT (final higher than starting) means exothermic. A negative ΔT means endothermic.

Worked example — classifying a reaction from results

A student mixes 25 cm³ of hydrochloric acid with 25 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution in a polystyrene cup. They record:

  • Starting temperature of acid = 20.0 °C
  • Highest temperature reached after mixing = 27.4 °C

Step 1 — calculate the temperature change:

`` ΔT = T₂ − T₁ ΔT = 27.4 − 20.0 ΔT = +7.4 °C ``

Step 2 — interpret the sign and direction of heat transfer:

The temperature of the solution increased by 7.4 °C. The solution is the surroundings, so heat energy must have been transferred from the reacting chemicals to the surroundings.

Step 3 — classify the reaction:

Because heat is given out, the reaction is exothermic.

A full explain-answer worth 2 marks would read:

"The temperature of the solution rose by 7.4 °C, showing that heat energy was given out by the reaction to the surroundings, so the reaction is exothermic."

7.2.1.3 Endothermic Reactions F+H ▼

Endothermic Reactions

Spec point 7.11: Describe an endothermic change or reaction as one in which heat energy is taken in. Examined on both Foundation and Higher tier across Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

An endothermic reaction is one of the two ways a chemical reaction can transfer energy with its surroundings. Whereas an exothermic reaction gives heat out, an endothermic reaction takes heat in. That single directional difference drives everything you need to be able to do at GCSE: recognise endothermic reactions from temperature data, classify named examples, sketch their reaction profiles, and explain the temperature change in terms of energy flow.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Endothermic reactionA reaction in which heat energy is taken in from the surroundings, causing the surroundings to cool down (the temperature of the surroundings falls)
SurroundingsEverything around the reacting chemicals — usually the solution, the test tube, and the air. In a school experiment, the water in a beaker or the solution itself is the surroundings being measured
Heat energy changeThe transfer of thermal energy between the reaction (the system) and its surroundings
Temperature decreaseThe observable effect of an endothermic reaction on the surroundings — the thermometer reading drops

Why an endothermic reaction makes the surroundings get colder

Energy is needed to break chemical bonds in the reactants, and energy is released when new bonds form in the products. In an endothermic reaction, more energy is taken in to break the reactant bonds than is released when the product bonds form. The extra energy needed has to come from somewhere — and that "somewhere" is the surroundings. The reaction pulls thermal energy out of the water, solution, or air around it, so the surroundings cool down and the thermometer reading falls.

It is a common student mistake to think that a temperature rise is endothermic because "energy is being taken in". This is back-to-front. If the surroundings get hotter, energy is flowing out of the reaction into the surroundings — that is exothermic. If the surroundings get colder, energy is flowing into the reaction from the surroundings — that is endothermic. You always classify by what happens to the surroundings, not by what you imagine is happening inside the reaction beaker.

Common examples of endothermic reactions

You are expected to recognise these as standard endothermic processes:

  • Thermal decomposition reactions — for example, heating copper carbonate or calcium carbonate to break them down. These reactions only happen when heat is continuously supplied; the moment heating stops, the reaction stops.
  • The reaction between citric acid and sodium hydrogen carbonate (sodium bicarbonate) in water — a classic demonstration where the temperature of the solution falls visibly.
  • Dissolving ammonium nitrate (or other ammonium salts) in water — used in instant cold packs for sports injuries. The salt absorbs heat from the water as it dissolves, so the pack feels cold.
  • Photosynthesis — plants take in light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen.

A useful test: if a reaction needs continuous heating to keep going, it is endothermic (the heat is being absorbed). If a reaction releases heat once started and keeps going on its own, it is exothermic.

Comparing endothermic and exothermic reactions

FeatureEndothermicExothermic
Direction of heat energy transferHeat taken in from surroundingsHeat given out to surroundings
Effect on temperature of surroundingsDecreases (surroundings cool down)Increases (surroundings warm up)
Energy of products vs reactantsProducts have more energy than reactantsProducts have less energy than reactants
Bond breaking vs bond makingEnergy to break bonds > energy released making bondsEnergy released making bonds > energy to break bonds
Typical examplesThermal decomposition; dissolving ammonium nitrate; citric acid + sodium hydrogen carbonate; photosynthesisCombustion; neutralisation; respiration; most displacement reactions
Reaction profileProducts higher than reactantsProducts lower than reactants

Reaction (energy) profile for an endothermic reaction

A reaction profile is a simple energy diagram showing how the energy of the chemicals changes as the reaction proceeds. For an endothermic reaction:

  • The reactants sit at a lower energy level on the left.
  • The products sit at a higher energy level on the right.
  • The vertical gap between them represents the energy taken in from the surroundings.
  • The reaction has to climb that energy "hill", so the curve goes up overall from reactants to products.

This is the mirror image of an exothermic profile, where the products sit lower than the reactants.

Worked example — identifying an endothermic reaction from temperature data

A student mixes solid ammonium nitrate with 50 cm³ of water in a polystyrene cup. They record the temperature before mixing and the lowest temperature reached after mixing.

  • Starting temperature of water: 22.0 °C
  • Lowest temperature reached: 14.5 °C

*Step 1 — Calculate the temperature change.*

Temperature change = final temperature − starting temperature Temperature change = 14.5 − 22.0 = −7.5 °C

*Step 2 — Interpret the sign.*

The temperature has decreased by 7.5 °C. The surroundings (the water) have lost heat energy.

*Step 3 — Classify the reaction.*

Because the surroundings cooled down, heat energy was taken in by the reaction. This is therefore an endothermic process.

The same procedure works for any results table you are given in an exam. You do not need a fancy formula — just:

`` temperature change = final temperature − starting temperature ``

A negative result (temperature fell) means endothermic. A positive result (temperature rose) means exothermic.

How to write a full classification answer

Exam questions on this spec point almost always want you to do two things: state whether the reaction is endothermic or exothermic, and justify that classification using the data given. A typical 2-mark answer needs both halves:

  • Mark 1 — Identify the change: "The temperature of the surroundings decreased / fell from 22.0 °C to 14.5 °C."
  • Mark 2 — Link this to the reaction type: "This shows the reaction is endothermic because heat energy is taken in from the surroundings."

You will rarely score full marks for just writing "endothermic" with no reasoning. Always reference the temperature change and link it to energy being taken in.

7.2.2.1 Bonds & Energy Changes F+H ▼

Bonds & Energy Changes

Spec 7.12: The breaking of bonds is endothermic and the making of bonds is exothermic.

Spec 7.13: The overall heat change is exothermic if more energy is released forming product bonds than is absorbed breaking reactant bonds, and endothermic if less energy is released than was absorbed.

Examined on Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

This spec point is the mechanistic explanation behind why some reactions release heat and others absorb it. Reaction profiles tell you that a reaction is exothermic or endothermic; bond energetics tells you why. Examiners expect candidates to be able to link the two ideas in a written explanation, and they consistently report that the linking step is the part students find hardest.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Bond breakingThe separation of two atoms previously joined by a chemical bond; an endothermic process because energy must be supplied to overcome the attractive forces holding the atoms together
Bond makingThe formation of a new chemical bond between atoms; an exothermic process because energy is released as the new attractive forces lock the atoms together at a lower potential energy
Exothermic reactionA reaction in which more energy is released by forming new bonds in the products than is absorbed in breaking existing bonds in the reactants — the surroundings get hotter
Endothermic reactionA reaction in which less energy is released by forming new bonds in the products than is absorbed in breaking existing bonds in the reactants — the surroundings get colder
Bond energyThe energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond, usually given in kJ/mol (used quantitatively at Higher tier in spec point 7.2.3)

Why bond breaking is endothermic

A covalent bond is the result of two nuclei sharing a pair of electrons. The shared pair sits between the nuclei and is electrostatically attracted to both. That attraction is what holds the molecule together — it is a low-energy, stable arrangement.

To pull the two atoms apart you have to put energy in to overcome this attraction. The bonded atoms are at a lower energy than the separated atoms, so separating them requires lifting them up the energy scale. Anything that requires an input of energy from the surroundings is, by definition, endothermic.

A useful mental picture: imagine the bond as a stretched spring holding two balls together at their lowest energy point. You have to do work — supply energy — to pull the balls apart. The atoms gain that energy as potential energy. Heat is taken in from the surroundings, which is why bond breaking on its own would cool the surroundings.

Why bond making is exothermic

Forming a bond is the reverse process. Two separated atoms (or fragments) come together, the nuclei attract the shared electrons, and the system drops to a lower, more stable energy. The energy difference is released to the surroundings as heat.

Anything that releases energy to the surroundings is, by definition, exothermic. The bonded state is energetically downhill from the separated state, so forming bonds is always energetically favourable on its own.

A simple rule that examiners want you to be able to recall:

  • Breaking bonds → energy in → endothermic
  • Making bonds → energy out → exothermic

These two ideas are spec 7.12 and they are the building blocks of every other energy-change question in this section.

Why the overall reaction can go either way

Every chemical reaction involves both processes — old bonds in the reactants must break, and new bonds in the products must form. The overall heat change depends on which one releases or absorbs more energy.

Think of it as an energy balance sheet:

  • Energy in (debit) = total energy needed to break all the bonds in the reactants
  • Energy out (credit) = total energy released when all the bonds in the products form

The overall heat energy change of the reaction is the difference between these two totals:

`` Overall energy change = (energy absorbed breaking reactant bonds) − (energy released making product bonds) ``

If more energy is released by bond making than was needed for bond breaking, there is a net release of energy to the surroundings. The reaction is exothermic and the surroundings warm up.

If less energy is released by bond making than was needed for bond breaking, there is a net absorption of energy from the surroundings. The reaction is endothermic and the surroundings cool down.

This is exactly what spec 7.13 is testing — and the comparison phrasing matters. Examiners are very strict about candidates explicitly comparing the two amounts of energy, not just describing them separately.

Worked example: explaining the combustion of methane

Combustion is a classic exothermic reaction. Take the combustion of methane:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

*Step 1 — Identify bonds broken in the reactants:*

  • 4 × C–H bonds in CH₄
  • 2 × O=O bonds in O₂ (because there are 2 molecules of O₂)

Energy must be absorbed from the surroundings to break all six of these bonds.

*Step 2 — Identify bonds made in the products:*

  • 2 × C=O bonds in CO₂
  • 4 × O–H bonds in 2 H₂O molecules (each H₂O has 2 O–H bonds)

Energy is released to the surroundings as these six bonds form.

*Step 3 — Compare:*

The total energy released when the new C=O and O–H bonds form is greater than the total energy absorbed breaking the C–H and O=O bonds. Therefore, more energy comes out than went in. The reaction is exothermic and the surroundings get hotter.

Notice how the explanation links every step — it doesn't just list bonds broken and bonds made; it states which energy total is bigger and what that means. That comparison is exactly what examiners are looking for in a "3-mark explain" question.

Worked example: explaining an endothermic reaction

The thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate:

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

The reactant has strong ionic and covalent bonds inside the carbonate that must be broken. The products are calcium oxide (ionic) and carbon dioxide (with two C=O bonds). When this reaction is carried out, you have to keep supplying heat — the test tube cools the moment you remove the Bunsen.

Step 1 — Energy absorbed: Energy is taken in from the surroundings to break the bonds within the CaCO₃ lattice.

Step 2 — Energy released: Energy is released when the new bonds in CaO and the C=O bonds in CO₂ form.

Step 3 — Compare: Less energy is released by bond making in the products than is absorbed by bond breaking in the reactants. There is a net absorption of energy from the surroundings, so the reaction is endothermic and the surroundings get colder (which is why you have to keep heating).

Common student errors (and how to avoid them)

Reversing the rule. Many students mix up which way round bond breaking and bond making go. A quick memory aid: "breaking" sounds like effort — you have to put energy in to break something. "making" rewards you with energy out.

Counting bonds instead of weighing energies. Saying "more bonds are broken than made" misses the point — what matters is the amount of energy absorbed and released, not the number of bonds. Two strong bonds breaking can absorb more energy than three weaker bonds breaking.

Forgetting to compare. A common error is to write "energy is absorbed breaking bonds and released making bonds" and stop there. That describes both processes but doesn't say which one is bigger — so it can't explain why the reaction is exothermic (or endothermic). Always finish with a comparison sentence.

Wrong terminology. Using vague words like "energy is used up" or "energy is given off" without saying which process gives it off can lose marks. Use precise language: bond breaking absorbs energy from the surroundings; bond forming releases energy to the surroundings.

7.2.3.1 Bond Energy Calculations H only ▼

Bond Energy Calculations

Spec point 7.14: Calculate the energy change in a reaction given the energies of bonds (in kJ mol⁻¹). Higher tier only — appears on Paper 2 (1CH0/2H) for both Separate Chemistry and Combined Science.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Bond energyThe energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in the gaseous state, measured in kJ mol⁻¹
Bond breakingAn endothermic process — energy must be put in to pull bonded atoms apart
Bond makingAn exothermic process — energy is released out when new bonds form between atoms
Energy change (ΔH)The overall energy change of a reaction; equals (energy in to break bonds) − (energy out when bonds form)
Exothermic reactionA reaction that releases energy to the surroundings; ΔH is negative
Endothermic reactionA reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings; ΔH is positive

Why bond energies determine the overall energy change

Every chemical reaction involves rearranging atoms. To rearrange atoms you must first break the bonds in the reactants, then make new bonds to form the products. Breaking a bond always requires an input of energy (endothermic), while making a bond always releases energy (exothermic). The overall energy change of the reaction is simply the difference between these two quantities.

If the bonds in the products are stronger overall (more energy released making them than was put in to break the reactant bonds), the reaction releases net energy and is exothermic — ΔH is negative. If the bonds in the products are weaker overall (less energy released making them than was put in), the reaction takes in net energy and is endothermic — ΔH is positive.

This is the central idea you must carry through every calculation: breaking = energy in (positive), making = energy out (negative), and the sign on your final answer tells the examiner whether the reaction is exo- or endothermic.

The standard formula

There are two equivalent ways to write the calculation, and either will earn full marks if used correctly:

`` ΔH = (sum of bond energies of bonds broken) − (sum of bond energies of bonds made) ``

or equivalently:

`` ΔH = (energy in) − (energy out) ``

The result is in kJ mol⁻¹. A negative value means exothermic; a positive value means endothermic.

Step-by-step procedure

  1. Write out the balanced equation using displayed (structural) formulae. This forces you to see every individual bond — for example, C–H, C=C, H–Cl — rather than just molecular formulae.
  2. Count every bond in the reactants. List how many of each type of bond must be broken.
  3. Count every bond in the products. List how many of each type of bond must be made.
  4. Multiply each bond count by the bond energy (given in the question, in kJ mol⁻¹).
  5. Sum the bonds-broken total (energy in).
  6. Sum the bonds-made total (energy out).
  7. Subtract: ΔH = (energy in) − (energy out).
  8. Give the answer with the correct sign, the correct units (kJ mol⁻¹), and the correct conclusion (exothermic if negative, endothermic if positive).

The four marks on a typical exam question correspond directly to steps 5, 6, 7 and 8: one mark for the bonds-broken total, one for the bonds-made total, one for the subtraction, and one for the correctly signed final answer.

Worked example — combustion of methane

Calculate the energy change for the complete combustion of methane:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Bond energies (kJ mol⁻¹):

BondBond energy (kJ mol⁻¹)
C–H412
O=O498
C=O805
O–H463

*Step 1 — bonds broken (reactants):*

  • 1 × CH₄ contains 4 × C–H bonds = 4 × 412 = 1648 kJ mol⁻¹
  • 2 × O₂ contains 2 × O=O bonds = 2 × 498 = 996 kJ mol⁻¹
  • Total energy in = 1648 + 996 = 2644 kJ mol⁻¹

*Step 2 — bonds made (products):*

  • 1 × CO₂ contains 2 × C=O bonds = 2 × 805 = 1610 kJ mol⁻¹
  • 2 × H₂O contains 4 × O–H bonds = 4 × 463 = 1852 kJ mol⁻¹
  • Total energy out = 1610 + 1852 = 3462 kJ mol⁻¹

*Step 3 — subtract:*

ΔH = (energy in) − (energy out) = 2644 − 3462 = −818 kJ mol⁻¹

Step 4 — interpret: the answer is negative, so the combustion of methane is exothermic (which matches what you would predict for a fuel burning).

Worked example — hydrogenation-style reaction (C=C + H–Cl)

Calculate the energy change for the addition reaction:

C₂H₄ + HCl → C₂H₅Cl

Drawing the displayed formulae:

  • C₂H₄ has 1 × C=C bond and 4 × C–H bonds
  • HCl has 1 × H–Cl bond
  • C₂H₅Cl has 1 × C–C bond, 5 × C–H bonds and 1 × C–Cl bond

BondBond energy (kJ mol⁻¹)

*Bonds broken (reactants):*

  • 1 × C=C = 612
  • 4 × C–H = 4 × 413 = 1652
  • 1 × H–Cl = 432
  • Total energy in = 612 + 1652 + 432 = 2696 kJ mol⁻¹

*Bonds made (products):*

  • 1 × C–C = 347

Shortcut: cancelling unchanged bonds

In the second worked example, four of the C–H bonds are unchanged between reactant and product (you broke 4 and remade 4). You can save time by only counting the bonds that actually change. In the example above, the bonds that change are:

  • Broken: 1 × C=C (612) + 1 × H–Cl (432) = 1044
  • Made: 1 × C–C (347) + 1 × C–H (413) + 1 × C–Cl (346) = 1106

ΔH = 1044 − 1106 = −62 kJ mol⁻¹

Same answer, much less arithmetic. This shortcut is mathematically equivalent and earns full marks — but only use it once you are fully confident counting bonds, because it is easier to miscount which bonds are "new". The safest method in an exam is the full count, especially if you have time.

Why bond energies are average values

Bond energies in data tables are average (mean) values, taken across many different molecules in which that bond appears. The actual energy of a particular C–H bond in methane is slightly different from a C–H bond in ethanol, because the rest of the molecule pulls on the electrons in a slightly different way.

This means a bond energy calculation gives a value that is close to the true experimental ΔH, but not exactly equal. You don't need to comment on this unless asked — but it explains why textbook calculated values sometimes disagree by a few kJ mol⁻¹ with experimentally measured values.

Common student mistakes to avoid

  • Sign error. Forgetting the minus sign (or adding one when the reaction is endothermic) is the single most common source of lost marks. Always write the sign clearly.
  • Doing the subtraction the wrong way round. It is broken − made, not made − broken. If you reverse it, you get the right magnitude but the wrong sign.
  • Forgetting to multiply by the number of bonds. CH₄ has four C–H bonds, not one. 2H₂O has four O–H bonds, not two.
  • Counting only the molecular formula, not the structural formula. Drawing the displayed formula on your question paper makes counting unambiguous.
  • Mistaking double bonds for single bonds. C=C is not the same as C–C; O=O is not the same as O–O. Each has a separate, much larger bond energy.
  • Carrying through arithmetic errors from the calculator. Read your calculator carefully and write each subtotal on the page — that way the examiner can award the bonds-broken and bonds-made marks even if a later step goes wrong.

Mathematical Skills

This calculation maps to Mathematical Skills 1a and 1c (arithmetic computation): summing and subtracting bond-energy values in kJ mol⁻¹, with attention to the correct sign on the final answer.

`` ΔH = Σ(bond energies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond energies of bonds made) ``

  • Σ means "sum of" — add up every bond energy on that side.
  • A negative ΔH means exothermic (more energy released making bonds than was needed to break them).
  • A positive ΔH means endothermic (less energy released making bonds than was needed to break them).

Reaction Profiles

Spec point 7.16: Draw and label reaction profiles for endothermic and exothermic reactions, identifying activation energy. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

A reaction profile is a labelled diagram that shows how the energy of the chemicals changes as a reaction proceeds from reactants to products. It is one of the most heavily diagram-based topics in Section 7 — you are expected to draw the curve, label the axes, and identify specific features such as the activation energy and the overall energy change.

Because the question is almost always "draw and label", precision matters more than prose. A reaction profile that points the right way but is missing a label, or has the activation energy arrow drawn between the wrong two points, will lose marks even if the rest of the diagram is correct.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Reaction profileA diagram showing the energy of the reacting particles plotted against the progress (or "course") of the reaction
Activation energy (Eₐ)The minimum amount of energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur
Exothermic reactionA reaction that transfers energy from the chemicals to the surroundings, so the products have less energy than the reactants
Endothermic reactionA reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, so the products have more energy than the reactants
Overall energy change (ΔH)The difference in energy between the products and the reactants
ReactantsThe chemicals at the start of the reaction (left-hand side of the profile)
ProductsThe chemicals formed at the end of the reaction (right-hand side of the profile)

Why a reaction profile has a "hump" in the middle

Even reactions that release energy overall still have to get started. Before bonds can be broken in the reactants, the colliding particles must absorb enough energy to weaken and break those existing bonds. This initial energy investment is the activation energy.

Once the reactants have absorbed enough energy to reach the top of the "hump", the bonds in the reactants break and new bonds form to make the products. Bond making releases energy, so the curve falls again. The height of the hump above the reactants line is the activation energy. The vertical gap between reactants and products is the overall energy change.

This is the central misconception that examiners flag (see Examiner Report Insights below): the activation energy is not the gap between the reactants and the peak in some questions and the products and the peak in others — it is always measured from the reactant energy level to the top of the curve, regardless of whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic.

Exothermic reaction profile

In an exothermic reaction, energy is given out to the surroundings. The products sit lower on the energy axis than the reactants — they have less energy because some has been released as heat (or light, or sound). ΔH is negative.

`` Energy │ │ ___ │ / \ │ / \ Activation energy (Eₐ): reactants → top of curve │ ___/ \ │ |REACTANTS| \ │ \___ │ |PRODUCTS| │ │_________________________ → Progress of reaction ``

Required labels on an exothermic profile:

  • y-axis: Energy
  • x-axis: Progress of reaction (or "Course of reaction" / "Reaction coordinate")
  • Horizontal line for reactants on the left, at the higher level
  • Horizontal line for products on the right, at the lower level
  • A smooth curve linking the two, with a peak in the middle
  • Activation energy (Eₐ) arrow: from the reactants line vertically up to the top of the peak
  • Overall energy change (ΔH) arrow: between the reactants line and the products line (downwards, because products are lower)

Common everyday exothermic examples whose profiles you might be asked to draw: combustion of methane, neutralisation of an acid by an alkali, displacement of copper by magnesium, and respiration.

Endothermic reaction profile

In an endothermic reaction, energy is taken in from the surroundings. The products sit higher on the energy axis than the reactants — they have more energy than the chemicals started with. ΔH is positive.

`` Energy │ ___ │ / \ │ / \ │ / \___ │ / |PRODUCTS| │ ___ / │ |REACTANTS| │ │_________________________ → Progress of reaction ``

Required labels are the same as for the exothermic profile, but with the products line drawn above the reactants line. The activation energy is still measured from the reactants line up to the top of the peak — not from the products line.

Common endothermic examples in Edexcel papers: thermal decomposition of metal carbonates (e.g. copper carbonate, calcium carbonate), the reaction of ammonium chloride with water, photosynthesis, and electrolysis.

How to draw a profile in the exam — step-by-step

If a question says "Draw and label the reaction profile…", work through these steps in order:

  1. Draw and label the two axes — Energy on the vertical, Progress of reaction on the horizontal. No need for numerical scales unless the question gives you values.
  2. Decide whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic from the information in the question (look for clues such as "temperature rose", "energy was released", "combustion", "neutralisation", or "decomposition", "temperature fell", "absorbed heat").
  3. Draw the reactants line on the left — higher up if exothermic, lower down if endothermic.
  4. Draw the products line on the right — at the opposite level.
  5. Connect the two with a smooth curve that rises to a peak in the middle before coming back down to the product level. Even an endothermic profile must have a peak that is higher than the products line.
  6. Label both lines with the names (or formulae) of the chemicals — if the question gives you specific species, use them.
  7. Add a vertical arrow showing the activation energy from the reactants line up to the top of the curve. Label it Eₐ or "activation energy".
  8. Add a vertical arrow showing the overall energy change, ΔH — between the reactants and products lines.

Mathematical Skills — interpreting energy values from a profile

Edexcel maths skill 4a (translating information between graphical and numeric form) applies here. If a profile gives numerical energy levels on the y-axis, you must be able to extract ΔH and Eₐ visually.

The two equations the profile represents are:

`` ΔH = energy of products − energy of reactants Eₐ = energy at top of curve − energy of reactants ``

Worked example. A reaction profile shows the reactants at an energy level of 500 kJ/mol, the products at 200 kJ/mol, and the peak of the curve at 650 kJ/mol.

Step 1 — Identify whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic. Products (200) are lower than reactants (500), so the reaction is exothermic.

Step 2 — Calculate ΔH.

ΔH = 200 − 500 = −300 kJ/mol (negative, confirming exothermic).

Step 3 — Calculate Eₐ.

Eₐ = 650 − 500 = +150 kJ/mol (always positive, always measured from the reactants line).

Common student trap: subtracting the products level from the peak (650 − 200 = 450 kJ/mol) and calling that the activation energy. That is wrong — that figure represents the energy released when the products form from the activated complex, not the input energy required to start the reaction.

Reaction profiles are a Paper 2 topic (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H — Foundation and Higher tier). They appear mid-paper as a visual task, almost always as a "draw and label" question worth 2–3 marks.

Typical mark allocation in a 2-mark question is one mark for the correct overall shape (reactants and products at the right levels, with a peak in between) and one mark for correctly labelling the activation energy from the reactants up to the peak. A 3-mark version typically adds a mark for labelling ΔH (the overall energy change).

Foundation-tier versions may give you a labelled blank profile and ask you to use words from a box to fill in the labels (reactants, products, activation energy). Higher-tier versions usually ask you to draw the whole profile from scratch given only the names of the reactants and products and a statement about whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic.

Key Takeaways
  1. A reaction profile plots energy (y-axis) against progress of reaction (x-axis), showing how the chemicals' energy changes from reactants to products.
  2. Every reaction profile has a "hump" in the middle — the peak represents the energy required to break bonds in the reactants before new bonds can form.
  3. Exothermic profiles have products lower than reactants; ΔH is negative and energy is released to the surroundings.
  4. Endothermic profiles have products higher than reactants; ΔH is positive and energy is taken in from the surroundings.
  5. The activation energy (Eₐ) is always measured from the reactants line up to the top of the curve, never from the products line — this is the most common error flagged by examiners.
  6. Even endothermic reactions have an activation-energy peak that sits above the products line — without the peak, the activation-energy arrow cannot be drawn.
  7. A reaction profile must be drawn as a curve with a peak, not a straight line — a straight line makes it impossible to mark in the activation energy.
  8. ΔH = energy of products − energy of reactants; if numerical values are given on the y-axis, you must be able to read them off and subtract.
  9. Required labels on every profile: axes, reactants, products, activation energy (Eₐ) arrow, and overall energy change (ΔH) arrow.
  10. Reaction profiles are a Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H) topic, typically worth 2–3 marks per question, with command words "draw and label" or "use words from the box".
  11. Common exothermic examples: combustion, neutralisation, displacement, respiration. Common endothermic examples: thermal decomposition of carbonates, dissolving ammonium chloride, photosynthesis.
  12. Read the question carefully for the direction of the reaction — if it tells you the temperature rose, the reaction is exothermic; if the temperature fell, it is endothermic.
7.2.4.2 Activation Energy F+H ▼

Activation Energy

Spec point 7.15: Explain the term activation energy. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

Activation energy is one of the most heavily examined ideas in the rates and energetics unit, because it is the bridge between collision theory (why most collisions don't react) and reaction profiles (where the energy "hump" comes from). It also has a very specific definition, and an even more specific way it must be measured off a reaction-profile diagram. Getting either of those slightly wrong is one of the most common ways candidates lose marks on this part of the spec.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Activation energy (Eₐ)The minimum amount of energy that colliding reactant particles must have for a reaction to occur
Reaction profileA diagram showing the energy of the reactants and products against the progress of the reaction
Transition stateThe point of highest energy on a reaction profile, where bonds are partially broken and partially formed
Successful collisionA collision between reactant particles that has at least the activation energy and is in the correct orientation, so that a reaction occurs
Exothermic reactionA reaction that transfers energy from the chemicals to the surroundings; products are lower in energy than reactants
Endothermic reactionA reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings; products are higher in energy than reactants

Why a minimum energy is needed: linking activation energy to collision theory

A reaction will only happen when reactant particles collide, but not every collision causes a reaction. Two conditions both have to be met:

  1. The particles must collide with at least the activation energy.
  2. The particles must collide in the correct orientation (so that the right atoms are positioned to make and break bonds).

Activation energy exists because before new bonds can be formed in the product, existing bonds in the reactants have to be broken or stretched. Bond breaking is endothermic — it requires an input of energy. Until the colliding particles have enough kinetic energy to overcome that initial bond-breaking step, they just bounce apart unchanged.

Once the reactants have absorbed enough energy to reach the transition state (the very top of the energy curve on a reaction profile), the partially-broken bonds can rearrange and the new bonds in the product form. As those new bonds form, energy is released. Whether the overall reaction is exothermic or endothermic depends on the balance between the energy taken in to break the reactant bonds and the energy given out when product bonds form — but both exothermic and endothermic reactions still have to climb over the activation-energy hump first.

This is why even strongly exothermic reactions (like the combustion of methane) don't happen the instant you mix the reactants: methane and oxygen will sit together at room temperature without reacting because the molecules don't yet have enough energy to break the C–H and O=O bonds. You have to supply an initial spark or flame to give a few molecules enough energy to clear Eₐ, and once those have reacted, the energy they release supplies the activation energy for the next set of molecules.

How activation energy is shown on a reaction profile

This is the bit that is most often examined and most often misread. On a reaction profile:

  • The x-axis shows the progress of the reaction (reactants on the left, products on the right).
  • The y-axis shows energy.
  • A curved line rises from the reactant energy level, peaks at the transition state, and falls to the product energy level.

Activation energy is measured as a vertical arrow from the reactant energy level up to the very top of the peak. It is not the distance from the x-axis (the bottom of the diagram) to the peak, and it is not the distance between reactants and products — that vertical distance is the overall energy change of the reaction (ΔH), which is a different quantity.

`` Energy ▲ │ ╭───╮ ← transition state (peak) │ ╱ ╲ │ ╱ ╲ ↕ Eₐ measured here: │ ─────╱ ╲ from reactant level │ reactants ╲ to peak │ ╲──── products (exothermic shown) │ └─────────────────────────────► progress of reaction ``

For an exothermic reaction the product line ends up below the reactant line, but the activation energy is still measured from the reactant level (the starting energy) up to the peak — not from the lower product level.

For an endothermic reaction the product line ends up above the reactant line. The activation energy is still measured from the reactant energy level up to the peak. Because the products are higher than the reactants, the peak is also above the products, but the peak is higher still than both — and Eₐ runs from the reactants line up to that highest point.

Comparison: activation energy vs overall energy change

These two quantities are read off the same diagram but they mean very different things, and mixing them up is a classic exam mistake.

Notice that the rule for drawing Eₐ is the same in both cases — reactant level up to peak. Only the shape of the rest of the profile changes.

Catalysts and activation energy

A catalyst speeds up a reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway that has a lower activation energy. On a reaction profile this is shown by a second curve drawn between the same reactant and product levels, but with a lower peak.

Crucially:

  • The reactants and products are at the same energy levels with or without a catalyst.
  • The overall energy change (ΔH) is therefore unchanged.
  • The activation energy is reduced, so a greater proportion of the reactant particles have at least the activation energy at any given temperature — meaning more successful collisions per second, and so a faster rate.

A common exam-style sketch will draw both profiles on the same axes (one labelled "uncatalysed", one labelled "catalysed") to make the lower Eₐ clear.

Worked example: reading Eₐ off a profile

Suppose a reaction profile shows:

  • Reactants at an energy level of 100 kJ
  • Peak of the curve at 240 kJ
  • Products at 60 kJ

Calculate (a) the activation energy and (b) the overall energy change.

``` Eₐ = (energy at peak) − (energy at reactants) = 240 − 100 = 140 kJ

ΔH = (energy of products) − (energy of reactants) = 60 − 100 = −40 kJ (negative → exothermic) ```

Notice that the activation energy is not 240 kJ (that's the height of the peak above the x-axis, not above the reactants) and not 180 kJ (that's the height of the peak above the products). It is 140 kJ, the vertical distance from the reactants to the peak.

Mathematical Skills

This SP draws on mathematical skill 4a (translating information between graphical and numeric form). The procedure for extracting Eₐ from a profile diagram is:

  1. Identify the reactant energy level on the y-axis (the height at which the curve starts).
  2. Identify the peak of the curve (the highest point on the diagram).
  3. Read both values off the y-axis, including units (usually kJ or kJ/mol).
  4. Subtract: Eₐ = (energy at peak) − (energy at reactants).
  5. Quote the answer with the correct units. Eₐ should always come out positive.

If you are asked to draw and label the activation energy on a profile, draw a single vertical arrow with a head at each end (a double-headed arrow), starting on the reactant line and ending exactly at the peak, and label it "Eₐ" or "activation energy".

Examiner Report Insights

The standout examiner finding for this spec point is about where students draw the activation-energy arrow on a reaction-profile diagram — not about the definition itself.

  • 1CH0/2H — November 2021 Examiner Report (on drawing an endothermic profile for the reaction between ammonium chloride solution and another solution): "Although the majority could draw the correct curve with the peak above the energy level of ammonium chloride solution, many candidates showed the activation energy as the difference between the ammonium chloride solution energy level and the peak of the curve. A few candidates drew a straight line from reactants to products which left them with an impossible task of scoring a mark for the activation energy."

There are two distinct errors flagged in that report:

  1. Mismeasuring Eₐ. Candidates drew the curve with the peak in the right place but then labelled Eₐ as the gap between the ammonium chloride solution level (i.e. the starting reactant level on this particular diagram) and the peak. In context, the examiner is pointing out that the activation energy should be measured from the correct starting energy level up to the peak — students who confused the position of the reactant line lost the Eₐ mark even though their curve was acceptable.
  2. Drawing a straight line instead of a curve. A small number of candidates joined reactants to products with a straight line, so there was no peak above the reactant level at all. With no peak there is no transition state and so no possibility of labelling an activation energy — the Eₐ mark cannot be awarded.
