1.6.1.1 Relative Formula Mass F+H ▼

Relative Formula Mass

Spec 1.43: Calculate relative formula mass given relative atomic masses.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 and Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Relative atomic mass (Ar)The average mass of an atom of an element, taking into account its naturally occurring isotopes and their abundances, compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom
Relative formula mass (Mr or RFM)The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms shown in the chemical formula of a substance
SubscriptThe small number written after an element symbol that tells you how many atoms of that element appear in the formula unit (e.g. the 2 in H₂O)
Bracket multiplierA subscript written outside a bracket — it multiplies every atom inside the brackets (e.g. the 2 in Mg(OH)₂ applies to both O and H)
Water of crystallisationWater molecules locked into the crystal structure of a hydrated salt, shown after a centred dot — e.g. the 5H₂O in CuSO₄·5H₂O

*Why Ar has no units*

Relative atomic mass is a ratio — it compares the average mass of an atom of an element to one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Because both sides of the ratio are masses, the units cancel. That is also why relative formula mass has no units — it is built entirely from Ar values, so it inherits the same unitless ratio nature. In exam answers, never write "g" or "kg" next to a relative formula mass. The number stands alone.

*Why RFM matters*

Almost every quantitative chemistry calculation on this course flows through the relative formula mass. The mole (moles = mass ÷ Mr), percentage yield, percentage composition, empirical formula, water of crystallisation, reacting masses, and concentration calculations all start with you working out the RFM of one or more substances. Getting the RFM wrong propagates the error through every subsequent step, so this 2-mark calculation is one of the highest-leverage skills in the whole specification.

The Arithmetic

The recipe is the same every time. For a compound, you:

  1. Read every element symbol in the formula
  2. For each one, take the relative atomic mass (Ar) of that element from the periodic table
  3. Multiply Ar by the subscript that tells you how many atoms of that element are in the formula
  4. Add all the products together

`` Mr = Σ (Ar of each element × number of atoms of that element) ``

For Edexcel GCSE you can assume the following standard Ar values (always read them from the periodic table in the exam, but these are worth memorising):

Worked Examples

The examples below build up in difficulty. Read each one through and try the next on paper before checking your answer.

*Example 1 — Water, H₂O*

The formula tells you there are 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.

`` Mr(H₂O) = (2 × Ar(H)) + (1 × Ar(O)) = (2 × 1) + (1 × 16) = 2 + 16 = 18 ``

The RFM of water is 18.

*Example 2 — Carbon dioxide, CO₂*

One carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.

`` Mr(CO₂) = (1 × Ar(C)) + (2 × Ar(O)) = (1 × 12) + (2 × 16) = 12 + 32 = 44 ``

The RFM of carbon dioxide is 44.

*Example 3 — Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃** *(the classic exam trap)

One calcium, one carbon, three oxygens.

`` Mr(CaCO₃) = (1 × Ar(Ca)) + (1 × Ar(C)) + (3 × Ar(O)) = (1 × 40) + (1 × 12) + (3 × 16) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 ``

The RFM of calcium carbonate is 100.

The 68-instead-of-100 trap. This is one of the most heavily flagged arithmetic errors in the entire specification — see the Examiner Report Insights below. A student who forgets the subscript on oxygen will write:

`` Wrong: Mr(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + 16 = 68 ✗ ``

That answer treats the formula as if there were only one oxygen, ignoring the subscript 3. Every CaCO₃ calculation on every paper, every year, is hunting for the candidates who do this. The correct working multiplies oxygen by 3 before adding, giving 100. Always multiply Ar × subscript before you add anything.

*Example 4 — Magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)₂** *(the bracket trap)

Brackets are a different kind of trap. The subscript outside the bracket multiplies every atom inside the bracket — not just the symbol it sits next to. So in Mg(OH)₂ you have:

  • 1 magnesium atom
  • 2 oxygen atoms (because the bracket 2 multiplies the 1 O inside)
  • 2 hydrogen atoms (because the bracket 2 multiplies the 1 H inside)

`` Mr(Mg(OH)₂) = (1 × Ar(Mg)) + (2 × Ar(O)) + (2 × Ar(H)) = (1 × 24) + (2 × 16) + (2 × 1) = 24 + 32 + 2 = 58 ``

The RFM of magnesium hydroxide is 58.

The bracket error. A student who treats the bracket as just a label and forgets to multiply by 2 will write:

`` Wrong: Mr(Mg(OH)₂) = 24 + 16 + 1 = 41 ✗ ``

A different student who multiplies only the oxygen by 2 but forgets the hydrogen will write:

`` Wrong: Mr(Mg(OH)₂) = 24 + 32 + 1 = 57 ✗ ``

A safe technique is to rewrite the formula without brackets before you start: Mg(OH)₂ is the same as MgO₂H₂. That removes the ambiguity completely. Then the calculation is just 24 + 32 + 2 = 58.

*Example 5 — Aluminium sulfate, Al₂(SO₄)₃** *(brackets with a polyatomic ion)

This is the hardest pattern you will meet at GCSE. Two layers of multiplication operate at once: the subscript inside the bracket (the 4 on oxygen) and the subscript outside the bracket (the 3 on the whole sulfate).

Break it down element by element:

  • Al: 2 atoms (the subscript on Al)
  • S: 3 atoms (the subscript outside the bracket multiplies the 1 S inside)
  • O: 4 × 3 = 12 atoms (the 4 inside the bracket × the 3 outside the bracket)

`` Mr(Al₂(SO₄)₃) = (2 × Ar(Al)) + (3 × Ar(S)) + (12 × Ar(O)) = (2 × 27) + (3 × 32) + (12 × 16) = 54 + 96 + 192 = 342 ``

The RFM of aluminium sulfate is 342.

The temptation here is to multiply only one of the inside atoms by the outside subscript. Always work out the total number of each atom first, then multiply by Ar. Writing it as Al₂S₃O₁₂ before the calculation is the safest route.

*Example 6 — Hydrated copper sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O** *(water of crystallisation)

The centred dot (·) does not mean "multiplied by". It means "together with" — the 5 water molecules are part of the crystal structure. You treat them as additional atoms in the formula:

  • Cu: 1 atom
  • S: 1 atom
  • O from sulfate: 4 atoms
  • From the 5H₂O: 10 H atoms and 5 O atoms

So in total: 1 Cu, 1 S, (4 + 5) = 9 O, 10 H.

`` Mr(CuSO₄·5H₂O) = (1 × Ar(Cu)) + (1 × Ar(S)) + (9 × Ar(O)) + (10 × Ar(H)) = (1 × 63.5) + (1 × 32) + (9 × 16) + (10 × 1) = 63.5 + 32 + 144 + 10 = 249.5 ``

The RFM of hydrated copper sulfate is 249.5.

When Ar values are not whole numbers (Cl = 35.5, Cu = 63.5), decimal RFMs are normal. Do not round to a whole number at the end — write the full value.

*Example 7 — Ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃** *(an element appearing twice)

Nitrogen appears in two different parts of the formula — once in the ammonium group (NH₄⁺) and once in the nitrate group (NO₃⁻). You add together every N atom from anywhere in the formula.

  • N: 2 atoms (1 in NH₄, 1 in NO₃)
  • H: 4 atoms (all from NH₄)
  • O: 3 atoms (all from NO₃)

`` Mr(NH₄NO₃) = (2 × Ar(N)) + (4 × Ar(H)) + (3 × Ar(O)) = (2 × 14) + (4 × 1) + (3 × 16) = 28 + 4 + 48 = 80 ``

The RFM of ammonium nitrate is 80.

Examiner Report Insights

  • 1CH0/1F — June 2019 Examiner Report and 1CH0/1H — June 2019 Examiner Report: Calculating relative formula mass is generally handled well by candidates across both tiers. However, examiners specifically noted a repeating arithmetic error that costs marks every sitting: candidates fail to multiply the relative atomic mass of an element if it has a subscript. The flagged example was the relative formula mass of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), where a common error was "failing to multiply the relative atomic mass of oxygen by three, namely arriving at a value of 68" instead of the correct value of 100.
  • Key lesson from the report: The 68-versus-100 error for CaCO₃ comes from a student who writes Ca (40) + C (12) + O (16) = 68 — adding the Ar values as if every element appears only once — instead of Ca (40) + C (12) + (16 × 3) = 100. The same arithmetic error pattern repeats with every multi-atom formula on both Foundation and Higher tier. Always multiply Ar × subscript before you add. Show the multiplication step explicitly in your working: it earns the working mark even if you slip on the arithmetic at the end, and it visibly demonstrates that you understood the subscript.

Mathematical Skills

Spec 1.43 maps onto two of the Edexcel mathematical skills:

  • 1a — Recognise and use expressions in decimal form (needed because Ar values for chlorine (35.5) and copper (63.5) are decimals, so the RFM is a decimal value that must be reported in full, not rounded)
  • 1c — Use ratios, fractions and percentages (RFM is itself a ratio, and it underpins percentage composition and percentage yield calculations later)

*Procedure (memorise this):*

  1. Count every atom of every element in the formula — multiply through any brackets first
  2. Multiply each atom count by the Ar of that element
  3. Add the products together
  4. Write the answer with no units

If you can write the formula without brackets first (e.g. Mg(OH)₂ → MgO₂H₂), do that — it removes most of the error opportunities. If the formula contains a centred dot (·) for water of crystallisation, include those water atoms in the same single sum.

How Relative Formula Mass Appears in Exams

RFM is one of the most commonly examined calculation skills on the whole 1CH0 specification. It appears on Paper 1 and Paper 2, and across both tiers.

*Typical appearance:*

  • An extremely common short calculation question worth 2 marks, found in the early-to-middle sections of Paper 1 or Paper 2
  • Typically: 1 mark for correct working (showing Ar × subscript for each element), 1 mark for the correct final answer
  • The command word is almost always "Calculate"
  • Example sittings flagged in examiner reports: 1CH0/1H, June 2019, Q4(d); 1CH0/1F, June 2023, Q4(b)

*Foundation tier (1CH0/1F, 1CH0/2F):*

  • A simple compound (often a binary compound like H₂O, CO₂, MgO, NaCl) where the arithmetic is short
  • Sometimes embedded in a moles-from-mass question as the first 2 marks
  • Sometimes presented with the Ar values given inline, sometimes expecting you to read them off the periodic table

*Higher tier (1CH0/1H, 1CH0/2H):*

  • A compound with brackets and/or water of crystallisation (e.g. Mg(OH)₂, Al₂(SO₄)₃, CuSO₄·5H₂O)
  • Often the gateway calculation in a larger reacting-mass, percentage-yield, or empirical-formula question
  • The Ar values are typically read from the periodic table in the exam paper

*If you are asked to** "Calculate the relative formula mass of …", **always show:*

  1. The formula broken down into atom counts (e.g. CaCO₃ → 1 Ca, 1 C, 3 O)
  2. The multiplication step (e.g. 1 × 40, 1 × 12, 3 × 16)
  3. The sum (e.g. 40 + 12 + 48)
  4. The final answer (100), with no units

That layout earns full credit even on the harder bracket compounds.

Key Takeaways
  1. Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the average mass of an atom of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom — it has no units.
  2. Relative formula mass (Mr or RFM) is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms shown in a chemical formula — it also has no units.
  3. For every element in the formula, multiply Ar × subscript first, then add all the products together.
  4. The classic exam error is forgetting the subscript: Mr(CaCO₃) = 100, not 68. June 2019 examiner reports across 1CH0/1F and 1CH0/1H specifically flagged candidates "failing to multiply the relative atomic mass of oxygen by three".
  5. A bracket subscript multiplies every atom inside the brackets — Mg(OH)₂ contains 1 Mg, 2 O and 2 H, giving Mr = 58 (not 41 or 57).
  6. For complex bracketed formulae like Al₂(SO₄)₃, multiply each inside subscript by the outside subscript before summing: 2 Al, 3 S, 12 O → Mr = 342.
  7. The centred dot (·) in a hydrated salt formula means "together with" — count the water atoms in the same single sum (e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O has 1 Cu, 1 S, 9 O, 10 H → Mr = 249.5).
  8. Decimal Ar values (Cl = 35.5, Cu = 63.5) give decimal RFMs — do not round to a whole number.
  9. Show each Ar × subscript multiplication explicitly in your working — this earns the working mark (typically 1 of 2 marks) even if the final addition slips.
  10. RFM has no units — never write "g", "kg", or "g/mol" next to your answer.
  11. Rewriting bracketed formulae without brackets (Mg(OH)₂ → MgO₂H₂; Al₂(SO₄)₃ → Al₂S₃O₁₂) before calculating eliminates most error opportunities.
  12. RFM is the foundation calculation for every later mass-based skill: moles, reacting masses, percentage yield, percentage composition, and empirical formulae all start by calculating Mr.
1.6.2.1 Empirical Formulae F+H ▼

Empirical Formulae

Spec 1.44: Calculate the formulae of simple compounds from reacting masses and understand that these are empirical formulae.

Spec 1.45: Deduce (a) the empirical formula of a compound from the formula of its molecule, and (b) the molecular formula of a compound from its empirical formula and its relative molecular mass.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Empirical formulaThe simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound
Molecular formulaThe actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of the compound
Reacting massThe mass of an element that combines with (or is released from) another element in a reaction — used to work out how many moles of each element are present
Relative atomic mass (Ar)The average mass of an atom of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom — read from the periodic table
Relative formula mass (Mr)The sum of the Ar values of all the atoms shown in the formula of a compound

*The difference between empirical and molecular formulae*

The two formulae describe the same compound from two different angles. The empirical formula tells you only the ratio of atoms — it is the simplest version. The molecular formula tells you the exact count of atoms in one molecule. So glucose has the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆ (every glucose molecule contains exactly 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms), but its empirical formula is CH₂O (the C : H : O ratio simplifies down to 1 : 2 : 1).

For some compounds the empirical and molecular formula are identical — water is H₂O at both levels because the ratio 2 : 1 cannot be simplified further, and the molecule itself only contains 2 H and 1 O. For ionic compounds (like sodium chloride, NaCl) there is no such thing as a molecular formula because they exist as giant lattices, not molecules — the chemical formula given for an ionic compound is always an empirical formula.

Calculating an Empirical Formula from Reacting Masses (Spec 1.44)

This is the standard exam method. You start with the masses (in grams) of each element that combined to make the compound, and you finish with the simplest whole-number ratio.

*The five-step method:*

`` Step 1: Write the mass of each element (in g) Step 2: Divide each mass by the element's Ar → this gives moles Step 3: Divide each answer by the smallest of those numbers Step 4: If you do not have whole numbers, multiply through by a small integer Step 5: Write the empirical formula using these whole numbers as subscripts ``

The single most important step is Step 2. You divide the mass by the Ar — not the Ar by the mass. Inverting this fraction is one of the most-penalised errors at Higher tier in this topic.

Worked Example 1 — Magnesium Oxide

A student burned 2.4 g of magnesium in oxygen and produced 4.0 g of magnesium oxide. Calculate the empirical formula of magnesium oxide.

*Step 1 — Find the mass of each element*

  • Mass of Mg = 2.4 g (given)
  • Mass of O = mass of MgO − mass of Mg = 4.0 − 2.4 = 1.6 g

*Step 2 — Divide each mass by its Ar*

  • Mg: 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.1 mol
  • O: 1.6 ÷ 16 = 0.1 mol

*Step 3 — Divide both by the smallest*

  • Mg: 0.1 ÷ 0.1 = 1
  • O: 0.1 ÷ 0.1 = 1

*Step 4 — Check for whole numbers*

Both already whole — no multiplication needed.

*Step 5 — Write the formula*

The empirical formula of magnesium oxide is MgO.

This is the standard answer. Step 2 is also where the Foundation-tier error described in the 2019 examiner report wrecks the calculation:

1CH0/1F — June 2019 Examiner Report: "candidates multiplied the masses and relative formula masses, namely stating 3.5 × 7 = 24.5, followed by 4 × 16 = 64 then adding them together and arriving at a final answer of 88.5." Multiplying mass by Ar does not give you moles — it gives you nothing useful. Always divide.

Worked Example 2 — Avoiding the Inverted-Fraction Error

A compound contains 3.5 g of lithium (Ar = 7) and 4.0 g of oxygen (Ar = 16). Find its empirical formula.

*Step 2 — done correctly:*

  • Li: 3.5 ÷ 7 = 0.5 mol
  • O: 4.0 ÷ 16 = 0.25 mol

*Step 3 — divide both by the smallest (0.25):*

  • Li: 0.5 ÷ 0.25 = 2
  • O: 0.25 ÷ 0.25 = 1

*Empirical formula: Li₂O*

Now look at the wrong version: a Higher-tier candidate who inverts Step 2 would do 7 ÷ 3.5 = 2 and 16 ÷ 4.0 = 4. They would then divide by the smallest (2) to get Li 1 and O 2, giving the wrong formula LiO₂. Same numbers — totally wrong answer.

1CH0/1H — June 2019 Examiner Report: a common error was to "invert the initial fractions" (dividing relative atomic mass by mass instead of mass by relative atomic mass). The examiners noted that if the inversion was carried through consistently, error carried forward (ECF) marks could be awarded — but the final formula would still be wrong. Worth remembering: ECF means a Step 2 slip does not always cost you every mark, but it always costs you the final formula mark because that depends on the right ratio.

Worked Example 3 — A Non-Integer Ratio

A compound is formed from 5.6 g of iron and 2.4 g of oxygen. Find its empirical formula.

*Step 2 — Divide each mass by its Ar:*

  • Fe: 5.6 ÷ 56 = 0.1 mol
  • O: 2.4 ÷ 16 = 0.15 mol

*Step 3 — Divide by the smallest (0.1):*

  • Fe: 0.1 ÷ 0.1 = 1
  • O: 0.15 ÷ 0.1 = 1.5

*Step 4 — Multiply through to get whole numbers*

1.5 is not whole, so multiply both numbers by 2:

  • Fe: 1 × 2 = 2
  • O: 1.5 × 2 = 3

*Step 5 — Write the formula*

Empirical formula: Fe₂O₃

The trick to recognise: 1.5 means "multiply by 2", 1.33 (or 1.333…) means "multiply by 3", 1.25 means "multiply by 4". Do not round 1.5 to 2 — that gives the wrong answer (FeO instead of Fe₂O₃).

Deducing an Empirical Formula from a Molecular Formula (Spec 1.45a)

This is the simpler half of Spec 1.45. You are given the molecular formula and asked to write the empirical formula. The method is just to find the highest common factor of the subscripts and divide each by it.

*Worked example — Glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ to empirical formula*

The subscripts are 6, 12, 6. The highest common factor is 6.

  • C: 6 ÷ 6 = 1
  • H: 12 ÷ 6 = 2
  • O: 6 ÷ 6 = 1

*Empirical formula: CH₂O*

*More examples:*

Deducing a Molecular Formula from an Empirical Formula and Mr (Spec 1.45b)

This is the more demanding half of Spec 1.45. You are given the empirical formula and the Mr of the compound, and you have to find the molecular formula.

*The three-step method:*

`` Step 1: Work out the Mr of the empirical formula unit Step 2: Divide the compound's Mr by the empirical Mr → this gives n Step 3: Multiply each subscript in the empirical formula by n → molecular formula ``

Worked Example 4 — Empirical CH₂, Mr = 84

A compound has the empirical formula CH₂ and a relative molecular mass of 84. Deduce its molecular formula.

*Step 1 — Mr of the empirical unit CH₂*

  • C: 1 × 12 = 12
  • H: 2 × 1 = 2
  • Total: 12 + 2 = 14

*Step 2 — How many empirical units fit into the molecule?*

n = 84 ÷ 14 = 6

*Step 3 — Multiply the subscripts in CH₂ by 6*

  • C: 1 × 6 = 6
  • H: 2 × 6 = 12

*Molecular formula: C₆H₁₂*

1CH0/2H — June 2019 Examiner Report: On exactly this type of question (empirical CH₂, Mr = 84), examiners praised the variety of successful mathematical approaches, but flagged that some candidates "calculated the correct ratio but then incorrectly converted this into the molecular formula" — for example, leaving the answer as a ratio rather than C₆H₁₂. This connects to a related Higher-tier error noted on the same paper series: candidates who "worked all the way through to the simplest ratio, but did not give a final formula". A ratio like "1 : 2 : 1" or the number "6" on its own is not a chemical formula. You must finish by writing the formula in proper notation — element symbols with subscript numbers.

Worked Example 5 — Empirical CH₂O, Mr = 180

A sugar has empirical formula CH₂O and Mr = 180. Find its molecular formula.

*Step 1 — Mr of CH₂O*

  • C: 1 × 12 = 12
  • H: 2 × 1 = 2
  • O: 1 × 16 = 16
  • Total = 30

*Step 2 — n = 180 ÷ 30 = 6*

*Step 3 — Multiply each subscript by 6*

  • C: 1 × 6 = 6
  • H: 2 × 6 = 12
  • O: 1 × 6 = 6

Molecular formula: C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose)

The Three Error Patterns to Avoid

The examiner reports for 1CH0 June 2019 identified three repeated mistakes on empirical-formula questions. Train yourself to spot all three.

Mathematical Skills

The maths skills assessed under this spec point are:

  • 1a — Recognise and use expressions in decimal form (mass ÷ Ar regularly produces decimals like 0.1, 0.15, 0.0875)
  • 1c — Use ratios, fractions and percentages (the whole method is about simplifying a ratio)
  • 2a — Use an appropriate number of significant figures (Spec 1.44 only)

When you write your answer, do not over-round inside the calculation. Keep at least 2–3 decimal places until the very end, then round the final ratio sensibly to whole numbers. Rounding 0.149 to 0.1 too early can shift the ratio.

How Empirical Formulae Appear in Exams

Empirical formula questions are a regular feature of mid-to-late paper calculations on both tiers. The maths is identical across Combined and Separate; the difference is that Higher candidates may face an inversion or non-integer ratio that Foundation candidates would not.

*Foundation tier (1CH0/1F and 1CH0/2F):*

  • Mass-data calculations to find a simple empirical formula (e.g. magnesium oxide, copper oxide)
  • Typically 3 marks: 1 for dividing mass by Ar, 1 for finding the ratio, 1 for the final formula
  • The June 2019 1F examiner report shows these are "very difficult" for Foundation candidates — the multiplication error is the main cause

*Higher tier (1CH0/1H and 1CH0/2H):*

  • Mass-data empirical formula calculations (often with non-integer ratios needing × 2 or × 3 to clear)
  • Deduce molecular formula from empirical formula + Mr (typically 3 marks)
  • Specific question references from 1CH0 June 2019: empirical-formula calculation on Q2(d) of 1CH0/1H and molecular-formula-from-empirical on Q7(d) of 1CH0/2H

Key Takeaways
  1. The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound; the molecular formula is the actual number of atoms in one molecule.
  2. The two formulae can be the same (H₂O, CO₂) or different (CH₂O vs C₆H₁₂O₆) — they are the same whenever the molecular subscripts cannot be simplified further.
  3. To find an empirical formula from masses: mass → divide by Ar → divide by smallest → multiply if needed → write the formula with subscripts.
  4. Always divide mass by Ar — never multiply. Multiplying mass × Ar is the most common Foundation error and gives a meaningless number.
  5. Mass goes on top of the fraction, Ar goes on the bottom. Inverting this is the most common Higher-tier slip and gives the wrong final formula.
  6. If the ratio you get is not whole numbers, multiply through — 1.5 means × 2, 1.33 means × 3, 1.25 means × 4.
  7. To find an empirical formula from a molecular formula: divide every subscript by their highest common factor (e.g. C₆H₁₂O₆ ÷ 6 → CH₂O).
  8. To find a molecular formula from an empirical formula and Mr: divide the compound's Mr by the empirical-unit Mr to get n, then multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by n.
  9. For CH₂ with Mr = 84: empirical Mr = 14, n = 84 ÷ 14 = 6, so molecular formula = C₆H₁₂.
  10. Always finish by writing a proper chemical formula with element symbols and subscripts — a ratio like "1 : 2 : 1" or just a number is not a final answer and will lose the final mark.
  11. Ionic compounds (like NaCl) do not have molecular formulae — their chemical formula is always an empirical formula because they exist as lattices, not molecules.
  12. Empirical formula questions are typically 3 marks on both tiers: 1 mark for the divide-by-Ar step, 1 mark for the simplified ratio, and 1 mark for the final formula in proper notation.
1.6.3.1 Determine an Empirical Formula F+H ▼

Determine an Empirical Formula

Spec 1.46: Describe an experiment to determine the empirical formula of a simple compound such as magnesium oxide.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

This is a practical-skills spec point. You are expected to be able to describe the experiment, identify the apparatus, state the measurements that need to be taken, and explain why each procedural step matters. The maths skills the spec attaches to it (1a — decimals; 1c — ratios, fractions, percentages; 2a — significant figures) are the same skills you use in the empirical formula calculation itself.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Empirical formulaThe simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound
CrucibleA small heat-resistant ceramic container used to hold a solid that is being strongly heated
Pipeclay triangleA small triangular support made of wire and ceramic tubes; sits on a tripod and holds the crucible above the flame
Constant massThe point at which repeated heating and weighing produces no further change in mass — used as evidence that the reaction is complete
Oxide layerThe thin, dull coating of magnesium oxide that forms on the surface of magnesium ribbon in air and must be cleaned off before the experiment

The Underlying Chemistry

When magnesium is heated strongly in air, it reacts with oxygen to form magnesium oxide:

2Mg (s) + O₂ (g) → 2MgO (s)

The product, MgO, is a white solid. Magnesium has a fixed combining ratio with oxygen — one Mg atom combines with one O atom — so the empirical formula of the product should come out as MgO. The experiment works by measuring the mass of magnesium you started with and the mass of magnesium oxide you ended up with. The difference between those two masses is the mass of oxygen that reacted, because mass is conserved during a chemical reaction (no atoms are created or destroyed).

Once you have a mass of magnesium and a mass of oxygen, you divide each by the relative atomic mass to get moles of each element, and then divide both values by the smaller one to get the simplest whole-number ratio. That ratio is the empirical formula.

Method — Step by Step

  1. Clean the magnesium ribbon using emery paper or fine sandpaper until the surface is shiny silver. This removes the existing magnesium oxide layer that forms naturally in air. If you skip this step, some of the mass you record as magnesium is actually already MgO, and your final ratio will be wrong.
  2. Coil the magnesium ribbon loosely so it fits inside the crucible without sticking out.
  3. Weigh the empty crucible with its lid on the balance and record the mass.
  4. Place the cleaned magnesium ribbon inside the crucible, put the lid on, and reweigh. Record the new mass. The mass of magnesium is the difference between this reading and the empty-crucible reading.
  5. Set up the tripod and pipeclay triangle over a Bunsen burner on a heat-resistant mat. Place the crucible (lid on) onto the pipeclay triangle.
  6. Heat the crucible strongly with a roaring blue flame.
  7. Lift the lid briefly using tongs every 20–30 seconds, then quickly replace it. This allows fresh oxygen to enter the crucible to react with the magnesium, but the lid is kept on most of the time so that the white magnesium oxide smoke does not escape.
  8. Continue heating until there is no further sign of reaction (no more bright flame from the magnesium burning). The contents should now be a white/grey ash.
  9. Allow the crucible to cool on the heat-resistant mat. Do not put a hot crucible directly onto the balance — it can damage the balance, and the hot air rising from it gives an inaccurate reading.
  10. Reweigh the crucible, lid and contents. Record the mass.
  11. Reheat and reweigh the crucible. If the mass has changed, repeat. Continue until two consecutive weighings give the same reading — this is heating to constant mass, and it is the evidence that all of the magnesium has reacted.

Critical Procedural Details — and Why

Why clean the magnesium ribbon? Magnesium reacts slowly with oxygen in the air even at room temperature, so a piece of magnesium ribbon already has a thin layer of MgO on its surface. If you weigh the ribbon without cleaning it, the mass you record as "magnesium" includes some oxygen, and your calculated ratio will be wrong (you will end up with apparently more oxygen than there really was during the experiment).

Why use a crucible with a lid? The product, MgO, forms as a fine white powder and a white smoke. Without a lid, some of this smoke escapes from the crucible, so the final mass is too low and the calculated mass of oxygen is too small. A lid traps the smoke inside the crucible so all of the product is included in the final weighing.

Why lift the lid briefly? A sealed crucible would quickly run out of oxygen — the magnesium would stop reacting partway through and you would not have a complete conversion to MgO. Lifting the lid every 20–30 seconds admits fresh air without giving the smoke enough time to escape in significant quantities. It is a compromise between two competing needs: oxygen has to get in, but MgO has to stay in.

Why heat to constant mass? This is the most-tested explain step. The reasoning is: if the mass is still increasing on reheating, then some of the magnesium has not yet reacted with oxygen (more oxygen still needs to combine with it). Once the mass no longer changes between weighings, you have evidence that the reaction is complete. Without this step you cannot be sure all the magnesium has been converted to MgO, and the calculated formula could be wrong.

Why allow it to cool before weighing? Two reasons. First, hot equipment damages an electronic balance. Second, hot air rising from a hot crucible creates convection currents over the balance pan, giving a falsely low reading. Both effects ruin the accuracy of the experiment.

1.6.4.1 Conservation of Mass F+H ▼

Conservation of Mass

Spec 1.47: Explain the law of conservation of mass applied to (a) a closed system including a precipitation reaction in a closed flask, and (b) a non-enclosed system including a reaction in an open flask that takes in or gives out a gas.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Law of conservation of massThe total mass of the reactants is equal to the total mass of the products in any chemical reaction — mass is neither created nor destroyed
Closed systemA reaction container that does not allow matter (including gases) to enter or leave; the contents are sealed
Non-enclosed (open) systemA reaction container that allows gases to enter from the surroundings or escape to the surroundings — for example, an open flask, crucible or beaker
Precipitation reactionA reaction between two soluble salts in solution that produces an insoluble solid (the precipitate) plus another soluble product
Thermal decompositionA reaction in which a single compound is broken down into simpler substances by heat
ReactantsThe starting substances written on the left-hand side of the equation
ProductsThe substances formed in the reaction, written on the right-hand side of the equation

The Law of Conservation of Mass

The law of conservation of mass states that in every chemical reaction the total mass of the reactants is exactly equal to the total mass of the products. No mass is gained, no mass is lost — only rearranged.

*Why the law works at the particle level*

A chemical reaction is a rearrangement of atoms. The atoms in the reactants are pulled apart from one set of chemical bonds and rejoined into a new set of chemical bonds in the products. Crucially:

  • No new atoms are created
  • No atoms are destroyed
  • The same atoms that started the reaction are still there at the end — just bonded differently

Because every atom has a fixed mass, and because the count of each type of atom is unchanged, the total mass of "stuff" before the reaction must equal the total mass of "stuff" after. This is why a correctly balanced chemical equation always has the same number of atoms of each element on the left and right sides — the equation is the symbolic statement of conservation of mass.

For example, in the reaction:

*2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O*

  • Left-hand side: 4 H atoms + 2 O atoms
  • Right-hand side: 4 H atoms + 2 O atoms

The atoms have been rearranged from H–H and O=O bonds into O–H bonds, but every single atom is still present in the products. The mass is the same.

a) Closed Systems — Precipitation in a Sealed Flask

A closed system is the easiest case to picture. Nothing can enter or leave the reaction container, so every atom that started inside the flask must still be inside at the end. Because no matter has crossed the boundary of the system, the reading on the balance does not change during the reaction.

*Worked example — lead nitrate + potassium iodide*

When colourless lead(II) nitrate solution is mixed with colourless potassium iodide solution in a sealed conical flask, a bright yellow precipitate of lead(II) iodide forms:

*Pb(NO₃)₂ (aq) + 2 KI (aq) → PbI₂ (s) + 2 KNO₃ (aq)*

If you place the sealed flask on a top-pan balance, mix the two solutions, and watch the yellow PbI₂ precipitate appear:

  • The colour changes from colourless to cloudy yellow
  • A new solid forms
  • The balance reading does not change

That last observation is the experimental statement of conservation of mass. The atoms that were dissolved in the two starting solutions have been rearranged: lead and iodide ions have come together to form solid PbI₂, while potassium and nitrate ions stay in solution as KNO₃. No atoms have entered the flask. No atoms have left. The total mass is the same before and after.

A typical exam phrasing of the explanation:

"The mass does not change because the flask is sealed, so no substances can enter or leave. The atoms in the reactants are simply rearranged into the products — no atoms are created or destroyed."

b) Non-Enclosed Systems — When the Balance Reading DOES Change

In an open flask, the reaction container is not sealed. If the reaction produces a gas, that gas can escape into the surroundings, taking some mass with it. If the reaction uses a gas from the surroundings, that gas can enter the flask, adding mass to its contents.

The law of conservation of mass is not broken in either case. The total mass of all reactants (including any gas drawn in from the air) still equals the total mass of all products (including any gas released to the surroundings). What changes is the mass of the flask contents — because some of the matter has crossed the boundary of the flask.

Open System Example 1 — Mass Decreases (Gas Released)

*Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate*

*CaCO₃ (s) → CaO (s) + CO₂ (g)*

If you heat a sample of calcium carbonate in an open crucible on a balance:

  • Calcium carbonate breaks down into solid calcium oxide and carbon dioxide gas
  • The carbon dioxide escapes into the air
  • The balance reading decreases

The mass of solid left in the crucible is less than the mass of CaCO₃ you started with — but only because some of the original mass has left the crucible as gas. If you could capture every CO₂ molecule that escaped and weigh it together with the leftover CaO, the total mass would equal the mass of the original CaCO₃ exactly.

This is the standard "mass goes down" exam scenario. The explanation must say two things:

  1. The reaction produces a gas (CO₂)
  2. The gas escapes from the open container, so the mass of the contents decreases

Open System Example 2 — Mass Increases (Gas Taken In)

*Heating magnesium in air*

*2 Mg (s) + O₂ (g) → 2 MgO (s)*

If you heat a coil of magnesium ribbon in an open crucible on a balance:

  • The magnesium reacts with oxygen from the air
  • A white solid (magnesium oxide) is formed
  • The balance reading increases

The mass of solid in the crucible is now greater than the mass of magnesium you started with — but the extra mass has come from oxygen drawn out of the air. Every atom is still accounted for: if you weighed the magnesium plus all the oxygen that reacted, the total would equal the mass of the MgO produced.

This is the "mass goes up" scenario. The explanation must say two things:

  1. The reaction uses a gas from the air (O₂)
  2. The gas adds to the mass of the contents of the open container

Worked Numerical Example — Mass Balance

A student heats 5.0 g of copper carbonate in an open crucible until it has fully decomposed. The mass of the solid left in the crucible falls to 3.2 g.

*CuCO₃ (s) → CuO (s) + CO₂ (g)*

Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide gas released.

*Procedure:*

  1. By conservation of mass: mass of CuCO₃ = mass of CuO + mass of CO₂
  2. Rearrange: mass of CO₂ = mass of CuCO₃ − mass of CuO
  3. Substitute: mass of CO₂ = 5.0 g − 3.2 g
  4. Answer: mass of CO₂ = 1.8 g

The 1.8 g of CO₂ has not vanished — it has left the crucible as a gas. If the same reaction were carried out in a sealed flask, the balance would still read 5.0 g at the end (3.2 g of CuO solid + 1.8 g of CO₂ gas trapped inside).

Mathematical Skills

The key maths skill is 1a — recognise and use expressions in decimal form: subtract one decimal mass from another to find the mass of gas released or absorbed.

`` mass of gas released = starting mass of solid − final mass of solid mass of gas absorbed = final mass of solid − starting mass of solid ``

Show every step, include the units (always grams in this context), and give the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the data in the question.

The Critical Wording — "Released" vs "Evaporated"

The single most-penalised error in this topic is describing the loss of gas as evaporation.

WrongRight
"The mass decreases because the gas evaporates""The mass decreases because a gas (CO₂) is released and escapes from the open container"
"Water evaporates from the calcium carbonate""Carbon dioxide gas is produced by the reaction and escapes"
"The carbonate turns into a gas""The carbonate decomposes and gives off carbon dioxide gas"

Evaporation is a physical change — a liquid turning into a vapour without any change in chemical composition (water → water vapour). What happens in thermal decomposition is a chemical reaction: a brand new substance (carbon dioxide) is produced from atoms that were previously locked into a solid (calcium carbonate). The gas is made by the reaction; it is not evaporated from a liquid.

If you use the word "evaporation" in an answer about heating a carbonate or burning magnesium, you will lose the explanation mark even if everything else in your answer is correct.

"Describe" vs "Explain" — the 1-mark vs 2-mark gap

In open-system questions on this spec point, the command word is almost always explain, and the question carries 2 marks. To get both marks you must:

  1. Describe what happens to the mass (e.g. "the mass decreases" or "the mass increases") — this earns 1 mark
  2. Explain WHY by referring to the gas (e.g. "because CO₂ gas is released and escapes the open flask" or "because oxygen from the air reacts and is added to the mass of the solid") — this earns the second mark

A student who only says "the mass decreases" has described the change but not explained it. Examiners are explicit that this scores 1 out of 2 — you must always connect the change in mass to the gas leaving or entering the open container.

1.6.5.1 Reacting Masses F+H ▼

Reacting Masses

Spec 1.48: Calculate masses of reactants and products from balanced equations, given the mass of one substance.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Relative formula mass (Mr or RFM)The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms shown in the formula of a compound
Balanced equationAn equation in which the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides — the coefficients (big numbers) give the reacting ratio
Stoichiometric ratioThe whole-number ratio in which substances react and are produced, taken directly from the coefficients in the balanced equation
Reacting massThe mass of a reactant or product calculated from a balanced equation when the mass of one other substance is known
Mole (Higher tier)A measured amount of substance equal to mass ÷ Mr; substances react in the ratio shown by the balanced equation when measured in moles

Why the balanced equation matters

A balanced equation does more than make the atoms add up on each side — it tells you the ratio in which substances react and are produced. That ratio is given by the big numbers in front of each formula (the stoichiometric coefficients). If the equation says

2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO

then 2 units of magnesium react with 1 unit of oxygen to make 2 units of magnesium oxide. Those "units" can be molecules, formula units, moles, or — once you scale by relative formula mass — masses in grams or tonnes. The whole calculation in this spec point is just one question: how do I turn that small whole-number ratio into a ratio of real-world masses?

You cannot do a reacting-mass calculation from an unbalanced equation. If the equation is not balanced, the ratio is wrong, and every mass that follows will be wrong. Before you start, check that the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides — see 1.6.9.1 Writing & Balancing Equations.

Two equivalent methods — pick the one that suits you

There are two equally valid ways to answer a reacting-mass question. They give exactly the same final answer. Foundation candidates usually meet the ratio method first; Higher candidates are strongly encouraged to use the moles method (see examiner reports below).

*Method A — Ratio method (mass ratio scaled from RFM ratio)*

  1. Write out the balanced equation.
  2. Work out the RFM of the substance you are given and the substance you are asked about.
  3. Multiply each RFM by its coefficient in the balanced equation to get the reacting masses in the ratio of the equation.
  4. Scale the ratio so that the "given" reacting mass matches the actual mass in the question.
  5. Apply the same scale factor to the "asked about" reacting mass — that is your answer.

*Method B — Moles method (mass → moles → moles → mass)*

  1. Write out the balanced equation.
  2. Convert the given mass into moles using moles = mass ÷ Mr.
  3. Use the balanced equation ratio to convert the moles of the given substance into moles of the substance you are asked about.
  4. Convert those moles back into mass using mass = moles × Mr.

The moles method has one big advantage: it works for any ratio, no matter how awkward, without you having to do mental arithmetic on the scale factor. That is why it is recommended at Higher tier.

Worked Example 1 — 1:1 ratio (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂)

Question: Calcium carbonate decomposes on heating: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Calculate the mass of calcium oxide produced when 50 g of calcium carbonate fully decomposes. (Ar: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)

*Method A — ratio method*

Step 1. The equation is already balanced. The ratio of CaCO₃ : CaO is 1 : 1.

Step 2. RFM (CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. RFM (CaO) = 40 + 16 = 56.

Step 3. Reacting masses from the equation: 100 g CaCO₃ → 56 g CaO.

Step 4. We have 50 g of CaCO₃ — that is half of 100 g, so scale factor = 0.5.

Step 5. Mass of CaO = 0.5 × 56 = 28 g.

*Method B — moles method*

Step 1. Moles of CaCO₃ = 50 ÷ 100 = 0.5 mol.

Step 2. Ratio CaCO₃ : CaO = 1 : 1, so moles of CaO = 0.5 mol.

Step 3. Mass of CaO = 0.5 × 56 = 28 g.

Both methods give 28 g. Notice how the moles method has fewer steps once you are comfortable with it — and it gives the answer with no awkward fractional scale factor.

Worked Example 2 — 2:1 ratio (2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO)

Question: Magnesium burns in oxygen: 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO. Calculate the mass of magnesium oxide produced when 6.0 g of magnesium is completely burned. (Ar: Mg = 24, O = 16)

*Method A — ratio method*

Step 1. From the equation, the ratio of Mg : MgO is 2 : 2, which simplifies to 1 : 1.

Step 2. RFM (Mg) = 24. RFM (MgO) = 24 + 16 = 40.

Step 3. Reacting masses from the equation: (2 × 24) = 48 g Mg → (2 × 40) = 80 g MgO.

Step 4. We have 6.0 g of Mg, and the reacting mass of Mg is 48 g, so scale factor = 6.0 ÷ 48 = 0.125.

Step 5. Mass of MgO = 0.125 × 80 = 10 g.

*Method B — moles method*

Step 1. Moles of Mg = 6.0 ÷ 24 = 0.25 mol.

Step 2. Ratio Mg : MgO is 2 : 2 = 1 : 1, so moles of MgO = 0.25 mol.

Step 3. Mass of MgO = 0.25 × 40 = 10 g.

The 2 : 2 ratio simplifies to 1 : 1, but you must still show how you got there. Writing "2 Mg makes 2 MgO so the ratio is 1 : 1" is what earns the ratio mark. Some students lose marks by forgetting to consider the coefficients at all and just using the RFM ratio of 24 : 40 — which gives the same answer here only because the coefficients happen to be equal.

Worked Example 3 — 1:2 ratio (mass-to-mass through two species)

Question: Sodium reacts with water: 2 Na + 2 H₂O → 2 NaOH + H₂. Calculate the mass of water needed to react completely with 4.6 g of sodium. (Ar: Na = 23, H = 1, O = 16)

*Method A — ratio method*

Step 1. From the equation, the ratio of Na : H₂O is 2 : 2 = 1 : 1.

Step 2. RFM (Na) = 23. RFM (H₂O) = (2 × 1) + 16 = 18.

Step 3. Reacting masses: (2 × 23) = 46 g Na → (2 × 18) = 36 g H₂O.

Step 4. We have 4.6 g of Na. Scale factor = 4.6 ÷ 46 = 0.1.

Step 5. Mass of H₂O = 0.1 × 36 = 3.6 g.

*Method B — moles method*

Step 1. Moles of Na = 4.6 ÷ 23 = 0.2 mol.

Step 2. Ratio Na : H₂O = 1 : 1, so moles of H₂O = 0.2 mol.

Step 3. Mass of H₂O = 0.2 × 18 = 3.6 g.

This question demonstrates that the same approach works when the "asked about" species is a reactant rather than a product. The method does not change — the balanced equation gives you the ratio you need either way.

Worked Example 4 — A more challenging 2:3 ratio (Higher tier focus)

Question: Aluminium oxide is reduced by carbon: 2 Al₂O₃ + 3 C → 4 Al + 3 CO₂. Calculate the mass of aluminium produced when 51 g of aluminium oxide reacts completely. (Ar: Al = 27, O = 16, C = 12)

*Method B — moles method (recommended at Higher)*

Step 1. RFM (Al₂O₃) = (2 × 27) + (3 × 16) = 102.

Step 2. Moles of Al₂O₃ = 51 ÷ 102 = 0.5 mol.

Step 3. From the equation, 2 Al₂O₃ makes 4 Al, so the ratio Al₂O₃ : Al = 2 : 4 = 1 : 2. Moles of Al = 0.5 × 2 = 1.0 mol.

Step 4. Mass of Al = 1.0 × 27 = 27 g.

The ratio method works here too, but with awkward numbers (204 : 108) it is easy to slip up. This is why Higher candidates are encouraged to use the moles method — it scales cleanly through any ratio.

Set out your working — examiners insist on it

Look at how each worked example is laid out: equation, RFMs, ratio, scale, final answer. That structure is not optional decoration. Examiners explicitly mark on whether your working can be followed, and many candidates lose marks because their working is too disorganised for the examiner to award method marks even when the final answer is right (or partially right).

A useful rule of thumb: a Foundation-tier reacting-mass calculation is worth 3 marks (1 for the two RFMs, 1 for the ratio/scaling, 1 for the final mass). A Higher-tier version is usually 3–4 marks (sometimes a fourth mark is awarded for showing answers to an appropriate number of significant figures or with correct units). If your working is illegible, you can only earn the answer mark — and if your answer is wrong, you score zero.

The "RFM of product alone" trap — the most common Foundation error

This is the single biggest mark-loser at Foundation. The student is asked to calculate the mass of CaO produced from some mass of CaCO₃. They write RFM of CaO = 56 and stop, sometimes writing 56 g as their final answer.

That is not enough. The RFM of the product on its own is not the answer. What you have actually done is calculate the mass of one mole of the product — but the question is asking for a mass that depends on how much reactant you started with. The full method requires you to:

  1. Calculate the RFM of both the given substance and the substance you are asked about.
  2. Build the mass ratio from the balanced equation.
  3. Scale that ratio to the actual mass given in the question.

Skipping any of these three steps means missing the point of the question entirely. If you find yourself with a final answer that does not use the mass given in the question, you have made this mistake.

1.6.5.2 Percentage Composition F+H ▼

Percentage Composition

Spec: Calculate the percentage by mass of an element in a compound. Grouped under Topic 1.6 Calculations Involving Masses on the official Edexcel specification — there is no separate numbered spec line, but it is taught and examined alongside relative formula mass and reacting masses.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 and Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Percentage by massThe mass of one element in a compound expressed as a percentage of the total mass of the compound
Relative atomic mass (Ar)The average mass of one atom of an element compared with 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom
Relative formula mass (RFM)The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms shown in the formula of a compound
SubscriptThe small number after an element symbol in a formula — it tells you how many of that atom are in the formula unit (e.g. the 4 in CH₄ means 4 hydrogen atoms)
Significant figuresThe number of meaningful digits in a numerical answer; the question always tells you how many to round to

The Core Formula

The percentage by mass of an element in a compound is found using:

`` % by mass of element = (total mass of element in compound ÷ RFM of compound) × 100 ``

There are three things to extract from the formula of the compound before you start:

  1. The Ar of each element involved (from the periodic table you are given in the exam)
  2. The subscript on the element you are asked about — how many atoms of it are in one formula unit
  3. The total RFM of the whole compound

The "total mass of element" in the numerator is the Ar of that element multiplied by its subscript. This is the step that catches out most candidates. If you are asked for the percentage of oxygen in CaCO₃, the numerator is not 16 — it is 3 × 16 = 48, because there are three oxygen atoms in the formula. Forgetting to multiply by the subscript will give you a percentage that is far too low.

Why You Multiply Ar by the Subscript

The relative formula mass is a count of every single atom in the formula. When you add up the RFM of CaCO₃, you are doing 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. The 48 that comes from the three oxygens is part of the total mass, so when you ask "what fraction of that total comes from oxygen?", you have to use the same 48 — not the Ar of one O atom on its own.

Think of it like a fruit bowl. If the bowl contains 1 apple (40 g), 1 banana (12 g), and 3 oranges (16 g each), the total mass of fruit is 100 g. The fraction of the bowl that is orange is 48/100, not 16/100 — because there are three oranges, not one. The same principle applies to elements in a compound. The subscript is the count, and Ar is the mass per atom, so you multiply them together to get the total mass contributed by that element.

Worked Example 1 — Percentage of Carbon in Methane (CH₄)

*Find the percentage by mass of carbon in methane.*

Ar values: C = 12, H = 1.

Step 1 — Find the RFM of CH₄:

`` RFM(CH₄) = 12 + (4 × 1) = 16 ``

Step 2 — Find the total mass of carbon in the formula:

`` Mass of C = 1 × 12 = 12 ``

Step 3 — Apply the formula:

`` % C = (12 ÷ 16) × 100 = 75% ``

So methane is 75% carbon by mass. This is a sensible answer: there is far more mass tied up in the single carbon atom than in the four hydrogens (which together contribute only 4 out of 16).

Worked Example 2 — Percentage of Oxygen in Calcium Carbonate (CaCO₃)

*Find the percentage by mass of oxygen in CaCO₃.*

Ar values: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16.

Step 1 — Find the RFM of CaCO₃:

`` RFM(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 ``

Step 2 — Find the total mass of oxygen in the formula. There are three O atoms, so:

`` Mass of O = 3 × 16 = 48 ``

Step 3 — Apply the formula:

`` % O = (48 ÷ 100) × 100 = 48% ``

So 48% of the mass of calcium carbonate is oxygen. Notice how the answer would have been wrong (16%) if you had forgotten to multiply Ar by the subscript. The subscript on oxygen is doing all the work in this question.

Worked Example 3 — Mass of an Element from a Percentage (Butane)

This is the type of question examined in 1CH0/2F — June 2019 Examiner Report and 1CH0/2H — June 2019 Examiner Report. The question asked candidates to calculate the mass of carbon in 100 g of butane.

*Find the mass of carbon in 100 g of butane (C₄H₁₀).*

Ar values: C = 12, H = 1.

Step 1 — Find the RFM of C₄H₁₀:

`` RFM(C₄H₁₀) = (4 × 12) + (10 × 1) = 48 + 10 = 58 ``

Step 2 — Find the total mass of carbon in one formula unit:

`` Mass of C = 4 × 12 = 48 ``

Step 3 — Find the percentage of carbon in butane:

`` % C = (48 ÷ 58) × 100 = 82.758...% ``

Step 4 — Find the mass of carbon in 100 g of butane. Because the question asks about exactly 100 g, the percentage is the mass in grams (this is a convenient shortcut whenever the sample size happens to be 100 g):

`` Mass of C = 82.758...% × 100 g ÷ 100 = 82.758... g ``

Step 5 — Round to three significant figures (because the question asked for it):

`` Mass of C = 82.8 g (3 s.f.) ``

The two examiner-report observations from the June 2019 sitting are exactly the things this worked example is designed to fix. Foundation candidates were unsure "what was required in this calculation and where to start" (1CH0/2F — June 2019 Examiner Report) — the answer is that the method is procedural: write down RFM, write down mass of the named element, divide and multiply by 100, and only then scale to the given mass. Both Foundation and Higher candidates lost the final mark by failing to round to "three significant figures" (1CH0/2F — June 2019 Examiner Report; 1CH0/2H — June 2019 Examiner Report). The working can be perfect and you can still drop a mark if the final answer reads "82.7586%" or even "83%" when the question specifies three significant figures.

Worked Example 4 — Percentage with Brackets (Ammonium Sulfate)

Fertiliser questions are a frequent Edexcel context for percentage composition. Ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄, is a nitrogen-rich fertiliser, and questions sometimes ask "what is the percentage of nitrogen in this fertiliser?" so a farmer can compare it against other compounds.

*Find the percentage by mass of nitrogen in (NH₄)₂SO₄.*

Ar values: N = 14, H = 1, S = 32, O = 16.

Step 1 — Count the atoms carefully. The bracketed (NH₄) is multiplied by the 2 outside the bracket, so the formula contains:

  • 2 × N = 2 nitrogen atoms
  • 2 × 4 = 8 hydrogen atoms
  • 1 × S = 1 sulfur atom
  • 4 × O = 4 oxygen atoms

Step 2 — Find the RFM:

`` RFM((NH₄)₂SO₄) = (2 × 14) + (8 × 1) + 32 + (4 × 16) = 28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = 132 ``

Step 3 — Find the total mass of nitrogen:

`` Mass of N = 2 × 14 = 28 ``

Step 4 — Apply the formula:

`` % N = (28 ÷ 132) × 100 = 21.212...% ``

Step 5 — Round to three significant figures:

`` % N = 21.2% (3 s.f.) ``

The big trap with brackets is the same trap as the subscript trap, only worse — the 2 outside the bracket multiplies everything inside it. If you read the formula as containing only one N and four H, you will get an RFM of 118 instead of 132 and a percentage of about 11.9% — both wrong. Always expand brackets carefully before adding up the RFM.

Worked Example 5 — Hydrated Salt (Percentage of Water in CuSO₄·5H₂O)

A hydrated salt is a crystal that has water molecules locked into its structure as "water of crystallisation". The dot in the formula does not mean multiply — it is just a separator. So CuSO₄·5H₂O contains one copper sulfate unit and five separate water molecules per formula unit. Questions on hydrated salts often ask for the percentage of water in the crystal, because that is what gets driven off when you heat the salt to dehydrate it.

*Find the percentage by mass of water in CuSO₄·5H₂O.*

Ar values: Cu = 63.5, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1.

Step 1 — Find the RFM of the whole hydrated salt:

`` RFM(CuSO₄) = 63.5 + 32 + (4 × 16) = 63.5 + 32 + 64 = 159.5 RFM(5H₂O) = 5 × [(2 × 1) + 16] = 5 × 18 = 90 RFM(total) = 159.5 + 90 = 249.5 ``

Step 2 — The "element" here is actually water, and its total mass in the formula is 90.

Step 3 — Apply the formula:

`` % H₂O = (90 ÷ 249.5) × 100 = 36.072...% ``

Step 4 — Round to three significant figures:

`` % H₂O = 36.1% (3 s.f.) ``

This is the kind of calculation that underlies the empirical-formula-of-a-hydrated-salt practical, where you heat blue copper sulfate to drive off the water and weigh the white anhydrous solid that remains. The mass loss should match the percentage of water calculated here.

The Significant Figures Trap

Both June 2019 examiner reports identified rounding as a major mark-losing issue: "a major reason candidates across both tiers lost the final mark was failing to read the question carefully and not rounding their answer to the requested three significant figures" (1CH0/2F — June 2019 Examiner Report; 1CH0/2H — June 2019 Examiner Report).

What this means in practice:

  1. Before you start, find the sig-fig instruction. It is almost always in the line of the question that asks for the answer, sometimes in bold or in brackets. If it says "three significant figures", that is what your final answer must show — not four, not two.
  2. Do not round in the middle of the working. If you round 82.758...% to 83% halfway through and then multiply, the final answer drifts. Keep the long decimal in your calculator and only round at the very end.
  3. Count significant figures from the first non-zero digit. For 82.758%, three significant figures is 82.8%. For 0.04825 g, three significant figures is 0.0482 g — the leading zeros don't count.
  4. If no sig-fig instruction is given, match the data. If all the Ar values and masses you were given are to three significant figures, the answer should be too.

You will see the demand on sig figs reflected in the Mathematical Skills grid for this spec point: 2a (Use an appropriate number of significant figures) is explicitly required, alongside 1a (Recognise and use expressions in decimal form) and 1c (Use ratios, fractions and percentages).

Real-World Framing — Fertilisers and Economic Choice

Percentage composition is one of the spec points where the Edexcel question writers most commonly use a real-world context. The most frequent framing is fertiliser choice: nitrogen-based fertilisers are sold by the percentage of nitrogen they contain by mass, because more nitrogen per kg means more efficient crop growth per pound spent. A typical comparison is between ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃, ~35% N) and ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄, ~21% N) — you might be asked to calculate one or both percentages and then state which gives the better value for money for a given price per tonne. The percentage by mass of nitrogen is the chemistry; the comparison is the application.

Other common framings include:

  • Iron in iron ores. Different ores (haematite Fe₂O₃, magnetite Fe₃O₄) have different percentage compositions of iron, and miners will preferentially extract the ore with the highest iron content.
  • Water of crystallisation in hydrated salts. As in worked example 5, the percentage of water in a hydrate can be linked to a practical where the salt is dehydrated by heating.
  • Calcium in calcium-rich foods or supplements. Less commonly examined, but uses the same calculation.

When you see the real-world wrapper, do not let it distract you. The chemistry inside is always the same: identify the element, find its total mass in the formula, divide by RFM, multiply by 100.

Mathematical Skills

Three Edexcel maths skills are explicitly tagged for this spec point in your slice:

  • 1a — Recognise and use expressions in decimal form. The intermediate working (28 ÷ 132 = 0.21212...) is decimal, and you need to be confident handling repeating decimals on your calculator.
  • 1c — Use ratios, fractions and percentages. Percentage composition is a fraction (mass of element ÷ RFM) converted to a percentage — the bedrock skill of the topic.
  • 2a — Use an appropriate number of significant figures. As emphasised by both June 2019 examiner reports, this is where students lose final marks most often.

Typical tariff for this calculation in Edexcel papers is 2–3 marks. The two marks are usually one for the correct method (or RFM) and one for the correct final numerical answer to the required precision; the third mark, where present, is usually for an extra step (e.g. scaling to a given mass, or comparing two fertilisers).

1.6.6.1 Calculating Concentration F+H ▼

Calculating Concentration

Spec 1.49: Calculate the concentration of solutions in g dm⁻³.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

At this spec point you only need the mass form of concentration in g dm⁻³. The moles version (mol dm⁻³) is a separate Higher-tier spec point in Section 5 and is not required here.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ConcentrationThe mass of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution
SoluteThe substance that has been dissolved (the solid being weighed out)
SolventThe liquid the solute is dissolved in — usually water in GCSE problems
SolutionThe mixture formed when a solute dissolves in a solvent
Volume of solutionThe total volume of the solute + solvent mixture after dissolving (not just the volume of solvent)
g dm⁻³Grams of solute per cubic decimetre of solution — the unit used at this spec point
dm³Cubic decimetre. One dm³ is exactly the same volume as 1 litre (1 L) and equals 1000 cm³

What concentration actually tells you

Concentration is a measure of how crowded the solute particles are in the solvent. If you dissolve 10 g of salt in 1 dm³ of water, the concentration is 10 g dm⁻³. If you dissolve the same 10 g in only 0.5 dm³, the concentration doubles to 20 g dm⁻³ — same amount of solute, smaller volume, more crowded particles.

This is why concentration always has two ingredients: a mass and a volume. You cannot describe a solution as "concentrated" without giving both, and you cannot calculate a concentration without knowing both.

The Formula

The relationship between concentration, mass and volume is:

`` concentration (g dm⁻³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³) ``

Or in short form:

`` c = m / V ``

Where:

  • c = concentration in g dm⁻³
  • m = mass of solute in grams (g)
  • V = volume of solution in cubic decimetres (dm³)

Rearranging the formula gives the other two forms you may need:

To findRearrangement
Concentrationc = m ÷ V
Mass of solutem = c × V
Volume of solutionV = m ÷ c

These three rearrangements are exactly the same idea as the speed/distance/time triangle. If you remember c = m / V, you can rearrange to get the other two by basic algebra — no triangle drawing needed, but draw one if it helps.

The whole topic stands or falls on getting the volume units right. Memorise these equivalences:

  • To convert cm³ → dm³: divide by 1000
  • To convert dm³ → cm³: multiply by 1000

  • 250 cm³ = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.25 dm³
  • 500 cm³ = 500 ÷ 1000 = 0.5 dm³

Worked Example 1 — Volume already in dm³

Question: 5 g of sodium chloride is dissolved to make 0.5 dm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration in g dm⁻³.

*Procedure:*

  1. Write the formula: c = m / V
  2. Identify values: m = 5 g, V = 0.5 dm³ (already in dm³ — no conversion needed)
  3. Substitute: c = 5 ÷ 0.5
  4. Answer: c = 10 g dm⁻³

Check: the answer is a sensible number with the correct unit. Always include the unit in your final answer — examiners can withhold a mark if the unit is missing or wrong.

Worked Example 2 — Volume in cm³ (the standard exam version)

Question: 4 g of potassium nitrate is dissolved in water to make 250 cm³ of solution. Calculate the concentration of the solution in g dm⁻³.

*Procedure:*

  1. Spot the unit mismatch — the volume is in cm³, but the answer needs g dm⁻³, so the volume must be in dm³ before you divide
  2. Convert: 250 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.25 dm³
  3. Write the formula: c = m / V
  4. Substitute: c = 4 ÷ 0.25
  5. Answer: c = 16 g dm⁻³

Common student error: forgetting step 2 and doing 4 ÷ 250 = 0.016, then writing "0.016 g dm⁻³". This is wrong by a factor of 1000 and will lose at least one mark.

Worked Example 3 — Find the mass

Question: A solution of copper sulfate has a concentration of 20 g dm⁻³. What mass of copper sulfate is dissolved in 100 cm³ of this solution?

*Procedure:*

  1. Convert the volume: 100 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.1 dm³
  2. Choose the rearrangement: m = c × V
  3. Substitute: m = 20 × 0.1
  4. Answer: m = 2 g

Sense check: 100 cm³ is a tenth of a dm³, so the mass dissolved should be a tenth of 20 g, which is 2 g. The answer fits.

Worked Example 4 — Find the volume

Question: A student needs to prepare a solution containing 6 g of sodium hydroxide at a concentration of 24 g dm⁻³. What volume of solution must they make? Give your answer in dm³ and in cm³.

*Procedure:*

  1. Choose the rearrangement: V = m / c
  2. Substitute: V = 6 ÷ 24
  3. Answer in dm³: V = 0.25 dm³
  4. Convert to cm³: 0.25 × 1000 = 250 cm³

This kind of question is common in practical/required-practical-style write-ups, where the student is asked how much solvent to add to dissolve a measured mass to a target concentration.

Worked Example 5 — Two-step problem

Question: A student dissolves 8 g of solid X in water and makes the total volume of the solution up to 400 cm³. Calculate the concentration of the solution in g dm⁻³.

*Procedure:*

  1. Convert the volume: 400 cm³ ÷ 1000 = 0.4 dm³
  2. Write the formula: c = m / V
  3. Substitute: c = 8 ÷ 0.4
  4. Answer: c = 20 g dm⁻³

Note the wording "made up to" — this means the total volume of solution is 400 cm³, not the volume of water added. This is the volume that goes into the formula. Always read carefully for "made up to" vs "dissolved in" — both usually mean the same thing in GCSE, but it is the final volume of solution that matters, not the volume of solvent.

The Mole

Spec 1.50–1.51: Recall the definition of one mole as the Avogadro constant number of particles (6.02 × 10²³) and as a mass of 'relative particle mass' grams; calculate moles, particles and masses interchangeably.

Higher tier only · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Mole (mol)The amount of substance that contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules, formula units or ions) of that substance
Avogadro constantThe number of particles in one mole of any substance: 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
ParticleA general term used in the mole definition — it can mean atoms, molecules, formula units or ions, depending on the substance
Relative atomic mass (Ar)The mean mass of an atom of an element compared with 1/12 the mass of a ¹²C atom — numerically equal to the mass in grams of one mole of atoms of that element
Relative formula mass (Mr)The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula — numerically equal to the mass in grams of one mole of formula units
Molar massThe mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g mol⁻¹), numerically equal to Ar or Mr

Why chemists invented the mole

Chemical reactions happen between particles — individual atoms, molecules or ions. But you cannot count individual particles when you are weighing things out on a balance. A single atom is far too small and far too light to handle. What chemists need is a way to bundle particles together into a counted amount that is big enough to weigh on a normal lab balance — and that bundle is the mole.

One mole always contains the same number of particles: 6.02 × 10²³, known as the Avogadro constant. The size of this number is staggering — 602 sextillion. It was chosen so that the mass of one mole of any substance, in grams, is numerically equal to its relative formula mass (Mr). That single design choice is what makes the mole so powerful: it links the macroscopic world you can weigh (grams) to the microscopic world that actually reacts (particles).

This means three things are linked by one number, the Avogadro constant:

  • A mass in grams that you can weigh out on a balance.
  • An amount in moles — a counted bundle of particles.
  • A number of particles — the actual atoms, molecules or ions doing the chemistry.

You can move freely between these three quantities once you know two simple rules.

The two rules that connect everything

`` Rule 1: moles = mass ÷ Mr (or: mass = moles × Mr) Rule 2: number of particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³ ``

Every Higher-tier mole question is some combination of these two rules. The trick is reading the question carefully so you spot what you are given and what you are asked for — then chain the rules to bridge the two.

To go from…To…Use

The last two rows are the most demanding because they ask you to chain both rules together. They are exactly the style examiners target in 3-mark calculation questions at Higher tier.

The "what counts as a particle?" check

Before you use 6.02 × 10²³, you must know what kind of particle the question is asking about. The Avogadro constant counts whatever particle the substance is made of:

  • For an element that exists as atoms (e.g. copper, Cu, or any noble gas), one mole = 6.02 × 10²³ atoms.
  • For a molecular substance (e.g. water, H₂O, or carbon dioxide, CO₂), one mole = 6.02 × 10²³ molecules.
  • For an ionic compound (e.g. sodium chloride, NaCl, or copper iodide, CuI), one mole = 6.02 × 10²³ formula units — i.e. 6.02 × 10²³ pairs of Na⁺ and Cl⁻.
  • For a substance considered as separate ions (e.g. "how many Cl⁻ ions in one mole of NaCl?"), each formula unit contains one Cl⁻ ion, so the answer is again 6.02 × 10²³ ions.

If the question says "calculate the number of atoms" — count atoms. If it says "calculate the number of molecules" — count molecules. Mixing these up is one of the most punished mistakes on this topic.

Worked Example 1 — Moles from mass

Question: Calculate the number of moles of carbon dioxide in 22 g of CO₂. (Ar: C = 12, O = 16)

*Procedure:*

  1. Calculate Mr (CO₂) = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44.
  2. Use moles = mass ÷ Mr.
  3. moles = 22 ÷ 44 = 0.5 mol.

The answer carries the unit mol, not g, because we have converted to amount of substance.

Worked Example 2 — Mass from moles

Question: What is the mass of 0.2 mol of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃? (Ar: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)

*Procedure:*

  1. Calculate Mr (CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100.
  2. Use mass = moles × Mr.
  3. mass = 0.2 × 100 = 20 g.

Worked Example 3 — Number of particles from moles

Question: Calculate the number of water molecules in 0.25 mol of H₂O.

*Procedure:*

  1. Use particles = moles × Avogadro constant.
  2. particles = 0.25 × 6.02 × 10²³.
  3. particles = 1.505 × 10²³ molecules (≈ 1.51 × 10²³ to 3 sig fig).

Notice that the answer is in standard form. Answers involving the Avogadro constant will almost always be in standard form — write them that way deliberately.

Worked Example 4 — Particles from mass (the two-step chain)

Question: Calculate the number of copper atoms in 74 mg of copper. (Ar: Cu = 63.5)

This is the style of question that appeared as 1CH0/1H — June 2023 Q6(d) — see the Examiner Report Insights below.

*Procedure:*

  1. Convert units first. The mass is in milligrams, but Mr is in grams per mole, so you must convert: 74 mg = 74 ÷ 1000 = 0.074 g.
  2. Calculate moles: moles = 0.074 ÷ 63.5 = 1.165 × 10⁻³ mol (to 4 sig fig).
  3. Calculate particles: particles = 1.165 × 10⁻³ × 6.02 × 10²³.
  4. particles = 7.01 × 10²⁰ atoms (to 3 sig fig).

The two-step chain — mass → moles → particles — is the most demanding mole calculation in GCSE. Set it out clearly, line by line, so the examiner can follow each conversion.

Worked Example 5 — Number of atoms in one mole of a compound

Question: Calculate the total number of atoms in one mole of copper iodide, CuI.

This is the style of question that appeared as 1CH0/1H — June 2019 Q6(c).

*Procedure:*

  1. One formula unit of CuI contains 2 atoms (1 Cu + 1 I).
  2. One mole of CuI contains 6.02 × 10²³ formula units.
  3. Total atoms = 6.02 × 10²³ × 2 = 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms (≈ 1.20 × 10²⁴ to 3 sig fig).

The trap here is that you have to multiply the Avogadro constant by the number of atoms in one formula unit, because the question asks for the total number of atoms — not the number of formula units, and not the number of moles. Examiners specifically flagged this pitfall on 1CH0/1H June 2019 (see Examiner Report Insights).

1.6.8.1 Limiting Reagents H only ▼

Limiting Reagents

Spec 1.52: Explain why, in a reaction, the mass of product formed is controlled by the mass of the reactant which is not in excess.

Higher tier only · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Limiting reagentThe reactant that runs out first in a chemical reaction — it controls the maximum mass of product that can be formed
Reactant in excessA reactant that is present in a larger amount than is needed to react with the limiting reagent — some of it is left over when the reaction stops
Stoichiometric ratioThe whole-number ratio in which substances react, taken directly from the coefficients in the balanced equation
Maximum yieldThe mass of product that can be made when the limiting reagent has fully reacted — it cannot be exceeded no matter how much excess reactant is added
Mole (Higher tier)A measured amount of substance equal to mass ÷ Mr; reactants combine in the ratio shown by the balanced equation when measured in moles

Why one reactant controls how much product forms

A chemical reaction needs reactants to combine in the fixed ratio set by the balanced equation. If you put in more of one reactant than that ratio allows, the extra has nothing to react with. The reaction stops as soon as the first reactant is fully used up — and that reactant is the limiting reagent.

Think of a sandwich-making analogy. A sandwich needs 2 slices of bread + 1 slice of cheese. If you have 10 slices of bread but only 3 slices of cheese, you can only make 3 sandwiches. The cheese is the limiting reagent. You will have 4 slices of bread left over (10 − 6 = 4) — bread is in excess. Even if you put in 50 slices of bread, you still only get 3 sandwiches. The amount of product is set by whichever ingredient runs out first, never by the one you have lots of.

In chemistry the same logic applies, but you cannot judge "who runs out first" by mass alone. 6 g of magnesium is not the same number of atoms as 6 g of oxygen — magnesium atoms are heavier. You must compare reactants in moles, because the balanced equation gives you the mole ratio, not the mass ratio. This is why limiting-reagent problems are Higher tier only: they require you to be confident with the mole concept first.

Why excess matters in practice

Reactions in the lab and in industry are almost always set up with one reactant in excess deliberately. There are two main reasons:

  • To make sure the valuable reactant reacts completely. If you are trying to extract every gram of an expensive product, you want to be certain none of your costly reactant is wasted. You make the other (cheaper) reactant the excess one — its job is to "guarantee" the valuable reactant is fully consumed.
  • To speed up the reaction. With more of one reactant present, collisions between particles are more frequent and the reaction goes faster (link to collision theory).

The reactant in excess is therefore a deliberate choice — usually the cheaper or more abundant one. The reactant that limits is the one whose mass you care about for the yield calculation. The mass of product is always controlled by the limiting reagent, no matter how much extra of the other reactant you add.

The general method for limiting-reagent problems

Every limiting-reagent calculation follows the same four steps. Learn the sequence — only the numbers change.

  1. Write out and check the balanced equation. You cannot do the calculation from an unbalanced equation. Note the ratio in which the two reactants combine.
  2. Convert each reactant's mass to moles. Use moles = mass ÷ Mr for each reactant separately.
  3. Compare the moles to the equation's ratio. Whichever reactant has too few moles (relative to what the ratio demands) is the limiting reagent. The other one is in excess.
  4. Use the limiting reagent's moles to work out moles of product, then convert that to a mass with mass = moles × Mr.

The trickiest step is step 3 — comparing the moles fairly. The cleanest way is to divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient in the balanced equation. Whichever gives the smaller number is the limiting reagent. That single trick handles every ratio, no matter how awkward.

Worked Example 1 — 1:1 ratio

Question: 4.8 g of magnesium reacts with 4.8 g of oxygen: 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO. Which reactant is the limiting reagent, and what mass of magnesium oxide is produced? (Ar: Mg = 24, O = 16)

*Step 1 — Balanced equation and ratio.*

2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO. The reactants combine in the ratio Mg : O₂ = 2 : 1.

*Step 2 — Convert each reactant to moles.*

  • Mr (Mg) = 24, so moles of Mg = 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.2 mol.
  • Mr (O₂) = 32, so moles of O₂ = 4.8 ÷ 32 = 0.15 mol.

*Step 3 — Identify the limiting reagent.*

Divide each by its coefficient:

  • Mg: 0.2 ÷ 2 = 0.10
  • O₂: 0.15 ÷ 1 = 0.15

Mg gives the smaller number, so magnesium is the limiting reagent. Oxygen is in excess.

*Step 4 — Mass of product.*

From the equation, 2 Mg makes 2 MgO, so the Mg : MgO mole ratio is 1 : 1. Moles of MgO = 0.2 mol. Mr (MgO) = 24 + 16 = 40. Mass of MgO = 0.2 × 40 = 8.0 g.

Sense check: even though there is "enough" oxygen, only 0.2 mol of MgO can form because there are only 0.2 mol of Mg atoms to react. The excess oxygen does nothing once magnesium runs out.

Worked Example 2 — 1:2 ratio (carbonate + acid)

Question: 5.0 g of calcium carbonate is added to 50 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid containing 3.65 g of HCl: CaCO₃ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Identify the limiting reagent and calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced. (Ar: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16, H = 1, Cl = 35.5)

*Step 1 — Balanced equation and ratio.*

CaCO₃ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. The reactants combine in the ratio CaCO₃ : HCl = 1 : 2.

*Step 2 — Convert each reactant to moles.*

  • Mr (CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. Moles of CaCO₃ = 5.0 ÷ 100 = 0.050 mol.
  • Mr (HCl) = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5. Moles of HCl = 3.65 ÷ 36.5 = 0.100 mol.

*Step 3 — Identify the limiting reagent.*

Divide each by its coefficient:

  • CaCO₃: 0.050 ÷ 1 = 0.050
  • HCl: 0.100 ÷ 2 = 0.050

The two numbers are equal — this is the special case where the reactants are mixed in exactly the right ratio and neither is in excess. They both run out at the same time. Either could be called the "limiting" reagent because both fully react.

*Step 4 — Mass of product.*

From the equation, 1 CaCO₃ makes 1 CO₂, so moles of CO₂ = 0.050 mol. Mr (CO₂) = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44. Mass of CO₂ = 0.050 × 44 = 2.2 g.

This example shows the importance of dividing by the coefficient. If you had only compared moles directly (0.05 vs 0.10) you might have assumed CaCO₃ was the limiting reagent — but the equation needs twice as much HCl, so 0.10 mol of HCl is exactly the right amount to react with 0.05 mol of CaCO₃.

Worked Example 3 — 2:3 ratio (an awkward stoichiometry)

Question: 10.2 g of aluminium oxide reacts with 4.8 g of carbon in a thermal reduction: 2 Al₂O₃ + 3 C → 4 Al + 3 CO₂. Identify the limiting reagent and calculate the maximum mass of aluminium that can be made. (Ar: Al = 27, O = 16, C = 12)

*Step 1 — Balanced equation and ratio.*

The reactants combine in the ratio Al₂O₃ : C = 2 : 3.

*Step 2 — Convert each reactant to moles.*

  • Mr (Al₂O₃) = (2 × 27) + (3 × 16) = 102. Moles of Al₂O₃ = 10.2 ÷ 102 = 0.10 mol.
  • Mr (C) = 12. Moles of C = 4.8 ÷ 12 = 0.40 mol.

*Step 3 — Identify the limiting reagent.*

Divide each by its coefficient:

  • Al₂O₃: 0.10 ÷ 2 = 0.050
  • C: 0.40 ÷ 3 = 0.133

Al₂O₃ gives the smaller value, so aluminium oxide is the limiting reagent. Carbon is in excess.

*Step 4 — Mass of product.*

From the equation, 2 Al₂O₃ makes 4 Al, so the Al₂O₃ : Al mole ratio is 2 : 4 = 1 : 2. Moles of Al = 0.10 × 2 = 0.20 mol. Mass of Al = 0.20 × 27 = 5.4 g.

This worked example shows why the "divide moles by coefficient" trick is worth memorising. Comparing 0.10 mol to 0.40 mol directly might suggest Al₂O₃ is limiting "because there's less of it", but a 2 : 3 ratio means you need to scale before you can judge. Dividing by the coefficient handles the scaling automatically.

Explain-style answers — what examiners want

Spec 1.52 begins with "Explain why…". You may meet a non-numerical version of this content where you have to explain in prose why one reactant controls the product mass. A full-mark Explain answer at Higher tier should include all three of these ideas:

  • The reactants combine in a fixed ratio set by the balanced equation.
  • The reactant in excess is left over when the other runs out — the reaction stops as soon as the first reactant is fully used.
  • The reactant that runs out first (the limiting reagent) sets the maximum amount of product that can form; adding more of the excess reactant does not make any more product.

A weaker answer that says only "because there isn't enough of one reactant" misses the link to the equation ratio and usually scores 1 out of 2. Always name the balanced equation as the source of the ratio, and always make explicit that adding extra excess reactant does nothing.

Writing & Balancing Equations

Why equations have to balance

A chemical reaction is a rearrangement of atoms, not a creation or destruction of them. The same atoms that were present at the start of the reaction must all be present at the end — they have just been pulled apart from one set of bonds and joined into a new set. Because every atom has a fixed mass, this rearrangement is exactly why the law of conservation of mass holds (see 1.6.4.1 Conservation of Mass).

A correctly balanced symbol equation is the visual, algebraic statement of that physical truth. If you can count, say, 4 hydrogen atoms on the left, you must be able to count 4 hydrogen atoms on the right. If the counts disagree, the equation is wrong — it is telling you that atoms have appeared from nowhere or vanished into nothing, which never happens in a chemical reaction.

A balanced equation also carries a second piece of information that an unbalanced equation cannot: the ratio in which substances react and are produced. The big numbers in front of each formula (the coefficients) tell you that for every x units of one substance, y units of another are involved. That ratio is the foundation of every reacting-mass calculation — see 1.6.5.1 Reacting Masses.

Word equations (spec 0.2)

Word equations are the simplest way to record a reaction. They use names, not formulae. The reactants go on the left of the arrow and the products go on the right, with each substance separated by a + sign.

The general layout is:

reactant 1 + reactant 2 → product 1 + product 2

The arrow → means "reacts to make" or "produces". It always points from reactants to products.

*Worked examples — word equations*

  • Magnesium burns in oxygen: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
  • Hydrochloric acid neutralises sodium hydroxide: hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water
  • Calcium carbonate decomposes on heating: calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide
  • Zinc displaces copper from copper sulfate solution: zinc + copper sulfate → zinc sulfate + copper

A few rules to apply every time:

  1. Use names, never formulae. "Sodium chloride", not "NaCl".
  2. Use a single arrow → between reactants and products — not an equals sign.
  3. Use a + between each reactant and between each product.
  4. No state symbols are needed in a word equation (state symbols are a requirement of the symbol equation in spec 0.3).
  5. Use the names exactly as they appear in the question or as given by IUPAC convention — "magnesium oxide", not "Mg oxide".

If the question gives you the names of the reactants and products in the stem, use the names from the stem. You do not need to invent or guess names.

A note on reversible reactions: if a reaction is reversible, the single arrow is replaced by a double-headed equilibrium arrow (⇌). For example: nitrogen + hydrogen ⇌ ammonia. The same names go either side of the symbol.

Symbol equations and state symbols (spec 0.3)

A symbol equation replaces every name with a chemical formula. The structure is the same as a word equation — reactants on the left, products on the right, separated by an arrow — but you now need to:

  1. Use the correct chemical formula for every species.
  2. Include the state symbol in brackets after every formula.
  3. Balance the equation so that each element has the same total atom count on both sides.

State symbols are part of the spec text and must be included. Use:

  • (s) for solid
  • (l) for liquid (a pure substance in its liquid state, e.g. water from a reaction)
  • (g) for gas
  • (aq) for aqueous — a substance dissolved in water

Leave a small space between the formula and the state symbol — HCl (aq), not HCl(aq).

A simple worked layout: the neutralisation of hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide.

HCl (aq) + NaOH (aq) → NaCl (aq) + H₂O (l)

Check the atoms: 1 H + 1 Cl + 1 Na + 1 O + 1 H on the left, and 1 Na + 1 Cl + 2 H + 1 O on the right. Both sides give 2 H, 1 Cl, 1 Na and 1 O. The equation is balanced.

How to balance an equation — step by step

Use a fixed procedure every time. Doing the same steps in the same order is the difference between getting the balancing mark every time and losing it on harder reactions.

  1. Write the unbalanced equation using the correct formulae for every reactant and product. Do not change the formulae once they are written down — you balance by changing the big numbers in front, never the small numbers inside.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on each side. List them out as a tally.
  3. Pick one element that is out of balance. Start with an element that appears in only one reactant and one product (avoid hydrogen and oxygen until the end if you can — they often appear in several places).
  4. Add a coefficient in front of one of the formulae to even out that element.
  5. Re-count every element, because changing a coefficient changes more than one atom count.
  6. Repeat steps 3–5 until every element has the same total on both sides.
  7. Check by re-counting every element one final time.
  8. Add state symbols if the question asks for a balanced equation including states.

The golden rule, again: you only ever change the big coefficients in front of a formula — never the small subscripts inside a formula. Changing H₂O to H₂O₂ does not balance the equation, it changes the substance.

Diatomic elements — a balancing trap to know

Seven elements exist as diatomic molecules at room temperature. You must write them as the two-atom formula whenever they appear on their own (i.e. uncombined with another element):

  • H₂ hydrogen
  • N₂ nitrogen
  • O₂ oxygen
  • F₂ fluorine
  • Cl₂ chlorine
  • Br₂ bromine
  • I₂ iodine

Writing oxygen as O instead of O₂ is one of the most common balancing mistakes. The atom count looks balanced because you have miscounted the left-hand side from the start. If you ever see one of those seven elements in an equation as an uncombined element, it must be the diatomic formula.

Metallic and most other non-metal elements are written as a single letter or symbol with no subscript when they appear uncombined — for example Mg, Cu, C, Na. They are not diatomic.

Common balancing mistakes — and how to spot them

  1. Changing the subscripts inside a formula. Turning H₂O into H₂O₂ to "balance" oxygen is wrong — H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a completely different substance.
  2. Forgetting the diatomic elements. Writing O instead of O₂ in a combustion equation gives the wrong atom count from the start.
  3. Stopping after balancing one element. Changing a coefficient changes every atom count in that formula. After every change, re-count the whole equation.
  4. Leaving out state symbols. Spec 0.3 explicitly requires (s), (l), (g) and (aq). A balanced equation without state symbols can lose 1 mark.
  5. Using fractional coefficients on the final answer. A balanced GCSE equation should use the smallest whole-number ratio. If you end up with ½ O₂, multiply every coefficient by 2.
  6. Writing names instead of formulae in a symbol equation, or formulae instead of names in a word equation.

1.6.9.2 Ionic Equations H only ▼

Ionic Equations

Syllabus reference: 0.4 — Write balanced ionic equations. **Higher tier only**.

An ionic equation is a shortened version of a full balanced symbol equation. It shows only the particles that actually change during a reaction in aqueous solution. Any ion that appears unchanged on both sides — same formula, same charge, same state — is left out. These unchanged ions are called spectator ions because they are present in the beaker but take no part in the chemistry.

Ionic equations are a Higher-tier skill on Edexcel GCSE Chemistry. They test whether you really understand what is happening at particle level — not just whether you can balance numbers.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Ionic equationA balanced equation showing only the ions and atoms that take part in a chemical change, with all spectator ions removed
Spectator ionAn ion that is present in the reaction mixture but is unchanged before and after the reaction (same formula, same charge, same state on both sides)
Aqueous solutionA solution in which the solvent is water, shown by the state symbol (aq); ionic compounds that dissolve in water split fully into their separate ions
PrecipitateAn insoluble solid formed when two soluble solutions are mixed; shown by the state symbol (s)
NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base/alkali to form a salt and water; at ionic level, H⁺ ions react with OH⁻ ions
Displacement reactionA reaction in which a more reactive element pushes a less reactive element out of one of its compounds; at ionic level, electrons transfer between metal atoms and metal ions

Why ionic equations are useful

A full balanced equation lists every formula in the reaction, but it can hide what is really going on. For example, mixing silver nitrate solution with sodium chloride solution gives a white precipitate of silver chloride. The full equation is:

AgNO₃ (aq) + NaCl (aq) → AgCl (s) + NaNO₃ (aq)

The actual chemistry, though, is just the silver and chloride ions joining together to form an insoluble solid. The sodium and nitrate ions float around in solution before mixing and after mixing — they never touch each other or change. The ionic equation strips these spectators out:

Ag⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) → AgCl (s)

That single line tells you the whole reaction: a silver ion and a chloride ion combine to form solid silver chloride. The same equation describes any precipitation of silver chloride, regardless of which sodium-containing or silver-containing salt you start with. Ionic equations therefore reveal the underlying chemistry that the full equation can obscure.

When to split a formula into ions

This is the most important rule, and the one candidates most often get wrong:

State symbolWhat happens in the ionic equation
(aq) — ionic compound dissolved in waterSplit into the separate ions, with charges shown
(s) — solidLeave as the whole compound, do not split
(l) — pure liquid (e.g. H₂O)Leave as the whole molecule, do not split
(g) — gasLeave as the whole molecule, do not split
Element on its own (e.g. Zn, Cu, H₂)Leave as the element, do not split
Covalent molecule in solution (e.g. water from a neutralisation)Leave as the whole molecule, do not split

In other words, only ionic substances that are dissolved in water are written as separate ions. Everything else stays as it appears in the full equation.

The step-by-step process

You can convert any full balanced equation into an ionic equation by following the same five steps every time:

  1. Write the full balanced symbol equation, including state symbols. Without state symbols you cannot decide what to split.
  2. Split every (aq) ionic compound into its constituent ions, multiplying by the balancing number. Show the charge on each ion. Anything (s), (l), (g) or a pure element stays as it is.
  3. Identify spectator ions — any ion that appears identically on both sides of the equation.
  4. Cancel the spectator ions from both sides.
  5. Check that the remaining equation balances for both atoms and charge. The total charge on the left must equal the total charge on the right.

Worked example 1 — Precipitation: silver nitrate and sodium chloride

When solutions of silver nitrate and sodium chloride are mixed, a white precipitate of silver chloride forms. This is a classic test for chloride ions.

*Step 1 — full balanced equation, with state symbols*

AgNO₃ (aq) + NaCl (aq) → AgCl (s) + NaNO₃ (aq)

*Step 2 — split every (aq) ionic compound into ions*

Ag⁺ (aq) + NO₃⁻ (aq) + Na⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) → AgCl (s) + Na⁺ (aq) + NO₃⁻ (aq)

Notice that AgCl (s) is not split — it is a solid precipitate, not in solution.

*Step 3 — identify spectator ions*

Na⁺ (aq) appears unchanged on both sides. NO₃⁻ (aq) appears unchanged on both sides. Both are spectators.

*Step 4 — cancel the spectators*

Ag⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) → AgCl (s)

*Step 5 — check balance*

Atoms: 1 Ag and 1 Cl on each side. Charge: (+1) + (−1) = 0 on the left; 0 on the right (a neutral solid). Balanced.

Worked example 2 — Neutralisation: hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide

When a strong acid reacts with a strong alkali, the only chemistry happening is the combination of H⁺ ions with OH⁻ ions to form water. Everything else is a spectator.

*Step 1 — full balanced equation*

HCl (aq) + NaOH (aq) → NaCl (aq) + H₂O (l)

*Step 2 — split every (aq) ionic compound*

H⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) + Na⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → Na⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) + H₂O (l)

Water is a covalent molecular liquid, not an ionic compound in solution — it stays as H₂O (l).

*Step 3 — identify spectators*

Na⁺ (aq) is on both sides. Cl⁻ (aq) is on both sides.

*Step 4 — cancel*

H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l)

*Step 5 — check balance*

Atoms: 1 H from H⁺, plus 1 O and 1 H from OH⁻ → 2 H and 1 O on the left; 2 H and 1 O on the right. Charge: (+1) + (−1) = 0 on the left; 0 on the right. Balanced.

This same ionic equation describes the neutralisation of any strong acid by any strong alkali in solution — the spectators change, but the underlying chemistry does not.

Worked example 3 — Redox displacement: zinc and copper sulfate

A piece of zinc dropped into blue copper(II) sulfate solution slowly turns pinkish-brown as copper metal coats it, and the blue colour fades. This is a redox displacement.

*Step 1 — full balanced equation*

Zn (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → ZnSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s)

*Step 2 — split every (aq) ionic compound*

Zn (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) + Cu (s)

Zn (s) and Cu (s) are pure elements as solids — they do not split.

*Step 3 — identify spectators*

SO₄²⁻ (aq) is identical on both sides.

*Step 4 — cancel*

Zn (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + Cu (s)

*Step 5 — check balance*

Atoms: 1 Zn and 1 Cu on each side. Charge: 0 + (+2) = +2 on the left; (+2) + 0 = +2 on the right. Balanced.

This ionic equation makes it very clear what is happening: a zinc atom loses two electrons (oxidation) and a copper(II) ion gains those two electrons (reduction).

Balancing atoms AND charge

A common slip is to balance the atoms perfectly but leave the charges unbalanced. The total charge on the left of the arrow must equal the total charge on the right. Charge is conserved in every chemical reaction, just like mass.

For example, suppose a student writes:

Ag⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) → AgCl₂ (s) ← wrong

The atoms do not balance (1 Cl on the left, 2 Cl on the right) and the formula AgCl₂ does not even exist. The correct silver chloride formula is AgCl (silver is +1, chloride is −1, ratio 1:1). Always derive the correct ionic formula first before writing the equation, using the rules you applied in 1.3.3.2 Deducing Formulae.

Another example — combining sulfate and copper(II) ions:

Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2 OH⁻ (aq) → Cu(OH)₂ (s)

Here the balancing number 2 in front of OH⁻ matters for both atoms (you need 2 OH groups in copper hydroxide) and charge (the left side must equal 0, since the precipitate is neutral). Without the 2, the left side would have charge +1 and the right side 0, which is impossible.

Mathematical Skills

Ionic equations call on mathematical skill 1c — Use ratios, fractions and percentages. The key numerical work is the balancing-number ratio in front of each ion. For example, in:

Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2 OH⁻ (aq) → Cu(OH)₂ (s)

the 1 : 2 ratio between Cu²⁺ and OH⁻ is essential. The same ratio thinking is needed when balancing for charge: if the cation has charge +3 and the anion has charge −1, you will need three of the anion for the charges to balance (e.g. Al³⁺ + 3 Cl⁻ → AlCl₃, although in this example AlCl₃ in solution is also commonly written split).

Procedure:

  1. Write the correct ionic formula of each species using the periodic-table group rule and the polyatomic-ion list.
  2. Choose balancing numbers so that the total atoms of each element are equal on both sides.
  3. Add up the total charge on each side. If they are not equal, the balancing numbers are wrong — go back to step 2.
Key Takeaways
  1. An ionic equation shows only the particles that change during a reaction; spectator ions are cancelled out.
  2. Only aqueous ionic compounds — those with state symbol (aq) — are split into their separate ions in an ionic equation.
  3. Solids (s), liquids (l), gases (g), elements, and covalent molecules such as H₂O are not split, regardless of where they appear.
  4. The standard process is: full balanced equation → split (aq) ionic compounds → cancel ions identical on both sides → check atoms and charges balance.
  5. Charge must balance just like atoms: the total charge on the left must equal the total charge on the right.
  6. Silver nitrate + sodium chloride: ionic equation is Ag⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) → AgCl (s).
  7. Strong acid + strong alkali neutralisation: the ionic equation is always H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l), no matter which acid and alkali.
  8. Displacement of copper by zinc: Zn (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + Cu (s); this reveals that zinc is oxidised and copper(II) is reduced.
  9. Simply re-copying the full balanced equation in answer to an "ionic equation" question scores zero marks (1CH0/2H June 2019 Examiner Report).
  10. Common errors flagged by the November 2021 Examiner Report include strange chemical formulae such as "AgN", incorrect ion charges (e.g. Cl₂⁻ instead of Cl⁻), and pairing two negative ions together — none of which can ever be correct.
  11. Always derive each ion's correct charge from its position in the periodic table or the polyatomic-ion list before writing the equation.
  12. Ionic equations are Higher tier only, typically worth 2–3 marks, and can appear on either Paper 1 or Paper 2.

Deducing Stoichiometry

Spec 1.53: Deduce the stoichiometry of a reaction from the masses of the reactants and products.

Higher tier only · Separate (1CH0) + Combined (1SC0) · Paper 1 or Paper 2.

This Higher-tier spec point is the reverse of the reacting-masses calculation. In a normal reacting-mass question you are given a balanced equation and asked to scale a mass through its coefficients. Here you are given experimental masses and asked to work out the coefficients themselves. The big numbers in the balanced equation are not given — you have to deduce them from the data.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
StoichiometryThe whole-number ratio in which substances react and are produced, given by the coefficients (the big numbers) in front of each formula in a balanced equation
Stoichiometric coefficientThe whole number written in front of a formula in a balanced equation; it counts how many formula units (or moles) of that substance take part
MoleAn amount of substance equal to mass ÷ Mr; substances react in the ratio shown by the equation when measured in moles
Mole ratioThe ratio of moles of one substance to another in a reaction; equal to the ratio of their coefficients in the balanced equation
Simplest whole-number ratioThe mole ratio expressed in the lowest possible integers (e.g. 0.20 : 0.10 → 2 : 1)
Relative formula mass (Mr)The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the formula of a substance

Why mole ratio equals coefficient ratio

A balanced equation tells you how many particles of each substance take part in the reaction. For example, in

2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO

two atoms of magnesium react with one molecule of oxygen to make two formula units of magnesium oxide. That "2 : 1 : 2" relationship holds whether you have two of each particle, two billion of each, or two moles of each. A mole is just a fixed count of particles (6.02 × 10²³ of them), so the ratio of moles taking part is identical to the ratio of particles taking part, which is identical to the ratio of coefficients.

That is the whole logic behind this spec point: if you can measure the moles of each substance that actually reacted, then the simplest whole-number ratio of those moles is the stoichiometry of the balanced equation. The coefficients are not something you have to invent — the experimental data hands them to you, as long as you set the problem up correctly.

The four-step procedure

Every "deduce the stoichiometry" question follows the same four steps. Learn them in this order and you will not lose method marks even if the arithmetic at the end goes wrong.

  1. Mass of each substance. Read the masses straight from the question. If one mass is missing (typically the mass of a gas absorbed from or released to the air), use conservation of mass to find it: the total mass of reactants must equal the total mass of products.
  2. Mr of each substance. Calculate the relative formula mass of each reactant and product using the relative atomic masses given on the periodic table.
  3. Moles of each substance. Use moles = mass ÷ Mr to turn every mass into a number of moles. Keep at least 3 significant figures at this stage — do not round too early.
  4. Simplest whole-number ratio. Divide every mole value by the smallest mole value in your list. The result should be a set of numbers very close to small whole numbers (1, 2, 3, …) — those are the stoichiometric coefficients. If any of the numbers come out near .5 (e.g. 1.5), multiply the whole ratio by 2 to clear the fraction.

The mental check at the end: substitute your deduced coefficients back into the equation and verify the atoms balance. If they do not, you have either calculated an Mr wrongly or rounded too early.

Worked Example 1 — Magnesium burning in oxygen (mass increase)

Question: 4.8 g of magnesium ribbon is heated in air in an open crucible until it has completely reacted with oxygen. The mass of the white magnesium oxide left in the crucible is 8.0 g. Deduce the balanced equation for the reaction. (Ar: Mg = 24, O = 16)

Step 1 — masses. The mass of magnesium that reacted is 4.8 g, and the mass of magnesium oxide formed is 8.0 g. The mass of oxygen taken from the air is found by conservation of mass:

mass of O₂ = mass of MgO − mass of Mg = 8.0 − 4.8 = 3.2 g

Step 2 — Mr values. Mr (Mg) = 24. Mr (O₂) = 2 × 16 = 32. Mr (MgO) = 24 + 16 = 40.

*Step 3 — moles.*

  • Moles of Mg = 4.8 ÷ 24 = 0.20 mol
  • Moles of O₂ = 3.2 ÷ 32 = 0.10 mol
  • Moles of MgO = 8.0 ÷ 40 = 0.20 mol

Step 4 — simplest whole-number ratio. Divide every mole value by the smallest (0.10):

  • Mg : O₂ : MgO = 0.20 : 0.10 : 0.20 = 2 : 1 : 2

Balanced equation: 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO

Atom check: 2 Mg on each side; 2 O on each side. The deduced stoichiometry is consistent — the equation balances.

Worked Example 2 — Decomposition of a carbonate (mass decrease)

Question: A student heats 10.0 g of calcium carbonate in an open crucible until it has fully decomposed. The mass of the white solid left in the crucible is 5.6 g. The only other product is carbon dioxide gas. Deduce the balanced equation for the thermal decomposition. (Ar: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16)

Step 1 — masses. Mass of CaCO₃ = 10.0 g. Mass of CaO = 5.6 g. By conservation of mass:

mass of CO₂ = mass of CaCO₃ − mass of CaO = 10.0 − 5.6 = 4.4 g

Step 2 — Mr values. Mr (CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. Mr (CaO) = 40 + 16 = 56. Mr (CO₂) = 12 + (2 × 16) = 44.

*Step 3 — moles.*

  • Moles of CaCO₃ = 10.0 ÷ 100 = 0.100 mol
  • Moles of CaO = 5.6 ÷ 56 = 0.100 mol
  • Moles of CO₂ = 4.4 ÷ 44 = 0.100 mol

Step 4 — simplest whole-number ratio. Every mole value is the same:

  • CaCO₃ : CaO : CO₂ = 0.100 : 0.100 : 0.100 = 1 : 1 : 1

Balanced equation: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Atom check: 1 Ca, 1 C and 3 O on each side. The deduced 1 : 1 : 1 ratio is correct.

This example illustrates two important things. First, "1 : 1 : 1" really is a valid stoichiometry — students sometimes worry that a problem must produce a 2 : 1 or 2 : 3 ratio to count as a "real" deduction, but a 1 : 1 : 1 result is just as legitimate. Second, the data is internally consistent (every Mr × moles product gives back the original mass), which is the experimental statement of conservation of mass.

Worked Example 3 — A ratio that needs clearing of fractions

Question: When a sample of aluminium powder is heated strongly in oxygen, 2.7 g of aluminium reacts completely to form 5.1 g of aluminium oxide. Deduce the balanced equation. (Ar: Al = 27, O = 16)

Step 1 — masses. Mass of Al = 2.7 g. Mass of Al₂O₃ = 5.1 g. Mass of O₂ that reacted = 5.1 − 2.7 = 2.4 g.

Step 2 — Mr values. Mr (Al) = 27. Mr (O₂) = 32. Mr (Al₂O₃) = (2 × 27) + (3 × 16) = 102.

*Step 3 — moles.*

  • Moles of Al = 2.7 ÷ 27 = 0.100 mol
  • Moles of O₂ = 2.4 ÷ 32 = 0.075 mol
  • Moles of Al₂O₃ = 5.1 ÷ 102 = 0.050 mol

Step 4 — simplest whole-number ratio. Divide every value by the smallest (0.050):

  • Al : O₂ : Al₂O₃ = 0.100 : 0.075 : 0.050 = 2 : 1.5 : 1

A 1.5 has appeared — that means the ratio is not yet in whole numbers. Multiply the whole ratio by 2 to clear the fraction:

  • Al : O₂ : Al₂O₃ = 4 : 3 : 2

Balanced equation: 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃

Atom check: 4 Al on each side; 6 O on each side. The equation balances.

This is the most common "trap" in the Higher-tier version of the question. If any of your divided values comes out near .5, do not round it to 1 or to 2 — multiply the whole ratio by 2 to get clean integers. If a value comes out near .33 or .67, multiply the whole ratio by 3 instead.

Comparison — Empirical formula vs deducing stoichiometry

These two procedures look identical on paper, but they answer different questions. Knowing which one a problem is asking for is half the battle.

FeatureEmpirical formulaDeducing stoichiometry
What you are deducingThe simplest whole-number ratio of atoms within a single compoundThe simplest whole-number ratio of substances taking part in a reaction
Data usedMasses (or %) of each element in one compoundMasses of each reactant and product in a reaction
Divide byRelative atomic mass (Ar) of each elementRelative formula mass (Mr) of each substance
OutputA formula such as CH₂, Fe₂O₃A set of coefficients in front of formulae in a balanced equation
TierFoundation + HigherHigher tier only

Both techniques rely on the same underlying logic — "amount in moles is mass ÷ Mr" and "divide every mole value by the smallest to get the simplest whole-number ratio" — which is why students who have practised empirical-formula questions usually pick this skill up quickly.

Reverse-engineering an equation from incomplete data

Sometimes the question gives you the formula of every reactant and product but does not state the coefficients, and supplies only enough masses for you to deduce them. The procedure is unchanged: convert each given mass into moles, divide by the smallest mole value, multiply through to clear any fractions, and insert the resulting integers as the coefficients.

If you are given two masses but a third is missing, use conservation of mass to find it before you start the moles step. This is one of the reasons spec point 1.47 (conservation of mass) is a prerequisite for this one.

If you are given moles directly rather than masses, you can skip steps 1–3 and go straight to step 4 — divide every mole value by the smallest and clear any fractions.

Mathematical Skills

This spec point sits squarely on two mathematical skills:

  • 1a — Recognise and use expressions in decimal form. Masses are typically given as decimals (e.g. 4.8 g, 5.6 g); moles come out as decimals too (e.g. 0.10 mol, 0.075 mol).
  • 1c — Use ratios, fractions and percentages. Every problem ends with a simplest-whole-number ratio step.

`` Step A: moles of each substance = mass ÷ Mr Step B: divide every moles value by the smallest moles value Step C: if any answer ends in .5 → multiply the whole ratio × 2 if any answer ends in .33 → multiply the whole ratio × 3 Step D: the resulting integers are the stoichiometric coefficients ``

Keep at least 3 significant figures during the mole calculations. Rounding 0.0998 to 0.10 is fine; rounding 0.0998 to 0.1 and then dividing through is fine too — but rounding 0.0998 to 0 is catastrophic. Round only at the very end when you state the ratio as small whole numbers.
