Crude Oil

Spec 8.1 — Recall that hydrocarbons are compounds that contain carbon and hydrogen only. Spec 8.2 — Describe crude oil as a complex mixture of hydrocarbons, with carbon atoms in chains or rings, that is an important source of useful substances and a finite resource. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H), and on the equivalent Combined Science (1SC0) papers.

Key Definitions

What a hydrocarbon actually is

The definition of a hydrocarbon is one of the most heavily tested ideas in Section 8, and it is also one of the easiest marks to lose. A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen — nothing else. If a molecule contains oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, a halogen, or any other element, it is not a hydrocarbon, even if it also contains carbon and hydrogen.

For example:

  • CH₄ (methane) — hydrocarbon
  • C₈H₁₈ (octane) — hydrocarbon
  • C₂H₅OH (ethanol) — not a hydrocarbon (contains oxygen)
  • CH₃COOH (ethanoic acid) — not a hydrocarbon (contains oxygen)
  • CH₃Cl (chloromethane) — not a hydrocarbon (contains chlorine)

If you are asked "Define a hydrocarbon" or "What is a hydrocarbon?" the safest one-line answer is: "A compound containing only the elements carbon and hydrogen." The word only is what scores the mark — leaving it out is the most common reason answers are not credited.

What crude oil actually is

Crude oil is a thick, dark, treacle-like liquid pumped out of the ground. It is not a single substance — it is a complex mixture of many different hydrocarbons. "Complex" means there are very many different molecules in it, often hundreds or thousands of different hydrocarbons all jumbled together.

Because crude oil is a mixture and not a compound:

  • The hydrocarbons in it are not chemically bonded to each other
  • Each hydrocarbon keeps its own properties (its own boiling point, its own density)
  • The different hydrocarbons can therefore be physically separated from each other without breaking any chemical bonds — this is what makes fractional distillation possible

This is the conceptual key to the whole topic. If crude oil were a single compound, you could not separate it without a chemical reaction. Because it is a mixture, you can separate it using a physical process based on differences in boiling point.

Chains and rings

The carbon atoms inside crude-oil hydrocarbons are joined together in two main shapes:

  • Chains — long lines of carbon atoms, sometimes with branches. Most fuels from crude oil (petrol, diesel, kerosene) are chain hydrocarbons (the alkanes you will meet in 8.1.4).
  • Rings — carbon atoms joined in a closed ring. These are present in smaller amounts in crude oil.

You do not need to know the names, formulae or structures of any specific ring molecules at GCSE — the specification (8.2b) explicitly excludes this. You only need to know that they exist alongside the chain hydrocarbons. If an exam question asks about ring structures, it will be at the level of "crude oil contains hydrocarbons whose carbon atoms are arranged in chains or rings" — no more.

Why crude oil is so useful

Crude oil is described in the specification (8.2c) as "an important source of useful substances." There are two strands to that usefulness:

*1. Fuels*

When crude oil is separated by fractional distillation, the fractions are used as the everyday fuels that power modern life. The main fuels obtained are:

FractionTypical use
PetrolFuel for cars
Diesel oilFuel for cars, lorries, trains
KeroseneFuel for aircraft
Heavy fuel oilFuel for ships and for some power stations
LPG (refinery gases)Bottled gas for cooking and heating

You will need to be confident on these uses for the 1-mark "state a use" questions. Be precise: kerosene is for aircraft, not vague "vehicles." Diesel is for cars and lorries, not "petrol-driven cars."

*2. Feedstock for the petrochemical industry*

The same crude-oil fractions also act as raw starting materials (feedstocks) that the chemical industry uses to make a huge range of products. Examples include:

  • Plastics (poly(ethene), poly(propene), PVC)
  • Solvents (used in paints and adhesives)
  • Detergents (washing powders and washing-up liquids)
  • Lubricants (engine oils, greases)
  • Medicines (many pharmaceutical molecules start from crude-oil hydrocarbons)

A key revision tip: if a question asks you to give two uses of crude oil, give one fuel and one petrochemical product (e.g. "fuel for cars" + "raw material to make plastics"). Two different fuels will only score one of the two marks.

Why crude oil is a finite resource

Crude oil was formed over hundreds of millions of years from the remains of ancient marine organisms (mostly plankton) that died and were buried under layers of sediment. Heat and pressure underground gradually converted those remains into the hydrocarbon mixture we now extract.

Because new crude oil is forming far more slowly than we are using it up, the supply will eventually run out. That is what the specification means by finite. The contrast you should be ready to draw is with renewable resources (e.g. bioethanol made from sugarcane), which can be replaced as quickly as they are used.

Resource typeDefinitionExamples
Finite (non-renewable)Being used up faster than it is being madeCrude oil, natural gas, coal
RenewableCan be replaced as quickly as it is usedBioethanol, biodiesel, wood

A common exam trap: "non-renewable" and "finite" are treated as equivalent here. Both score the mark.

Drawing the link between hydrocarbons and the rest of Section 8

Spec point 8.1.1.1 is the gateway to almost everything else in Section 8. The reason this is the first spec point in the section is that it sets up the vocabulary you need to discuss fractional distillation (8.1.2), combustion (8.1.3), the alkanes (8.1.4) and cracking (8.1.5). If you are shaky on the definition of a hydrocarbon, you will lose marks all the way through the topic — not just here.

A useful mental model:

  • Crude oil = the mixture (raw material, finite)
  • Hydrocarbons = the molecules inside the mixture (carbon + hydrogen only)
  • Fractions = groups of similar-sized hydrocarbons separated by boiling point
  • Fuels and petrochemicals = the end uses of those fractions

8.1.2.1 Fractional Distillation of Crude Oil F+H ▼

Fractional Distillation of Crude Oil

Spec point 8.3: Describe and explain the separation of crude oil into simpler, more useful mixtures by the process of fractional distillation. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 2 (Separate Chemistry and Combined Science).

Crude oil straight out of the ground is not useful as a fuel — it is a thick, dark, sticky mixture of hundreds of different hydrocarbons, with chain lengths ranging from very small (one or two carbons) to very large (more than seventy carbons). To turn it into the petrol, diesel, jet fuel, and bitumen that society actually uses, the mixture must be split into groups of hydrocarbons with similar chain lengths. The process that does this is fractional distillation, carried out in a tall vertical column at an oil refinery.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Fractional distillationA physical separation process that splits a liquid mixture into groups of components (fractions) based on differences in boiling point
FractionA group of hydrocarbons collected together from the column because they have similar chain lengths and therefore similar boiling points
HydrocarbonA compound containing only hydrogen and carbon atoms
Boiling pointThe temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas (or vice versa) at atmospheric pressure
Fractionating columnThe tall industrial tower in which crude oil is separated; it is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top
CondenseTo change state from gas to liquid; this happens when a hydrocarbon vapour reaches a part of the column that is below its boiling point

The Process Step by Step

Fractional distillation of crude oil is a continuous industrial process — once the column is running, crude oil flows in and fractions flow out without stopping. The steps are:

  1. Heating the crude oil. Crude oil is heated in a furnace to around 350 °C so that almost all of it evaporates and turns into a hot mixture of hydrocarbon vapours. Only the very longest-chain hydrocarbons (those that make up bitumen) stay as a liquid.
  2. Vapours enter the fractionating column. The hot vapour passes into the bottom of a tall vertical column. The column is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top, so there is a temperature gradient running up the tower.
  3. Vapours rise up the column. As the vapours rise, they cool down. Each hydrocarbon keeps rising until it reaches a level of the column where the temperature is just below its own boiling point.
  4. Vapours condense at different heights. When a hydrocarbon reaches a level cooler than its boiling point, it condenses into a liquid and is collected on a tray inside the column at that height.
  5. Fractions are piped out. Each tray has an outlet pipe that takes the collected liquid (the fraction) out of the column for further processing or use.
  6. Bitumen leaves the bottom; gases leave the top. Very long-chain hydrocarbons (bitumen) never evaporate and are tapped off as a thick liquid from the bottom. Very short-chain hydrocarbons (refinery gases) never condense and leave the top of the column as gases.

Why Fractional Distillation Works — Linking Chain Length, Intermolecular Forces, and Boiling Point

The whole process depends on one underlying idea: the longer the hydrocarbon chain, the higher its boiling point.

Hydrocarbon molecules are held to one another by weak intermolecular forces. Longer molecules have more points of contact and therefore stronger overall intermolecular forces between them. More energy is needed to overcome those forces and separate the molecules into the gas phase, so longer hydrocarbons boil at higher temperatures.

This is what creates the separation in the column:

  • Long-chain hydrocarbons (high boiling points) cannot stay as vapour for long. As soon as they rise a little way up the column and cool down slightly, they reach their boiling point and condense. They are collected near the bottom, where it is still hot.
  • Short-chain hydrocarbons (low boiling points) stay as vapour all the way up the column because they need to cool down to a much lower temperature before they will condense. They are collected near the top, where it is coolest, or leave as gases.
  • Medium-chain hydrocarbons condense at intermediate heights, depending on their chain length.

So the temperature gradient inside the column — hot at the bottom, cool at the top — combined with the steady rise in boiling point with chain length, sorts the hydrocarbons by size automatically.

How Properties Change Up and Down the Column

The fractions produced have predictable property trends that follow directly from chain length. You should be able to state and use these in the exam.

PropertyLong chains (bottom of column)Short chains (top of column)
Chain lengthMany carbons (e.g. 20–70+)Few carbons (e.g. 1–10)
Boiling pointHighLow
Viscosity (thickness)High — thick, stickyLow — runny, easily flows
VolatilityLow — does not evaporate easilyHigh — evaporates easily
FlammabilityLow — hard to igniteHigh — ignites easily
Colour of flame / smokeSmoky, sooty flameClean flame
Position in columnBottom (hottest)Top (coolest)

These trends are the reason different fractions are used for different jobs: bitumen is so viscous and non-volatile that it makes a good road surface, while petrol and refinery gases are volatile and flammable enough to be useful fuels.

Worked Example — Predicting Where a Hydrocarbon Condenses

A hydrocarbon has a boiling point of 150 °C. Where in the fractionating column would you expect it to condense, compared with a hydrocarbon of boiling point 30 °C?

Step 1: Higher boiling point means longer chain length and stronger intermolecular forces. Step 2: The 150 °C hydrocarbon will condense as soon as the column temperature drops just below 150 °C. This happens fairly low down the column where it is still hot. Step 3: The 30 °C hydrocarbon will stay as a vapour until it has risen near the top of the column where the temperature has dropped close to room temperature. Conclusion: The 150 °C hydrocarbon condenses near the bottom; the 30 °C hydrocarbon condenses near the top (or leaves as a refinery gas if the top is still warmer than 30 °C).

Why It Is a Physical Process, Not a Chemical One

Fractional distillation does not break or make any chemical bonds inside the hydrocarbon molecules. The molecules going into the column are exactly the same molecules coming out the other side — they have just been sorted by size. This means:

  • No new substances are made; only existing substances are separated.
  • The process is reversible in principle (you could re-mix the fractions back into crude oil).
  • It relies on physical properties (boiling point) rather than chemical reactions.

This is an important distinction from cracking, which is a chemical process where long-chain hydrocarbons are broken into shorter ones.

How to Score Full Marks on a "Describe and Explain" Question

If you see a 3- or 4-mark question that says "Describe and explain how crude oil is separated by fractional distillation", your answer needs to hit these points:

  1. Crude oil is heated so that it evaporates and becomes a mixture of hydrocarbon vapours.
  2. The vapours enter a fractionating column that is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top.
  3. The vapours rise up the column and cool down as they go.
  4. Each hydrocarbon condenses (turns from gas to liquid) when it reaches a part of the column below its own boiling point.
  5. Long-chain hydrocarbons have high boiling points and condense near the bottom; short-chain hydrocarbons have low boiling points and condense near the top (or leave as gases).
  6. The liquid fractions are collected on trays at different heights and piped out of the column.

Hitting points (3), (4), and (5) — the link between temperature gradient, boiling point, and chain length — is what turns a "describe" answer into an "explain" answer and earns the full marks.

8.1.2.2 The Main Fractions F+H ▼

The Main Fractions

Spec 8.4: Recall the names and uses of the following fractions: gases (domestic heating and cooking), petrol (fuel for cars), kerosene (fuel for aircraft), diesel oil (fuel for some cars and trains), fuel oil (fuel for large ships and in some power stations), and bitumen (to surface roads and roofs). Foundation + Higher · Separate (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 2.

Crude oil on its own is a thick, dark, sticky liquid that is almost useless. Its value comes from the fact that it is a mixture of many different hydrocarbons, and these hydrocarbons can be separated by fractional distillation into groups (called fractions) with similar boiling points and similar chain lengths. Each fraction has its own combination of properties — viscosity, volatility, flammability — that makes it suited to a particular job. This spec point is straightforward recall, but it is examined on almost every Paper 2, often as a matching exercise or short "state the use" question. Getting the pairings the wrong way round (petrol vs diesel is the classic slip) is the single most common error.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
FractionA group of hydrocarbons from crude oil with similar chain lengths and similar boiling points, separated by fractional distillation
HydrocarbonA compound containing only hydrogen and carbon atoms
VolatilityHow easily a liquid turns into a vapour; high-volatility liquids evaporate readily at low temperatures
ViscosityHow thick or "runny" a liquid is; viscous liquids flow slowly
FlammabilityHow easily a substance catches fire and burns
Boiling pointThe temperature at which a liquid turns into a gas

The Six Fractions and Their Uses

You must be able to recall the name of each fraction and its use — both directions. Examiners ask the question both ways: sometimes giving you the fraction and asking for the use, sometimes giving the use and asking for the fraction.

FractionMain UseWhy That Use?
Gases (refinery gases / LPG)Domestic heating and cookingVery short hydrocarbon chains (1–4 carbons), low boiling point, gaseous at room temperature, extremely flammable — burn cleanly with a controllable flame, easy to pipe into homes
PetrolFuel for carsShort chains (around 5–10 carbons), low boiling point, highly volatile (vaporises readily inside an engine cylinder so it ignites quickly with a spark), very flammable
KeroseneFuel for aircraftMedium chain length (around 10–16 carbons), still fairly volatile and flammable, but burns more steadily than petrol — important at altitude where reliable ignition matters
Diesel oilFuel for some cars and trainsLonger chains (around 14–20 carbons), higher boiling point, less volatile than petrol; releases a lot of energy per litre when compressed and ignited
Fuel oilFuel for large ships and in some power stationsLong chains, high boiling point, viscous, less flammable; cheap, energy-dense, and well-suited to very large slow-burning engines and industrial boilers
BitumenTo surface roads and roofsVery long chains (over 70 carbons typically), very high boiling point, almost solid at room temperature, very viscous, non-volatile; spreads as a thick waterproof layer that sets hard

Order on the fractionating column (top to bottom): gases → petrol → kerosene → diesel oil → fuel oil → bitumen. The further down the column a fraction is collected, the longer its molecules, the higher its boiling point, and the more viscous and less flammable it becomes.

Why Each Fraction Suits Its Use

*Gases — domestic heating and cooking*

Short hydrocarbons such as methane, ethane, propane, and butane are gaseous at room temperature. They mix with air easily, ignite quickly with a small spark or pilot light, and burn cleanly. Because they can be piped (mains gas) or bottled (LPG / Calor gas) under modest pressure, they are convenient for cooking hobs, central-heating boilers, and gas fires.

*Petrol — fuel for cars*

A petrol engine works by injecting a fine spray of petrol and air into a cylinder, then igniting the mixture with a spark plug. This only works if the fuel vaporises easily — which petrol does because its molecules are short and its boiling point is low. The high flammability is exactly what an internal-combustion engine needs for a rapid, controlled explosion.

*Kerosene — fuel for aircraft*

Aircraft engines (jet turbines) need a fuel that burns steadily and reliably, including at high altitude where temperatures are very low. Kerosene's chain length sits between petrol and diesel — it is volatile enough to atomise and ignite, but its boiling point is high enough that it does not vaporise prematurely or freeze in flight. It also has a high energy density, which matters when an aircraft has to carry every kilogram of fuel into the air.

*Diesel oil — fuel for some cars and trains*

Diesel engines compress the fuel-air mixture so much that it ignites without a spark. This needs a fuel that does not ignite too readily on its own at low pressure — so a less volatile fuel than petrol is ideal. Diesel's longer chains release more energy per litre, which is why diesel engines are typically more efficient than petrol engines and are favoured for heavy vehicles and trains.

*Fuel oil — fuel for large ships and in some power stations*

Fuel oil is cheap, energy-dense, and burns slowly. Large marine engines and power-station boilers can handle a viscous, less-volatile fuel because they pre-heat it before combustion. Cost matters far more than convenience on this scale, so a long-chain fraction that would be useless in a car is ideal in a ship or generator.

*Bitumen — to surface roads and roofs*

Bitumen is so viscous it behaves like a soft solid at room temperature. It is not used as a fuel because its molecules are too long to vaporise and burn cleanly. Instead, it is used as a waterproof binder: mixed with stone chippings it becomes tarmac for road surfaces, and spread on roofs it forms a flexible waterproof layer.

How the Trends Link to the Uses

The reason this spec point makes sense (rather than being six random facts to memorise) is that all six uses follow from the same underlying chain-length trend:

PropertyShort chains (top of column)Long chains (bottom of column)
Boiling pointLowHigh
VolatilityHighLow
ViscosityLow (runny)High (thick)
FlammabilityHighLow
State at room temperatureGas or thin liquidThick liquid or near-solid

So the gases (very short chains) make excellent domestic fuels because they ignite easily and mix with air. As you move down — petrol, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil — the chains get longer, the fuel becomes less volatile but more energy-dense per litre, and you move from light vehicles to heavy ones to ships and power stations. By the time you reach bitumen, the chains are too long for the fraction to be useful as a fuel at all, so its use shifts to surfacing roads and roofs.

If you remember this trend, you can usually reason your way to the correct use even if you forget the pairing.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Calling diesel oil "petrol for cars" — diesel and petrol are different fractions with different chain lengths and different engine types.
  • Pairing kerosene with cars or trains — kerosene is for aircraft.
  • Putting the fractions in the wrong order on the column — gases at the top, bitumen at the bottom.
  • Saying bitumen is used as a fuel — it is used for road surfacing and roofing, not combustion.
  • Confusing fuel oil and diesel — fuel oil goes into large ships and power stations, diesel goes into cars and trains.
  • Writing "kerosine" or "kerosine oil" — the spec uses kerosene.

  1. Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation into six main fractions you must be able to name and pair with their uses.
  2. Gases (refinery gases / LPG) are used for domestic heating and cooking — they are short-chain, very volatile, and burn cleanly.
  3. Petrol is used as fuel for cars — short-chain, highly volatile, ignites readily with a spark.

  5. Diesel oil is used as fuel for some cars and trains — longer chains, less volatile than petrol, ignites under compression.

8.1.2.3 Trends in Properties F+H ▼

Trends in Properties

Spec 8.5 — Explain how hydrocarbons in different fractions differ in number of carbon/hydrogen atoms, boiling point, ease of ignition and viscosity; and that fractions are mostly members of the alkane homologous series. Foundation + Higher tier, Paper 2, both 1CH0 and 1SC0.

Crude oil is a mixture of hundreds of different hydrocarbons. Fractional distillation sorts them into fractions — groups of hydrocarbons whose molecules have a similar number of carbon atoms and therefore similar boiling points. The fractions you meet at GCSE (refinery gases, gasoline/petrol, kerosene, diesel oil, fuel oil, bitumen) form a clear series. As you move down the fractionating column, the molecules get bigger, and a predictable set of physical properties changes with them.

The single chemical idea that drives all four trends is the strength of intermolecular forces between hydrocarbon molecules. Longer carbon chains have more electrons and a greater surface area in contact with neighbouring molecules. That gives stronger intermolecular forces, and stronger intermolecular forces take more energy to overcome.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
FractionA group of hydrocarbons collected together from crude oil that have similar boiling points and a similar number of carbon atoms
HydrocarbonA compound containing only the elements carbon and hydrogen
AlkaneA saturated hydrocarbon with the general formula C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub>
Homologous seriesA family of compounds with the same general formula, similar chemical properties and a gradual trend in physical properties

Most fractions are alkanes

Crude oil is mostly a mixture of alkanes. Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons — every carbon-carbon bond is a single covalent bond. They have the general formula C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub>, meaning if you know the number of carbon atoms in an alkane molecule, you can work out the number of hydrogens.

The four trends summarised

The explanation always loops back to the same idea: longer chain → stronger intermolecular forces → harder to vaporise / harder to ignite / thicker liquid.

Why intermolecular forces drive the trends

The covalent bonds inside a hydrocarbon molecule (C-C and C-H bonds) are strong. But when you boil a hydrocarbon you don't break those bonds — you only have to overcome the much weaker intermolecular forces between separate molecules.

Two factors make these intermolecular forces stronger in longer chains:

  1. Number of electrons. Each extra carbon and hydrogen brings extra electrons. More electrons mean larger temporary fluctuations in electron density, which produce stronger induced attractions between neighbouring molecules.
  2. Surface contact. A long, straight molecule lies alongside its neighbour over a much greater area than a short, compact one. More contact means more of those weak attractions adding up.

This is why even a small change in chain length produces a noticeable change in boiling point, and a large change in chain length (say, from C<sub>4</sub> butane to C<sub>40</sub> bitumen) takes you all the way from a gas at room temperature to a thick, black solid.

Worked example — predicting properties of an unknown fraction

A fraction is collected at a temperature where the hydrocarbons contain about 18 carbon atoms per molecule.

  • Likely fraction: Diesel oil (C<sub>14</sub>–C<sub>20</sub>).
  • Approximate formula range: Around C<sub>18</sub>H<sub>38</sub> using C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub>.
  • Boiling point: Relatively high — diesel boils at around 250–350 °C, much higher than petrol.
  • Ease of ignition: Lower than petrol. Diesel does not vaporise as easily, so it does not catch fire from a single spark like petrol does; diesel engines compress the fuel to make it ignite.
  • Viscosity: Higher than petrol but lower than fuel oil. Diesel feels "oilier" than petrol when poured.

Notice how every prediction comes from the same logical chain: longer molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → property follows the trend.

Comparing two fractions — exam technique

A common exam style is to give you a table of two fractions (e.g. petrol vs diesel, or kerosene vs diesel) and ask you to compare their properties or explain a difference.

A high-mark answer always has two parts:

  1. State the property and the direction of the difference (e.g. "diesel has a higher boiling point than petrol").
  2. Link it to chain length and intermolecular forces (e.g. "because diesel molecules have longer carbon chains, the intermolecular forces between them are stronger, so more energy is needed to separate them into a gas").

If a question gives you two lines on the answer sheet (one for each fraction), use them as the structure tells you to — write one fraction's property on the first line and the comparison/contrast on the second line. Do not restate the same idea twice.

8.1.3.1 Homologous Series F+H ▼

Homologous Series

Spec point 8.6 — Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

A homologous series is a family of organic compounds that share a common structural pattern. You need to be able to explain what makes a set of molecules a homologous series, recognise neighbouring members, and predict how their physical and chemical properties change as you move up the series. The examples used at GCSE are almost always the alkanes (and sometimes the alkenes and alcohols), but the four defining features apply to every homologous series.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Homologous seriesA family of organic compounds that share the same general formula, differ by CH₂ between neighbouring members, show a gradual change in physical properties, and have similar chemical properties
General formulaA formula in terms of n (the number of carbon atoms) that fits every member of the series — e.g. CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for alkanes
Molecular formulaThe actual number of each type of atom in one molecule of a specific compound — e.g. C₃H₈ for propane
Neighbouring membersTwo compounds adjacent to each other in a homologous series, whose molecular formulae differ by exactly one CH₂ unit
Functional groupThe reactive part of a molecule that gives a homologous series its characteristic chemical reactions (e.g. C=C in alkenes, –OH in alcohols)
HydrocarbonA compound that contains only hydrogen and carbon atoms

The four defining features

Every homologous series shows all four of the following features. In an exam question that asks you to explain why two compounds belong to the same homologous series, you should be picking from this list.

*1. Same general formula*

Every member of a homologous series fits a single formula in terms of n, where n is the number of carbon atoms. For alkanes, n = 1 gives methane (CH₄), n = 2 gives ethane (C₂H₆), n = 3 gives propane (C₃H₈), and so on. The general formula is what allows you to write the molecular formula of any member of the series once you know n, even for compounds you have never seen before.

*2. Differ by CH₂ between neighbouring members*

If you take two adjacent members of a homologous series and compare their molecular formulae, you will always find a difference of one carbon atom and two hydrogen atoms — that is, one CH₂ unit. Compare methane (CH₄) with ethane (C₂H₆): the difference is C₁H₂, which is CH₂. Compare propane (C₃H₈) with butane (C₄H₁₀): the difference is again CH₂. This is not a coincidence; it is a direct consequence of the general formula.

*3. Gradual variation in physical properties*

As you move up a homologous series (more carbon atoms), physical properties change in a predictable, gradual way. Boiling point is the classic example: methane boils at –162 °C and is a gas at room temperature, but as you go up the alkanes the boiling points rise smoothly until, by the time you reach C₁₈H₃₈, the compound is a solid at room temperature. Density, viscosity, and flammability also change gradually. The change is gradual, not sudden — there is no point at which the properties jump.

*4. Similar chemical properties*

Every member of a homologous series reacts in essentially the same way, because every member contains the same functional group (or, for alkanes, the same overall single-bonded saturated hydrocarbon framework). All alkanes undergo complete and incomplete combustion in the same way. All alkenes decolourise bromine water. All alcohols react with sodium to release hydrogen. The rate and exact conditions may shift slightly along the series, but the type of reaction is the same.

Why CH₂ — the underlying reason

This is the part that students who simply memorise the four bullet points tend to miss. You are not just learning a rule; the rule comes from how the general formula is constructed.

Take the alkanes, general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. If you have a member with n carbon atoms, it has (2n + 2) hydrogen atoms. The next member has (n + 1) carbon atoms and 2(n + 1) + 2 = (2n + 4) hydrogen atoms. Subtract: the next member has one more carbon and two more hydrogens — exactly one CH₂ unit more. The same algebra works for alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ) and alcohols (CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH).

That is why the "differ by CH₂" rule is a consequence of having a shared general formula, not an independent fact. In exam answers, mentioning both points scores both marks because the spec lists them separately, but the underlying chemistry is one idea.

The alkanes — worked comparison table

The alkane homologous series is the example you will almost certainly meet in an exam. Learn the first five or six members thoroughly.

  • Every neighbouring pair differs by CH₂ in molecular formula. Ethane (C₂H₆) → propane (C₃H₈) adds CH₂. Pentane (C₅H₁₂) → hexane (C₆H₁₄) adds CH₂. The pattern is exact and consistent.
  • Boiling point rises steadily as n increases. The rise is not linear (it gets smaller per step as the series goes up), but it is always in the same direction.
  • The state at room temperature changes from gas to liquid between butane and pentane. There is no single member where it "switches"; it is just that as the boiling point passes through 20–25 °C, the next member is liquid.

Why boiling point rises along the series

This is universal physical chemistry, not a spec point you have to memorise — but it makes the trend much easier to remember.

Bigger alkane molecules have more electrons. More electrons mean stronger temporary (induced) dipole–dipole interactions between molecules — sometimes called London dispersion forces. The intermolecular forces are not chemical bonds; they are weak attractions between separate molecules. Bigger molecules → stronger intermolecular forces → more energy needed to separate them into a gas → higher boiling point.

The covalent C–C and C–H bonds inside the molecule are not broken when an alkane boils. Only the intermolecular forces between molecules are overcome. This is a common student trap — boiling does not "break the molecule".

Worked example — predicting a molecular formula

Question: Decane is the 10th member of the alkane homologous series. Use the general formula to deduce its molecular formula.

Step 1. Identify the general formula of the alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂

Step 2. Identify n. Decane is the 10th alkane, so n = 10.

Step 3. Substitute into the general formula:

  • Number of carbon atoms = n = 10
  • Number of hydrogen atoms = 2n + 2 = (2 × 10) + 2 = 22

*Step 4.** Write the molecular formula: **C₁₀H₂₂*

Check: Decane should differ from nonane (C₉H₂₀) by one CH₂ unit. C₁₀H₂₂ − C₉H₂₀ = CH₂. Correct.

Worked example — explaining "neighbouring members"

Question: Butane has molecular formula C₄H₁₀. Pentane has molecular formula C₅H₁₂. Explain, using these formulae, why butane and pentane are neighbouring members of the same homologous series. (2 marks)

*Step 1.** Compare the molecular formulae. Pentane has one more carbon and two more hydrogen atoms than butane. The difference is CH₂. *(1 mark)

Step 2. Show that both fit the alkane general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂:

  • Butane: n = 4, so 2n + 2 = 10. C₄H₁₀ fits.
  • Pentane: n = 5, so 2n + 2 = 12. C₅H₁₂ fits.

Both members fit the same general formula and they differ by exactly CH₂, so they are neighbouring members of the same homologous series. (1 mark)

The examiner needs to see the CH₂ difference and (ideally) the shared general formula explicitly stated using the formulae given. 8.1.4.1 Complete Combustion F+H ▼

Complete Combustion

Spec 8.7: Describe the complete combustion of hydrocarbon fuels as a reaction in which (a) carbon dioxide and water are produced; (b) energy is given out. Foundation + Higher tier, both 1CH0 and 1SC0, Paper 2.

Complete combustion is one of the most important reactions in the whole of GCSE Chemistry because it sits at the heart of how the world gets its energy. Every time petrol burns in a car engine, natural gas burns in a boiler, or a Bunsen burner is lit in the lab on its non-luminous "blue" flame, the same family of reaction is happening: a hydrocarbon is reacting with plenty of oxygen, releasing energy, and producing carbon dioxide and water as the only carbon- and hydrogen-containing products.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
HydrocarbonA compound made of carbon and hydrogen atoms only
CombustionA reaction in which a substance reacts with oxygen and releases energy
Complete combustionCombustion of a hydrocarbon in a plentiful (excess) supply of oxygen, producing carbon dioxide and water as the only products
Exothermic reactionA reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, usually as heat (the surroundings get hotter)
FuelA substance that releases useful energy when it burns
OxidationA reaction in which a substance gains oxygen (a useful way of thinking about combustion at GCSE)

What complete combustion actually is

A hydrocarbon contains only two elements: carbon and hydrogen. When it burns in a plentiful supply of oxygen:

  • the carbon atoms are fully oxidised to carbon dioxide (CO₂)
  • the hydrogen atoms are fully oxidised to water (H₂O)
  • a large amount of energy is released as heat (and usually light)

That is the entire story of the spec point. The two products — carbon dioxide and water — and the fact that energy is given out are the three facts that must be in your answer to a "describe complete combustion" question.

A general word equation for the complete combustion of any hydrocarbon is therefore:

*hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water*

This works whether the hydrocarbon is methane in natural gas, butane in a camping stove, octane in petrol, or an alkene such as ethene. The fuel changes; the products do not.

Why both products are formed

A common student error is to think that only carbon dioxide is produced "because the fuel is a hydrocarbon and carbon makes CO₂". You must include water as a product as well — the hydrogen atoms in the fuel have to go somewhere, and in an oxygen-rich flame they bond with oxygen to form H₂O. If a flame is hot enough, the water leaves as steam (water vapour), which is why you often see condensation forming on a cold surface held above a Bunsen flame.

Why energy is given out (exothermic)

Combustion is exothermic. In any reaction, energy is needed to break the bonds in the reactants and energy is released when new bonds form in the products. In complete combustion:

  • the bonds being made — the C=O bonds in CO₂ and the O–H bonds in H₂O — are stronger (release more energy) than the bonds being broken — the C–C and C–H bonds in the fuel and the O=O bonds in oxygen
  • because more energy is released by forming bonds than is taken in by breaking them, the overall reaction transfers energy to the surroundings as heat

That is the underlying reason hydrocarbons are useful as fuels: their combustion releases a lot of energy per gram, which can be converted to heat, electricity or motion.

Worked example 1 — methane (natural gas)

Methane (CH₄) is the main component of natural gas, used in domestic boilers and gas hobs. Its complete combustion is the cleanest of all the alkanes because methane has the highest H : C ratio.

Word equation:

*methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water*

Balanced symbol equation:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

How the balancing works:

  1. Start by writing the unbalanced equation: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  2. Carbon: 1 C on the left in CH₄, 1 C on the right in CO₂ — carbon is balanced.
  3. Hydrogen: 4 H on the left in CH₄, only 2 H on the right in H₂O. Put a 2 in front of H₂O → CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Now 4 H on each side.
  4. Oxygen: count the O atoms on the right — 2 (from CO₂) + 2 (from 2H₂O) = 4 O. On the left there are only 2 O in O₂, so put a 2 in front of O₂ → CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
  5. Check each element one final time: C 1=1, H 4=4, O 4=4. Balanced.

Worked example 2 — butane (camping gas / lighters)

Butane (C₄H₁₀) is used in disposable lighters and portable camping stoves. It has more carbon and hydrogen atoms per molecule than methane, so the balanced equation needs larger numbers.

Word equation:

*butane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water*

Balanced symbol equation:

2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O

How the balancing works:

  1. Unbalanced: C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  2. Carbon: 4 C on the left → put 4 in front of CO₂ → C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → 4CO₂ + H₂O.
  3. Hydrogen: 10 H on the left → put 5 in front of H₂O → C₄H₁₀ + O₂ → 4CO₂ + 5H₂O. Now 10 H on each side.
  4. Oxygen: count O on the right — 8 (from 4CO₂) + 5 (from 5H₂O) = 13 O atoms. But O₂ is a diatomic molecule, so 13 atoms would mean "6.5 O₂", which is not allowed.
  5. Double everything so the oxygens come out whole: 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O.
  6. Final check: C 8=8, H 20=20, O 26=26. Balanced.

This "double everything" trick is the standard way to balance complete combustion of an alkane with an odd number of hydrogen pairs (propane, butane, pentane, etc.).

Worked example 3 — ethene (an alkene)

Ethene (C₂H₄) is an alkene. Although alkenes are not normally used as fuels (they are more valuable as feedstocks for polymers), they still undergo complete combustion in the same way.

Balanced symbol equation:

C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O

Quick balance check: C 2=2, H 4=4, O 6=6.

Ethene appears in past papers because writing its complete-combustion equation tests two skills at once: spotting that it is still a hydrocarbon (so the products are still CO₂ and H₂O), and balancing an equation where the formula is C₂H₄, not C₂H₆.

State symbols

In a typical exam answer at GCSE, state symbols are usually not required unless the question specifically asks for them. If you do add them:

  • the hydrocarbon may be gas, liquid or solid depending on chain length — methane is (g), petrol/octane is (l) at room temperature
  • oxygen is (g)
  • carbon dioxide is (g)
  • water is (g) at the temperature of the flame, but typically written as (l) in standard-conditions equations

Examples:

CH₄ (g) + 2O₂ (g) → CO₂ (g) + 2H₂O (l)

Conditions for complete combustion

Complete combustion only happens when there is a plentiful (excess) supply of oxygen. In practice this means a good air supply at the flame — for example, opening the air-hole of a Bunsen burner gives a roaring blue flame which represents complete combustion, while closing it gives a yellow luminous flame and soot, which represents incomplete combustion (see 8.1.4.2 Incomplete Combustion). Watch for these visual clues in exam questions:

  • blue, non-luminous flame → plenty of oxygen → complete combustion
  • yellow, sooty flame → limited oxygen → incomplete combustion

8.1.4.2 Incomplete Combustion F+H ▼

Incomplete Combustion

Spec 8.8: Explain why the incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons can produce carbon and carbon monoxide. Spec 8.9: Explain how carbon monoxide behaves as a toxic gas. Spec 8.10: Describe the problems caused by incomplete combustion producing carbon monoxide and soot in appliances that use carbon compounds as fuels.

This SP is examined at Foundation and Higher tier, on Paper 2 of both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CombustionAn exothermic reaction in which a fuel reacts with oxygen, releasing energy as heat (and usually light)
Complete combustionCombustion in a plentiful supply of oxygen, producing only carbon dioxide and water
Incomplete combustionCombustion in a limited supply of oxygen, producing carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot) alongside, or in place of, carbon dioxide
HydrocarbonA compound containing only carbon and hydrogen atoms
Carbon monoxide (CO)A colourless, odourless, toxic gas produced when a fuel is burned in insufficient oxygen
SootFine particles of solid carbon produced when a hydrocarbon burns in a very limited oxygen supply
HaemoglobinThe iron-containing protein in red blood cells that normally binds and transports oxygen around the body

Why incomplete combustion happens

When a hydrocarbon burns, every carbon atom in the fuel needs to combine with oxygen. Each carbon atom needs two oxygen atoms to be fully oxidised to carbon dioxide (CO₂), and each pair of hydrogen atoms needs one oxygen atom to form water (H₂O).

If the oxygen supply is plentiful, every carbon atom gets the two oxygen atoms it needs and complete combustion occurs. If the oxygen supply is limited, there is not enough oxygen to fully oxidise every carbon atom. The reaction then produces one of two alternative carbon-containing products:

  • Carbon monoxide (CO) — each carbon atom only finds one oxygen atom instead of two, so it is only partly oxidised
  • Carbon (C) — solid carbon particles ("soot"), formed when there is so little oxygen that some carbon atoms are not oxidised at all

In a real flame all three products (CO₂, CO and C) can be formed at the same time, in different proportions, depending on how much oxygen is reaching the reaction zone. Hydrogen is much easier to oxidise than carbon, so water vapour is still produced even when the oxygen supply is too poor to fully oxidise the carbon.

Balanced equations for incomplete combustion

Two parallel "incomplete" pathways exist. You should be confident writing equations for both, using the fuel given to you in the question.

*The CO pathway — all carbon atoms become carbon monoxide*

For methane:

2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O

For propane (C₃H₈):

2C₃H₈ + 7O₂ → 6CO + 8H₂O

For heptane (C₇H₁₆), where every carbon atom forms carbon monoxide:

2C₇H₁₆ + 15O₂ → 14CO + 16H₂O

*The C (soot) pathway — all carbon atoms become solid carbon*

For methane:

CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O

For propane:

C₃H₈ + 2O₂ → 3C + 4H₂O

For heptane:

C₇H₁₆ + 4O₂ → 7C + 8H₂O

Notice in every case that the hydrogen still goes to water — only the carbon "destination" changes between CO₂, CO and C. The coefficient on O₂ falls as the fuel is "starved" of oxygen: complete combustion of heptane needs 11 O₂; CO formation only needs 7½ (or 15 when written as whole numbers); pure soot formation only needs 4.

Balancing tip: balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen last. If your O₂ coefficient comes out as a fraction, double everything to give whole-number coefficients (this is what gives the "2C₇H₁₆ … 15O₂" style in the CO equation above).

Why carbon monoxide is toxic

Oxygen is normally carried around your body by haemoglobin in your red blood cells. Oxygen molecules bind reversibly to the iron centre in haemoglobin in the lungs, are carried in the bloodstream, and are released to respiring tissues.

Carbon monoxide is dangerous because it binds to the same site on haemoglobin that oxygen normally uses — and it binds far more strongly than oxygen does. Once CO is attached, it does not release easily, so that haemoglobin molecule can no longer pick up and deliver oxygen. As more CO enters the blood, more haemoglobin is locked out of action.

The result is that the body's cells are starved of oxygen, even though the person is still breathing. Symptoms progress from headache, dizziness and confusion to unconsciousness and, at high enough exposures, death. Because CO is colourless and odourless, victims often have no warning — this is the reason carbon monoxide is sometimes called "the silent killer" and why CO alarms are fitted near gas boilers and wood-burning stoves.

In an exam, the standard 2-mark explanation should make two clear points:

  1. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells (in place of oxygen)
  2. This reduces the amount of oxygen the blood can carry to the body's cells / tissues

Problems caused by incomplete combustion in appliances

Domestic appliances that burn carbon-based fuels — gas boilers, gas hobs, gas fires, wood-burning stoves, oil-fired heaters, paraffin lamps — all rely on a steady supply of air. If the air supply is blocked (dust, a blocked flue, a poorly maintained burner) or if the appliance is in a poorly ventilated room, the flame becomes oxygen-starved and starts to combust incompletely. Three connected problems then follow:

*1. Production of carbon monoxide (a toxic gas)*

A faulty gas boiler that has slipped into incomplete combustion produces CO that leaks into the room. Because CO is colourless and odourless, occupants do not realise they are inhaling it. This is the cause of accidental CO poisoning every year and is the reason annual servicing of gas appliances and the use of CO detectors are recommended.

*2. Deposition of soot inside the appliance*

The solid carbon particles (soot) settle on the inside surfaces of the burner, heat exchanger, flue and chimney. Soot is a black, sooty powder that:

  • Blocks the flow of gases, restricting the air supply further and making incomplete combustion even worse (a vicious cycle)
  • Coats heat exchangers, acting as a thermal insulator and reducing the efficiency of heat transfer to the room or the water being heated — so the appliance burns more fuel for the same warmth
  • Builds up in flues and chimneys, where it can eventually catch fire (a chimney fire)

*3. Less energy released per mole of fuel*

Complete combustion fully oxidises every carbon atom and releases the maximum amount of energy. Incomplete combustion leaves carbon partially oxidised (as CO) or unoxidised (as C), so less energy is given out per mole of fuel burned. The appliance becomes less efficient: more fuel is needed to produce the same amount of heat, which is wasteful and costs more money.

Worked example: balancing an incomplete combustion equation

Question: Write the balanced equation for the incomplete combustion of octane (C₈H₁₈) where every carbon atom forms carbon monoxide.

*Step 1 — Write the unbalanced skeleton with the correct products.*

Hydrogen goes to water; every carbon goes to CO (as stated):

C₈H₁₈ + O₂ → CO + H₂O

*Step 2 — Balance carbon.*

8 C on the left, so 8 CO on the right:

C₈H₁₈ + O₂ → 8CO + H₂O

*Step 3 — Balance hydrogen.*

18 H on the left, so 9 H₂O on the right:

C₈H₁₈ + O₂ → 8CO + 9H₂O

*Step 4 — Balance oxygen last.*

Right-hand side: 8 (from CO) + 9 (from H₂O) = 17 O atoms. Left-hand side needs 17 O atoms, so 17/2 = 8½ O₂.

C₈H₁₈ + 8½O₂ → 8CO + 9H₂O

*Step 5 — Clear the fraction by doubling.*

2C₈H₁₈ + 17O₂ → 16CO + 18H₂O

Check: C 16 = 16 ✓, H 36 = 36 ✓, O 34 = 34 ✓. The equation balances.

8.1.5.1 Formation of Sulfur Dioxide F+H ▼

Formation of Sulfur Dioxide

Spec point 8.11: "Explain how impurities in some hydrocarbon fuels result in the production of sulfur dioxide" — Foundation + Higher tier, Paper 2, 1CH0 + 1SC0.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
HydrocarbonA compound made of hydrogen and carbon atoms only
Fossil fuelA fuel formed from the remains of dead organisms over millions of years (e.g. coal, crude oil, natural gas)
ImpurityA substance present in a fuel that is not part of the main hydrocarbon — in this context, mainly sulfur
CombustionThe reaction of a fuel with oxygen, releasing energy
Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)An acidic, toxic gas produced when sulfur impurities in a fuel burn in the presence of oxygen
Acidic oxideAn oxide that reacts with water to form an acidic solution — SO₂ dissolves to form sulfurous acid and contributes to acid rain

Where the sulfur comes from

Hydrocarbon fuels — petrol, diesel, kerosene, fuel oil, coal and natural gas — are obtained from crude oil and from underground gas/coal deposits. Although their main components are hydrocarbon molecules (compounds of carbon and hydrogen only), the raw fossil material from which they are refined also contains small amounts of other elements that were trapped in the original biological matter and surrounding rock millions of years ago.

The most important of these "extra" elements, for the purposes of pollution, is sulfur. Sulfur ends up chemically combined inside the fuel in the form of sulfur-containing organic compounds (for example thiols and thiophenes in crude oil fractions, or pyritic and organic sulfur in coal). At GCSE level you are not expected to name those compounds — you only need to be clear that the sulfur is an impurity in the fuel, that it is present in small amounts, and that it is released when the fuel is burned.

Different fuels contain different amounts of sulfur. Coal and heavy fuel oils (used in some power stations and large ships) typically contain more sulfur than petrol, diesel or natural gas. Modern petrol and diesel sold at fuel stations in the UK are deliberately desulfurised at the refinery to keep emissions low, but the underlying chemistry of "sulfur impurity → SO₂ on burning" is the same for any sulfur-containing fuel.

Why burning the fuel produces sulfur dioxide

Combustion is the reaction of a fuel with oxygen. When the main hydrocarbon part of a fuel burns completely it produces carbon dioxide and water:

CH₄ (g) + 2O₂ (g) → CO₂ (g) + 2H₂O (l)

The sulfur impurities inside the fuel react with oxygen at the same time. Sulfur is a non-metal, and like other reactive non-metals it burns in oxygen to form its non-metal oxide. The oxidation product at the temperatures reached in a normal flame is sulfur dioxide:

S (s) + O₂ (g) → SO₂ (g)

So during combustion of a sulfur-containing fuel, two combustion processes happen side by side:

  1. Hydrocarbons burn → carbon dioxide + water
  2. Sulfur impurities burn → sulfur dioxide

That is the heart of what spec point 8.11 wants you to be able to explain. A good written answer states:

  • The fuel is a hydrocarbon but contains sulfur as an impurity.
  • When the fuel is burned (combusted), the sulfur also reacts with oxygen.
  • The product of that reaction is sulfur dioxide, SO₂.
  • The equation: S + O₂ → SO₂.

Sulfur dioxide is an acidic oxide

Sulfur dioxide is classified as an acidic oxide because it reacts with water to form an acidic solution. In the atmosphere it dissolves in water droplets to form sulfurous acid:

SO₂ (g) + H₂O (l) → H₂SO₃ (aq)

It can also be further oxidised in the atmosphere to sulfur trioxide (SO₃), which dissolves in water to form sulfuric acid:

2SO₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2SO₃ (g) SO₃ (g) + H₂O (l) → H₂SO₄ (aq)

These acidic solutions are the chemistry of acid rain, which is covered in detail in 8.1.5.2 Acid Rain. For spec point 8.11 itself, you only need to know that the SO₂ formed during combustion of sulfur-containing fuels is the source of one of the two main acidic gases responsible for acid rain — the other being oxides of nitrogen (see 8.1.5.3 Formation of Nitrogen Oxides).

Worked example: writing the equation for combustion of sulfur impurities

You may be asked to "give the equation" or "write a balanced equation" for the formation of sulfur dioxide when sulfur impurities in a fuel burn.

Step 1 — Identify the reactants. The sulfur in the fuel reacts with oxygen from the air.

Reactants: S and O₂

Step 2 — Identify the product. At the temperatures of combustion in a normal engine or furnace, the oxide formed is sulfur dioxide.

Product: SO₂

Step 3 — Check the atom balance.

S (s) + O₂ (g) → SO₂ (g)

  • Sulfur: 1 on the left, 1 on the right ✓
  • Oxygen: 2 on the left, 2 on the right ✓

Step 4 — Add state symbols (with a space between formula and state symbol).

S (s) + O₂ (g) → SO₂ (g)

The equation is already balanced — no coefficients needed.

Comparing the main pollutants from burning a hydrocarbon fuel

A common exam task is to compare the pollutants released when fuels are burned. The table below summarises the main combustion products you need to be able to discuss for spec point 8.11 and its neighbours.

Why low-sulfur fuels matter

Because the sulfur is locked into the fuel as an impurity, you cannot stop it producing SO₂ by adjusting the burner — wherever the fuel goes, the sulfur goes with it, and any oxygen reaching the flame will oxidise it. The only practical way to reduce SO₂ emissions from transport and heating fuels is to remove the sulfur before the fuel is sold (desulfurisation at the refinery), giving "low-sulfur" or "ultra-low-sulfur" petrol and diesel. For coal-fired power stations, the SO₂ is instead removed from the flue gases after combustion using a process called flue gas desulfurisation, in which the gases are passed through wet calcium oxide or calcium carbonate slurries that react with and trap the SO₂. You do not need detailed industrial knowledge of these processes for spec point 8.11, but knowing that they exist helps you discuss strategies to reduce acid-rain pollution in an extended question.

8.1.5.2 Acid Rain F+H ▼

Acid Rain

Spec point 8.12 — Explain some problems associated with acid rain caused when sulfur dioxide dissolves in rain water. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

Rainwater is naturally slightly acidic (around pH 5.6) because carbon dioxide in the air dissolves to form a weak solution of carbonic acid. Acid rain is rain that is significantly more acidic than this — typically pH 4 or lower — because the atmosphere also contains acidic pollutant gases. The two pollutants you are expected to know for Edexcel GCSE are sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ). When these dissolve in water droplets in clouds they form sulfurous, sulfuric and nitric acids, which then fall as rain, snow, sleet or fog and damage the natural and built environment.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Acid rainRain with a pH significantly below the natural value (around 5.6), caused by acidic pollutant gases such as sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides dissolving in atmospheric water
Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)An acidic gas produced when sulfur-containing fossil fuels are burned; the main cause of acid rain on the Edexcel spec
Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)A mixture of nitrogen monoxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), formed when nitrogen and oxygen from the air react together at the high temperatures inside engines
Sulfurous acid (H₂SO₃)The weak acid formed when SO₂ first dissolves in water
Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄)The strong acid formed when sulfurous acid is further oxidised in the atmosphere
Nitric acid (HNO₃)A strong acid formed when nitrogen dioxide reacts with water and oxygen in the air

Why sulfur dioxide produces acid rain

Sulfur is present as an impurity in coal, in crude oil, and therefore in fuels refined from oil such as petrol, diesel and heavy fuel oil. When these fuels are burned in power stations, factories, vehicles and homes, the sulfur is oxidised to sulfur dioxide:

S + O₂ → SO₂

SO₂ is a colourless, pungent, acidic gas. It rises with the rest of the flue gases, mixes into the air, and is carried by the wind — often for hundreds of kilometres before it comes back down. While it is in the atmosphere, two things happen:

  1. SO₂ dissolves in droplets of water in clouds to form sulfurous acid:

SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃

  1. SO₂ is also slowly oxidised by oxygen in the air (with sunlight and water vapour speeding the process up) to form sulfur trioxide, which dissolves in water to form sulfuric acid:

2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃ SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄

So when the water in the clouds falls as rain, it carries with it a mixture of sulfurous and sulfuric acids. This is the chemistry behind why "SO₂ dissolved in rain water" is so damaging.

Why nitrogen oxides also produce acid rain

At normal air temperatures the nitrogen (N₂) and oxygen (O₂) in the atmosphere do not react — the N≡N triple bond is far too strong. But inside a car or lorry engine, combustion temperatures are very high, and at those temperatures N₂ and O₂ react directly:

N₂ + O₂ → 2NO

The nitrogen monoxide (NO) leaves the exhaust and is quickly oxidised in the open air to nitrogen dioxide:

2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂

Nitrogen dioxide then reacts with water (and more oxygen) in clouds to form nitric acid:

4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃

This is why questions about vehicle emissions, traffic pollution and acid rain often appear together: SO₂ tends to be a power-station problem (coal/oil), whereas NOₓ tends to be a traffic problem (engines).

The problems caused by acid rain

Acid rain has a wide range of damaging effects on living things, on water supplies, on soil, and on the built environment. For the 6-mark extended questions on this topic you are expected to give several distinct points, each linked to a clear chemical explanation. The five most commonly tested harms are below.

*1. Damage to trees and forests*

Acid rain damages trees in two ways:

  • Direct damage to leaves. The acid attacks the waxy cuticle on the leaf surface and damages cell tissue, leaving leaves discoloured (often yellow or brown) and unable to photosynthesise properly. Conifer trees are especially vulnerable because their needles stay on the tree for years, so they are repeatedly exposed to acid deposition.
  • Indirect damage through the soil. Acid rain soaks into the soil and dissolves essential nutrient ions such as calcium (Ca²⁺), magnesium (Mg²⁺) and potassium (K⁺), which are then washed away (leached) before roots can take them up. At the same time, the acid releases toxic ions — particularly aluminium (Al³⁺) — from previously insoluble compounds in the soil. The soluble Al³⁺ damages root tips and stops the tree taking up water and nutrients efficiently. Whole forests can die back over many years.

*2. Acidification of lakes and rivers (damage to fish and aquatic life)*

When acid rain runs off the land into lakes and rivers, the pH of the water falls. Fish eggs will not hatch below about pH 5, and adult fish die at lower pH values still. Aluminium ions washed out of surrounding soil add to the problem: Al³⁺ in solution irritates fish gills, causing a build-up of mucus that suffocates the fish. Insects, amphibians and the plants at the bottom of the food chain are also killed, so the whole aquatic ecosystem collapses. Some lakes in Scandinavia and Scotland have become so acidic that they are biologically dead.

*3. Damage to limestone and marble buildings and statues*

Many historic buildings — castles, cathedrals, statues, gravestones — are built from limestone or marble, both of which are mostly calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). Calcium carbonate is a base, so it reacts with the sulfuric and nitric acids in acid rain in a neutralisation reaction:

CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂ CaCO₃ + 2HNO₃ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + H₂O + CO₂

The calcium sulfate produced is slightly soluble in water and is gradually washed away, eroding the surface of the stone. Fine details on statues and carvings are slowly lost, and the surface of the building looks pitted and dirty. This is irreversible damage: once the carbonate is reacted away it cannot be replaced.

*4. Corrosion of metal structures*

Acid rain attacks exposed metals such as the steel used in bridges, railings, lampposts, car bodies, pylons and ships. Iron reacts with the dilute sulfuric and nitric acids in acid rain to form soluble iron salts:

Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂

The acid speeds up the rusting process because it removes the protective layer of iron oxide from the surface and exposes fresh metal underneath. Galvanised (zinc-coated) steel, lead flashing on roofs, and copper roof tiles are all attacked in a similar way. The result is expensive repair and replacement costs and shorter lifetimes for infrastructure.

*5. Damage to soil pH and crop yields*

When acid rain soaks into soil, the H⁺ ions react with the nutrient ions held on soil particles and displace them, so they are washed away in the drainage water. Calcium, magnesium and potassium are all essential for plant growth, so as the soil becomes more acidic and these nutrients are lost, crop yields fall. Many crop plants also grow poorly below a particular soil pH — most cereals prefer near-neutral soil. Farmers can partly fix this by spreading lime (calcium carbonate or calcium hydroxide) on fields to neutralise the acidity, but that is an extra cost and only treats the symptoms.

8.1.5.3 Formation of Nitrogen Oxides F+H ▼

Formation of Nitrogen Oxides

Spec point 8.13 — Explain why, when fuels are burned in engines, oxygen and nitrogen can react together at high temperatures to produce oxides of nitrogen, which are pollutants. Foundation + Higher tier, Paper 2, examined on both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science).

Nitrogen oxides are a family of pollutant gases formed inside the hot combustion chambers of car, lorry, bus and aeroplane engines. They are not produced by the fuel itself burning — they are produced from the air that is drawn into the engine to supply the oxygen needed for combustion. Understanding this spec point means understanding why a reaction that doesn't normally happen in the atmosphere can suddenly proceed once a fuel is ignited inside an engine.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ)A collective term for the gaseous oxides of nitrogen, mainly nitrogen monoxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), produced when nitrogen and oxygen react at high temperature
Nitrogen monoxide (NO)A colourless gas formed when nitrogen and oxygen react directly inside a hot engine
Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂)A brown, toxic gas formed when nitrogen monoxide reacts with more oxygen in the air after leaving the engine
PollutantA substance released into the environment that causes harm to living organisms, materials or the climate
Activation energyThe minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to take place
Internal combustion engineAn engine in which a fuel-air mixture is burned inside a sealed cylinder to drive a piston

Why nitrogen and oxygen do not normally react in the atmosphere

Air is roughly 78% nitrogen (N₂) and 21% oxygen (O₂), so the two gases are in constant contact at ground level. Despite this, the atmosphere is not slowly turning into nitrogen oxides. The reason is the very strong triple bond in the N₂ molecule.

The N≡N triple bond is one of the strongest covalent bonds known. To make nitrogen react, that triple bond has to be broken first, and breaking it requires a large input of energy. In other words, the reaction:

N₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2NO (g)

has a very high activation energy. At everyday atmospheric temperatures (around 15–25 °C), almost no nitrogen and oxygen molecules collide with enough energy to break the N≡N bond, so the reaction effectively does not happen. This is exactly what we want — if it did happen, the atmosphere would be full of toxic, acidic gases.

Why high engine temperatures change everything

Inside the combustion chamber of an internal combustion engine, the fuel-air mixture is ignited (by a spark in a petrol engine, by compression in a diesel engine). The flame temperature inside a typical engine cylinder can climb well above 1000 °C. At these temperatures three things happen at once:

  1. Particle kinetic energy increases dramatically. Gas molecules move much faster, so collisions are much more energetic.
  2. A much larger fraction of collisions exceed the activation energy. Suddenly, plenty of N₂–O₂ collisions carry enough energy to break the N≡N bond.
  3. The rate of reaction between N₂ and O₂ becomes significant. A reaction that was effectively switched off at room temperature is now switched on.

The overall equation for the first stage is:

N₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2NO (g)

Once the hot exhaust gases leave the engine and mix with cooler air outside, the nitrogen monoxide reacts further with atmospheric oxygen to give nitrogen dioxide:

2NO (g) + O₂ (g) → 2NO₂ (g)

Nitrogen dioxide is the brown gas often seen in heavy smog over cities. Together, NO and NO₂ are referred to as NOₓ.

A common student trap: the fuel is not the source of the nitrogen

A frequent misconception is that nitrogen oxides come from "nitrogen in the fuel". Petrol, diesel and kerosene are hydrocarbons — they contain only carbon and hydrogen, not nitrogen. The nitrogen in NO and NO₂ comes from the nitrogen in the air that is drawn into the engine to provide oxygen for combustion. Roughly four-fifths of every breath of air the engine pulls in is nitrogen, and at the temperatures inside the cylinder a small percentage of it ends up reacting with oxygen.

This means that any combustion in a hot engine — even of a perfectly clean fuel — will tend to produce some nitrogen oxides, simply because the engine breathes air rather than pure oxygen.

Why nitrogen oxides are pollutants

Nitrogen oxides cause several different environmental and health problems, which is why they are classed as pollutants:

ProblemCause
Acid rainNO₂ dissolves in atmospheric water droplets to form a mixture of nitrous and nitric acids, which fall as acid rain
Damage to plants and aquatic lifeAcid rain lowers the pH of soils, lakes and rivers, harming trees, fish and other aquatic organisms
Damage to buildings and statuesAcid rain reacts with carbonates in limestone and marble, eroding stonework over time
Respiratory problemsBreathing in NO₂ irritates the lungs and airways and can worsen conditions like asthma
Photochemical smogNOₓ reacts with unburnt hydrocarbons in sunlight to form the brown, ground-level smog seen over busy cities

The connection to acid rain links this spec point directly to 8.1.5.2 Acid Rain and to the parallel pollutant chemistry covered in 8.1.5.1 Formation of Sulfur Dioxide. In an extended question you may be expected to bring all three together.

8.1.6.1 Hydrogen as a Fuel F+H ▼

Hydrogen as a Fuel

Spec 8.14 — Evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of using hydrogen, rather than petrol, as a fuel in cars. Foundation + Higher tier, Paper 2, both 1CH0 and 1SC0.

Hydrogen is being developed as a possible replacement for petrol in cars because burning it produces only water — no carbon dioxide and none of the pollutants associated with petrol engines. However, hydrogen has serious practical problems that mean it has not yet replaced petrol on any large scale. The exam point here is evaluation: you must be able to weigh the pros against the cons rather than just listing facts about either fuel.

The combustion reaction

When hydrogen burns in oxygen, the only product is water:

2H₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2H₂O (l)

There is no carbon in the fuel, so there is no way to produce CO₂, CO or particulates from the combustion reaction itself. This is the single biggest argument in favour of hydrogen as a road fuel.

Petrol is a mixture of hydrocarbons. A typical component is octane, C₈H₁₈, which burns as:

2C₈H₁₈ (l) + 25O₂ (g) → 16CO₂ (g) + 18H₂O (l)

Even with complete combustion, petrol produces carbon dioxide — a greenhouse gas that contributes to climate change. If combustion is incomplete (which is normal in a real car engine) it also produces carbon monoxide, soot (carbon particulates) and unburnt hydrocarbons. Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) form because the high engine temperatures cause atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen to react. These are all toxic or environmentally damaging pollutants that hydrogen avoids.

Where the hydrogen actually comes from

A crucial point for evaluation questions: hydrogen is not a primary energy source. It does not occur on Earth in useful amounts as H₂ gas — it has to be made, and making it requires energy. The two common industrial methods are:

1. Electrolysis of water: Passing an electric current through water (with a small amount of acid or alkali to make it conduct) splits it into hydrogen and oxygen:

2H₂O (l) → 2H₂ (g) + O₂ (g)

If the electricity comes from renewable sources (wind, solar, hydroelectric) this method produces hydrogen with essentially no carbon emissions. If the electricity comes from a fossil-fuel power station, the carbon dioxide is simply moved from the car's exhaust to the power station's chimney — overall emissions may be no better than burning petrol.

2. Steam reforming of methane: Natural gas (mainly CH₄) is reacted with steam at around 800 °C:

CH₄ (g) + 2H₂O (g) → CO₂ (g) + 4H₂ (g)

This is currently the cheapest way to make hydrogen, but it produces carbon dioxide as a by-product. So although the car emits only water, the overall process still releases greenhouse gases. This is why the green credentials of hydrogen depend entirely on how it is made.

Petrol, by contrast, is refined from crude oil — a finite, non-renewable resource. The energy cost of refining is significant, and extraction also produces emissions, but the established global supply chain makes petrol cheap and reliable.

Storage and energy density

At room temperature and pressure, hydrogen is a gas. To carry enough hydrogen in a car to give a useful driving range, it must be either:

  • Compressed to around 350–700 times atmospheric pressure in heavy, reinforced tanks, or
  • Cooled to a liquid at −253 °C, which requires highly insulated cryogenic tanks and constantly uses energy to keep cold.

Both options are expensive, take up a lot of space, and raise safety questions because hydrogen is extremely flammable across a wide range of mixtures with air.

Petrol is a liquid at room temperature and stores easily in a thin metal or plastic tank. Per litre it carries far more energy than even compressed hydrogen — meaning a petrol car needs a much smaller, lighter, cheaper fuel tank than an equivalent-range hydrogen car. Per kilogram, hydrogen actually carries about three times the energy of petrol, but the bulkiness of the storage system cancels much of this advantage out.

Refuelling infrastructure

In the UK there are tens of thousands of petrol stations. The refuelling network for petrol is more than a century old, fully regulated, and reaches every town. A driver can refuel in two or three minutes at any one of them.

There are only a handful of hydrogen filling stations in the whole country. Building a national network would cost billions of pounds and take many years. Until the network exists, a hydrogen car is only practical for drivers who never travel far from one of those few stations. This "chicken and egg" problem — drivers won't buy hydrogen cars without filling stations, and companies won't build filling stations without hydrogen cars — is one of the strongest practical arguments against hydrogen at the moment.

Renewability

Hydrogen made by electrolysis using renewable electricity is, in effect, a renewable fuel: the water produced when it burns can be split again, and the energy input comes from sunlight, wind or moving water that will not run out.

Petrol comes from crude oil, which is a fossil fuel. It is non-renewable: once a reserve is used up it cannot be replaced on any human timescale. Eventually petrol will become too expensive or simply run out, regardless of any environmental arguments.

Cost, safety and engine technology

Hydrogen cars are currently much more expensive to buy than equivalent petrol cars, partly because they are produced in tiny numbers. The fuel itself is also more expensive per mile driven, mainly because of the energy needed to make and compress the hydrogen.

Hydrogen is extremely flammable and burns with an almost invisible flame, which means a leak can be hard to detect. However, because hydrogen is lighter than air it tends to disperse upwards quickly in the open, whereas petrol vapour is heavier than air and pools at ground level. Both fuels have well-understood safety risks; neither is inherently "safer".

Existing petrol engines cannot run on hydrogen without significant modification. Most hydrogen cars actually use hydrogen fuel cells (see 5.4.2.1 Fuel Cells) rather than burning the hydrogen in a conventional engine, but spec 8.14 is about hydrogen as a combustion fuel rather than the fuel-cell route.

Fossil Fuels

Spec point 8.15: Recall that petrol, kerosene and diesel oil are non-renewable fossil fuels obtained from crude oil and methane is a non-renewable fossil fuel found in natural gas. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 2.

This is a short, recall-level spec point, but it underpins a large block of Topic 8.1 content. If you cannot reliably state which fuels come from crude oil and which come from natural gas, you will lose easy marks in the early parts of fuels questions and you will also struggle with the longer follow-up parts on fractional distillation, combustion and environmental impact. The aim of this note is to lock down the four named fuels, the two fossil-fuel sources, the meaning of the word "non-renewable", and the most common ways examiners ask about these ideas.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition

The four named fuels in this spec point

Edexcel is very specific in spec point 8.15: four fuels are named, and each one has a specified source. You must be able to match each fuel to its source without hesitation.

FuelSourceTypical use

Petrol, kerosene and diesel oil are all mixtures of hydrocarbons separated from crude oil by fractional distillation. They are not single compounds — they are fractions, each containing a range of hydrocarbon molecules with similar boiling points and similar chain lengths. Methane, in contrast, is a single, pure compound (CH₄) and is the main component of natural gas.

A common slip in exams is to write that "methane comes from crude oil" or that "petrol comes from natural gas". Both are wrong. Anchor the rule in your memory like this: three crude-oil fractions (petrol, kerosene, diesel) plus one natural-gas fuel (methane).

Why fossil fuels are non-renewable

Fossil fuels formed over hundreds of millions of years from the remains of dead organisms — mostly tiny marine plants and animals for crude oil and natural gas, and ancient land plants for coal — that were buried under sediment, compressed, and chemically altered in the absence of oxygen.

Two things make them non-renewable:

  1. The timescale of formation is enormous. The processes that produced today's reserves of crude oil and natural gas took tens to hundreds of millions of years. Even though the same processes are still happening somewhere on Earth, the rate at which new fossil fuels form is negligible compared with the rate at which we currently burn them.
  2. The reserves are finite. Crude oil and natural gas exist in underground reservoirs of a fixed size. Once a reservoir is emptied, it does not refill on any timescale that is useful to a human society.

For a resource to count as "renewable", it must replenish itself on a timescale of years or decades — sunlight, wind, biomass (e.g. ethanol from sugar cane) and falling water all qualify. Fossil fuels do not, which is why all four fuels in this spec point are classified as non-renewable.

Why this distinction matters in exams

Examiners often build longer questions around the simple recall in spec point 8.15. For example:

  • "State one non-renewable fuel obtained from crude oil." (1 mark — accept petrol, kerosene, diesel, or any other named fraction.)
  • "Identify the main fossil fuel found in natural gas." (1 mark — answer is methane.)
  • "Give one reason why methane is described as a non-renewable fuel." (1 mark — because it is being used up faster than it is being formed; or because it took millions of years to form and the reserves are finite.)
  • "Explain why petrol is described as a fossil fuel." (1–2 marks — because it is obtained from crude oil, which was formed over millions of years from the remains of dead organisms.)

If you misname a fuel, you can lose the mark even if the rest of your reasoning is correct. Stick to the four exact names in the spec where possible.

Crude oil and natural gas: how the four fuels are obtained

Although this SP is only the recall point, the way the fuels are obtained is examined together with it and is worth understanding:

  • Crude oil is pumped from underground reservoirs and then sent to an oil refinery. There, it is heated and passed into a fractionating column, where the different hydrocarbon fractions separate out by boiling point. Petrol, kerosene and diesel oil are three of these fractions. (Other fractions you may meet — refinery gases, naphtha, fuel oil and bitumen — are not named in spec point 8.15 but appear elsewhere in Topic 8.1.)
  • Natural gas is also extracted from underground reservoirs, often associated with crude oil deposits. The gas is processed to remove impurities (such as water, sulfur compounds and any longer-chain hydrocarbons), leaving mostly methane, which is then distributed through pipelines.

The key contrast is that crude oil is a liquid mixture that needs to be split into useful fractions, while natural gas is essentially a single useful fuel (methane) that just needs cleaning up.

What happens when these fuels burn

Although spec point 8.15 is purely a recall point about sources, every exam question that names these fuels eventually goes on to ask about combustion, so it is worth being ready.

All four fuels burn in oxygen to release energy. The general word equation for complete combustion of any hydrocarbon is:

*hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water*

For methane specifically:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

If there is not enough oxygen, incomplete combustion occurs and may produce carbon monoxide (CO) and/or solid carbon (soot, C) instead of, or as well as, carbon dioxide. Petrol, kerosene and diesel oil all contain a range of hydrocarbons, but the same general pattern applies — complete combustion gives CO₂ and H₂O; limited oxygen gives CO and/or C.

This is also where the environmental issues with fossil fuels start to enter the picture: CO₂ from complete combustion contributes to the enhanced greenhouse effect, CO from incomplete combustion is toxic, soot causes respiratory problems and global dimming, and sulfur impurities in some fractions (especially diesel and fuel oil) can produce sulfur dioxide and acid rain. Those issues are examined under later Topic 8.1 spec points, but they are why the renewable-vs-non-renewable distinction made here matters.

8.1.7.1 Cracking F+H ▼

Cracking

Spec 8.16: Explain how cracking involves the breaking down of larger, saturated hydrocarbon molecules (alkanes) into smaller, more useful ones, some of which are unsaturated (alkenes).

Spec 8.17: Explain why cracking is necessary.

Cracking is one of the most important reactions in the petrochemical industry. After crude oil has been separated by fractional distillation, the supply of long-chain fractions (such as bitumen, heavy fuel oil and lubricating oil) is far greater than the demand. At the same time, the demand for shorter-chain products — particularly petrol, diesel and the alkenes used to make polymers — is much higher than the natural supply. Cracking solves this imbalance by chemically breaking long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful molecules.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CrackingThe breaking down of larger, saturated hydrocarbon (alkane) molecules into smaller, more useful hydrocarbons, some of which are unsaturated (alkenes)
Saturated hydrocarbonA hydrocarbon containing only single C–C bonds — every carbon is bonded to the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms (general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ for alkanes)
Unsaturated hydrocarbonA hydrocarbon containing at least one C=C double bond — alkenes have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ
Thermal decompositionBreaking a substance down using heat; cracking is a type of thermal decomposition
CatalystA substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up; in catalytic cracking, hot powdered aluminium oxide or silica is used
FeedstockThe starting material fed into an industrial process — in cracking this is usually a long-chain alkane fraction such as naphtha

Why Cracking Is Necessary

The fractions obtained directly from the fractional distillation of crude oil are not produced in the proportions that match consumer demand. Long-chain fractions — heavy fuel oil, bitumen and lubricating oil — are produced in large quantities, but their uses are limited (mainly tar for roads, ship fuel and machine oils). In contrast, demand is high for:

  • Petrol and diesel — short-chain alkanes used as fuel for cars, vans and lorries
  • Alkenes — particularly ethene and propene, which are the building blocks of poly(ethene), poly(propene) and many other polymers
  • Hydrogen — used to make ammonia in the Haber process and in fuel cells

Without cracking, refineries would be left with huge stockpiles of unwanted long-chain hydrocarbons and a chronic shortage of petrol-grade fuel and alkenes for the plastics industry. Cracking therefore matches supply to demand by turning low-value, low-demand material into high-value, high-demand products.

*Why long-chain hydrocarbons are less useful as fuels*

Long-chain alkanes have higher boiling points, are more viscous and are harder to vaporise and ignite. A petrol engine needs a fuel that vaporises easily so it can mix with air in the cylinder and ignite cleanly. Bitumen, by contrast, is a thick semi-solid at room temperature — it cannot be burned in an internal-combustion engine. Shorter chains are more volatile, less viscous and ignite more readily, which is why petrol (typically C₅–C₁₀) and diesel (typically C₁₀–C₁₅) are far more in demand than the heavier fractions.

What Happens During Cracking

Cracking involves passing the vaporised long-chain alkane over a hot catalyst, or simply heating it strongly. The high temperature provides enough energy to break some of the strong C–C single bonds in the long chain. The molecule splits into two or more fragments. To balance the hydrogens, at least one of the smaller fragments must contain a C=C double bond — this is the alkene.

The key features of every cracking reaction are:

  1. The reactant is a saturated alkane (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂).
  2. The conditions are high temperature (typically around 600–700 °C) and often a catalyst such as aluminium oxide or silica.
  3. The products are a shorter alkane plus one or more alkenes (and sometimes hydrogen).
  4. The total number of carbon atoms and hydrogen atoms on each side of the equation is conserved — cracking is just a rearrangement of atoms.

Because the alkane reactant has the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ and an alkene has the formula CₙH₂ₙ, the products always have fewer hydrogens per carbon than the reactant. The "missing" hydrogens go into the shorter alkane fragment, which keeps the general alkane formula.

Worked Example — Cracking Dodecane

Dodecane is a C₁₂ alkane found in the kerosene/naphtha fractions. A typical cracking reaction breaks it into one shorter alkane plus two ethene molecules.

*Step 1 — Write the formula of the reactant.*

Dodecane is an alkane with 12 carbons: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ with n = 12, so the formula is C₁₂H₂₆.

*Step 2 — Write the formulae of the named products.*

  • Octane is an alkane with 8 carbons: C₈H₁₈
  • Ethene is an alkene with 2 carbons: C₂H₄

*Step 3 — Balance carbons.*

LHS: 12 carbons. RHS so far: 8 (from octane) + 2 (from one ethene) = 10. Need 2 more carbons. Add a second ethene: 8 + 2 + 2 = 12 carbons. ✓

*Step 4 — Check hydrogens.*

LHS: 26 hydrogens. RHS: 18 (octane) + 4 (ethene) + 4 (ethene) = 26. ✓

*Final balanced equation:*

C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + 2 C₂H₄

*Mass check (conservation of mass)*

Using Ar values C = 12, H = 1:

  • Mr (C₁₂H₂₆) = (12 × 12) + (26 × 1) = 144 + 26 = 170
  • Mr (C₈H₁₈) = (12 × 8) + (18 × 1) = 96 + 18 = 114
  • Mr (C₂H₄) = (12 × 2) + (4 × 1) = 24 + 4 = 28
  • Total RHS = 114 + (2 × 28) = 114 + 56 = 170 ✓

*Step 5 — Use the equation to calculate a mass.*

What is the maximum mass of octane produced when 340 g of dodecane is cracked according to this equation?

  • Moles of dodecane = 340 ÷ 170 = 2.00 mol
  • From the equation, 1 mol dodecane → 1 mol octane, so moles of octane = 2.00 mol
  • Mass of octane = moles × Mr = 2.00 × 114 = 228 g

So 340 g of dodecane gives a maximum of 228 g of octane plus 112 g of ethene (4.00 mol × 28 = 112 g). Notice that 228 + 112 = 340 g — mass is conserved, as it must be in any balanced equation.

Deducing an Unknown Cracking Product

A common exam variation gives you a cracking equation with one unknown formula and asks you to deduce it. You apply conservation of atoms: total carbons on each side must match, and total hydrogens on each side must match.

Example: Decane is cracked according to the equation

C₁₀H₂₂ → hexane + X

where X is a single alkene. Deduce the molecular formula of X.

  • LHS: 10 C and 22 H
  • Hexane (C₆H₁₄) accounts for 6 C and 14 H
  • X must contain: 10 − 6 = 4 carbons, and 22 − 14 = 8 hydrogens
  • X = C₄H₈ — this matches the alkene general formula CₙH₂ₙ (with n = 4), so X is an alkene (butene). ✓

Mathematical Skills

*Skill 1c — Use ratios, fractions and percentages.*

In cracking calculations the key formula is the moles relationship from the balanced equation:

`` moles = mass ÷ Mr mass = moles × Mr ``

*Procedure:*

  1. Write the balanced equation for the cracking reaction.
  2. Calculate the Mr of each species using Ar values from the periodic table.
  3. Convert the given mass into moles (mass ÷ Mr).
  4. Use the molar ratio from the balanced equation to find the moles of the required product.
  5. Convert moles back into mass (moles × Mr) for the final answer.
  6. Check conservation of mass: total mass of products = mass of reactant.

8.2.1.1 Earth's Early Atmosphere F+H ▼

Earth's Early Atmosphere

Spec 8.18–8.20: the origin, composition, and early development of Earth's atmosphere — including how volcanic gases formed the first atmosphere and how oceans were produced by condensation of water vapour.

Examined on: Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 2

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AtmosphereThe layer of gases held around the Earth by gravity
Volcanic activityThe release of magma, ash, and gases from inside the Earth through volcanoes
Early atmosphereThe mixture of gases thought to have surrounded the Earth around 4.5 billion years ago
CondensationThe change of state from gas to liquid that occurs when a vapour cools below its boiling point
Water vapourThe gaseous form of water, present in the atmosphere as H₂O (g)

How the Early Atmosphere Formed

For the first few hundred million years of Earth's existence, the planet was extremely hot and geologically very active. Volcanoes erupted continuously across the surface, releasing huge volumes of gases trapped inside the Earth. These volcanic gases collected around the planet, held in place by gravity, and formed what we call the early atmosphere.

The composition of the early atmosphere is therefore essentially a record of which gases were released by volcanoes. Today, scientists study modern volcanic emissions — together with rocks, fossils, and ice cores — to deduce what the early atmosphere most likely contained. Because we cannot directly sample air from 4.5 billion years ago, the early-atmosphere composition is described as what scientists "think" the atmosphere contained rather than as established fact. This uncertainty is important: it is why spec 8.19 uses the phrase "thought to contain" and why exam questions often ask candidates to suggest rather than state.

*Why a hot, volcanic Earth produced a CO₂-rich atmosphere*

The gases trapped inside molten rock are mainly carbon dioxide, water vapour, nitrogen, and small amounts of methane and ammonia. As the Earth's interior cooled and the surface solidified, volcanoes acted as vents — releasing these gases into space around the planet. Because there were no plants or photosynthesising organisms to remove CO₂, and no living things to release O₂, the atmosphere built up with very high CO₂ levels and essentially no oxygen.

*Why little or no oxygen*

Modern Earth has an oxygen-rich atmosphere only because of photosynthesis by plants, algae, and cyanobacteria. In the early atmosphere there were no such organisms, so no oxygen was being produced. Any small amount of oxygen released by volcanic processes would quickly react with iron, sulfur, or methane in the surroundings and be removed. This is why exam answers must say little or no oxygen — saying simply "no oxygen" is correct in spirit, but the spec phrase keeps the door open to trace amounts.

*Why so much carbon dioxide*

Volcanic gases are roughly 50–90% CO₂ today, and were probably similar billions of years ago. With no plants or oceans absorbing it, CO₂ built up in the atmosphere to very high levels. Most of this CO₂ has since been removed in two main ways:

  1. Dissolution in the oceans once they formed
  2. Locked up in carbonate rocks (limestone, chalk) and in fossil fuels formed from dead organisms

Formation of the Oceans

As the Earth slowly cooled, the temperature at the surface eventually dropped below 100 °C — the boiling point of water at the relevant pressure. When this happened, the water vapour in the atmosphere could no longer remain as a gas. It began to condense: changing state from H₂O (g) to H₂O (l).

`` H₂O (g) → H₂O (l) water vapour → liquid water ``

This condensed water fell as torrential rain over millions of years. The liquid water collected in the lowest parts of the Earth's surface — the great basins between continental rock — and gradually filled them. These pooled volumes of liquid water became the first oceans.

*Why oceans only formed once Earth cooled*

While the surface temperature was above 100 °C, any water on the ground would re-evaporate immediately. The atmosphere could hold enormous quantities of water vapour because it was so hot. Once the surface dropped below 100 °C, the gas-to-liquid balance shifted — condensation overtook evaporation, rainfall built up faster than it could re-evaporate, and standing water became permanent.

*Why ocean formation was important for atmospheric change*

Once oceans existed, large quantities of CO₂ began to dissolve in the seawater. This was one of the major routes by which the atmospheric CO₂ levels eventually fell — long before photosynthesis became the second great driver. Dissolved CO₂ then reacted with dissolved metal ions (calcium, magnesium) to form carbonate sediments that later became carbonate rocks, locking that carbon away from the atmosphere for billions of years.

8.2.1.2 How Carbon Dioxide Decreased F+H ▼

How Carbon Dioxide Decreased

Spec 8.21: Explain how the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere was decreased when carbon dioxide dissolved as the oceans formed.

Foundation + Higher tier · Separate (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 2.

Earth's earliest atmosphere — formed by intense volcanic activity around 4.6 billion years ago — was dominated by carbon dioxide, with significant water vapour and small amounts of methane, ammonia and nitrogen. Today, CO₂ is a trace gas at roughly 0.04% (about 400 ppm). The story of how the level fell by several orders of magnitude is built on a small number of well-defined chemical and biological processes. This SP focuses on the dissolving route — what happened as the planet cooled and the oceans first formed.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AtmosphereThe mixture of gases surrounding the Earth, held in place by gravity
SolubleAble to dissolve in a solvent (here, water) to form a solution
CarbonateAn ionic compound containing the CO₃²⁻ ion (e.g. CaCO₃, MgCO₃)
PrecipitateAn insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed, or when a dissolved substance can no longer remain in solution
Sedimentary rockA rock formed when small particles or precipitates settle, accumulate and become compacted over long periods of time
Fossil fuelA fuel formed over millions of years from the remains of dead plants or animals (coal, crude oil, natural gas)

Starting point — why CO₂ was so high in the early atmosphere

When the Earth's surface was still extremely hot (in some models above 1,000 °C), liquid water could not exist. Volcanoes released enormous quantities of gases, the most abundant of which were carbon dioxide and water vapour. Smaller amounts of methane, ammonia and nitrogen were also released. Because there were no oceans, no green plants and no rocks containing fixed carbon, almost all of the carbon on Earth was sitting in the atmosphere as CO₂.

This is the starting condition for the present spec point: a hot, dry planet with a thick CO₂-rich atmosphere. Two things had to happen for the CO₂ level to fall — Earth had to cool, and CO₂ had to be removed from the atmosphere by physical or chemical processes.

Step 1 — The Earth cools and the oceans form

As the planet's surface cooled below about 100 °C, water vapour in the atmosphere could condense. Over many millions of years, sustained condensation produced rain that filled low-lying regions of the Earth's crust. This was the formation of the oceans — a single, enormous body of liquid water in contact with the atmosphere above it.

The key point for this SP is that the oceans introduced a new "sink" for CO₂. Before the oceans existed, gases released by volcanoes simply built up in the atmosphere because they had nowhere to go. Once liquid water was present, soluble atmospheric gases — most importantly CO₂ — could be absorbed at the air–water interface.

Step 2 — Carbon dioxide dissolves in the oceans

CO₂ is moderately soluble in water. When CO₂ dissolves, some of it reacts with the water to form a weak acid, carbonic acid:

CO₂ (g) + H₂O (l) → H₂CO₃ (aq)

This reaction is reversible and only a small fraction of the dissolved CO₂ converts to H₂CO₃ at any instant. However, the simple act of dissolving — removing CO₂ molecules from the gas phase and incorporating them into the ocean — was enough to lower atmospheric CO₂ steadily over geological time.

Why this matters: dissolving is a physical removal mechanism. Every CO₂ molecule that enters the ocean is one less in the atmosphere. Because the oceans cover roughly 70% of Earth's surface and are kilometres deep, they represent a vast reservoir capable of absorbing huge quantities of CO₂.

Step 3 — Carbonates form and are locked up in rocks

Dissolved CO₂ (and the carbonic acid it produces) reacted with metal ions in the seawater — particularly calcium and magnesium ions washed in from the weathering of rocks on land. These reactions produced insoluble metal carbonates:

Ca²⁺ (aq) + CO₃²⁻ (aq) → CaCO₃ (s)

Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) is the chemical formula of limestone and chalk. It is highly insoluble, so once formed it falls out of solution as a precipitate and settles on the ocean floor. Over hundreds of millions of years these precipitates were compacted by overlying sediment to form sedimentary rocks — the limestones, chalks and dolomites that today make up a huge proportion of Earth's crust.

Carbon was also locked into rocks by a second route: marine organisms (corals, shellfish, plankton) build their hard parts from CaCO₃, and when they died their shells fell to the sea floor and added to the same sedimentary record.

The chemical message is the same in either case — atmospheric CO₂ was first absorbed into the oceans, then converted to solid carbonates, and is now stored in rocks. That carbon is no longer in the atmosphere acting as a greenhouse gas.

Step 4 — Carbon stored as fossil fuels

A separate (but related) route also removed CO₂ from the atmosphere on long timescales: photosynthesis by green plants and marine phytoplankton converted CO₂ into organic matter. When those organisms died, some of the carbon-rich remains were buried under sediment before they could fully decay. Over millions of years, heat and pressure converted this material into fossil fuels — coal (from land plants), and crude oil and natural gas (from marine micro-organisms).

Fossil fuels therefore represent atmospheric carbon that was photosynthesised out of the air and then locked underground. This is the link to combustion, climate change, and the modern carbon cycle (see 8.2.2.1 Greenhouse Gases).

Summary table — where the early CO₂ went

Removal routeWhat happenedFinal destination
Dissolving in oceansCO₂ moved from gas phase into seawaterDissolved CO₂ / carbonic acid in oceans
Carbonate precipitationDissolved CO₂ reacted with metal ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺)Insoluble metal carbonates → sedimentary rocks (limestone, chalk)

The dissolving route (highlighted in spec point 8.21) is the first and largest of these — the others all depend on CO₂ first being removed from the atmosphere into water.

Why CO₂ specifically — what about the other gases?

A useful "why" question is: of all the gases volcanoes released, why did CO₂ in particular get removed by dissolving while nitrogen built up?

  • Carbon dioxide is a polar-bonded molecule that is moderately soluble in water and reacts to form carbonic acid. This pulls it out of the atmosphere.
  • Nitrogen (N₂) has a triple bond and is essentially unreactive at low temperatures. It is also poorly soluble in water. So as the atmosphere lost CO₂ and gained O₂ (from photosynthesis), the relatively unreactive nitrogen accumulated. Today nitrogen is the most abundant atmospheric gas at about 78%.
  • Water vapour condensed and fell as rain — this is what formed the oceans in the first place.

This explains why today's atmosphere is dominated by N₂ (78%) and O₂ (21%) with only trace CO₂ (~0.04%): the soluble and reactive gases were removed; the inert ones remained.

Worked exam-style explanation

A typical 3-mark "Explain" question looks like this:

> Explain how the percentage of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere decreased over time as the oceans formed. (3 marks)

A full-mark answer should include three linked points:

  1. As the Earth cooled, water vapour condensed to form the oceans.
  2. Carbon dioxide dissolved in the ocean water (it is soluble).
  3. Dissolved CO₂ reacted with metal ions to form insoluble carbonates (e.g. CaCO₃), which were locked up as sedimentary rocks (limestone/chalk), and/or the carbon was locked up in fossil fuels formed from dead organisms.

A common mistake is to stop after step 2 — "CO₂ dissolved in the oceans" — which gets one mark but leaves the explanation incomplete. The full mark scheme expects you to follow the carbon all the way into rocks or fossil fuels.

Common student errors

  • Confusing this SP with the rise of oxygen. CO₂ fell both because it dissolved and because plants photosynthesised it into glucose and O₂. The spec point 8.21 focuses on dissolving; the photosynthesis route is covered separately in 8.2.1.3.
  • Writing that CO₂ "evaporated" or "disappeared". Gases do not simply disappear — they are physically moved (into the ocean) or chemically changed (into carbonates, fossil fuels). Always name the destination.
  • Saying "CO₂ dissolved into rocks". CO₂ dissolves in water. Carbonates form in solution and only then become rocks. Get the order right: gas → dissolved → solid → rock.
  • Forgetting the cooling step. Without the Earth cooling below 100 °C, water vapour could not condense, so there were no oceans for the CO₂ to dissolve in. Cooling is the trigger for everything else.

8.2.1.3 How Oxygen Increased F+H ▼

How Oxygen Increased

Spec 8.22: Explain how the growth of primitive plants used carbon dioxide and released oxygen by photosynthesis and consequently the amount of oxygen in the atmosphere gradually increased.

This spec point asks you to link a biological process (photosynthesis) to a chemical change in the composition of the atmosphere over billions of years. The story is short to remember but easy to muddle on the exam — students often know that "plants made oxygen" but cannot explain the link to carbon dioxide decreasing at the same time, or cannot quote the photosynthesis equation. Both ideas are needed for full marks on an Explain question.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
PhotosynthesisThe process by which green plants and algae use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen
Primitive plantsEarly photosynthesising organisms (algae and, later, simple land plants) that evolved in the oceans and gradually colonised the land
AlgaeSimple aquatic photosynthesising organisms; the first organisms on Earth thought to have released oxygen into the atmosphere
AtmosphereThe layer of gases surrounding the Earth; its composition has changed over the planet's 4.6-billion-year history

The photosynthesis equation

The single most important thing to memorise for this spec point is the balanced equation for photosynthesis:

6CO₂ (g) + 6H₂O (l) → C₆H₁₂O₆ (aq) + 6O₂ (g)

In words: six molecules of carbon dioxide react with six molecules of water, in the presence of light energy and chlorophyll, to produce one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen.

A simpler word equation is also acceptable:

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

Either form earns the mark for State the equation for photosynthesis — but the symbol equation must be balanced and must show state symbols correctly: CO₂ as a gas, H₂O as a liquid (in the leaf), glucose as aqueous (dissolved in cell sap), and O₂ as a gas released to the atmosphere.

*Why photosynthesis matters for the atmosphere*

Photosynthesis does two things to the atmosphere at the same time:

  1. It removes carbon dioxide from the air (CO₂ is the reactant).
  2. It releases oxygen into the air (O₂ is the product).

That is why a good Explain answer always mentions both — not just "plants made oxygen". A 6-mark question that links plant life to changes in the atmosphere wants you to address CO₂ going down and O₂ going up, because the same reaction causes both.

How the rise of oxygen actually happened

The currently accepted story, in the order events are believed to have occurred:

  1. Earth's early atmosphere (around 4 billion years ago) was dominated by carbon dioxide, with water vapour, small amounts of methane and ammonia, and almost no oxygen. It is thought to have been similar to the atmospheres of Mars and Venus today.
  2. Water vapour condensed as the Earth cooled, forming the oceans.
  3. Algae evolved in the oceans around 2.7 billion years ago. These were the first photosynthesising organisms on Earth.
  4. Algae began photosynthesis, removing CO₂ from the atmosphere and releasing O₂.
  5. For hundreds of millions of years, almost all the oxygen produced reacted with dissolved iron and other reducing substances in the oceans — so it did not build up in the atmosphere immediately. (This is interesting context; you do not need to memorise the iron chemistry for GCSE, but it explains why the rise was slow.)
  6. Once those "oxygen sinks" were used up, oxygen started to accumulate in the atmosphere.
  7. Plants evolved on land later (around 470 million years ago), and the increase in oxygen accelerated as land plants spread and photosynthesised on a vast scale.
  8. Today, the atmosphere contains approximately 21% oxygen and only about 0.04% carbon dioxide.

The key Edexcel point is that the rise in oxygen was gradual, not sudden — it took billions of years and depended on the spread of photosynthesising life.

Linking the rise in O₂ to the fall in CO₂

A common exam trap is to discuss oxygen and carbon dioxide as if they were unrelated. They are not. Every mole of O₂ released by photosynthesis corresponds (stoichiometrically) to a mole of CO₂ removed from the atmosphere — look at the balanced equation:

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

For every six moles of CO₂ consumed, six moles of O₂ are released. So as photosynthesis spread:

  • Carbon dioxide in the atmosphere decreased (it was being used as a reactant).
  • Oxygen in the atmosphere increased (it was being released as a product).

Other processes also lowered CO₂ at the same time (CO₂ dissolving in the oceans, carbon being locked up in carbonate rocks like limestone, and later in the bodies of organisms that became fossil fuels) — those are covered in 8.2.1.2 How Carbon Dioxide Decreased. For this spec point, focus on the photosynthesis link.

8.2.1.4 The Test for Oxygen F+H ▼

The Test for Oxygen

Spec 8.23: Describe the chemical test for oxygen.

Tier: Foundation + Higher · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 2.

This is a narrow recall spec point. The test for oxygen is one of four core gas tests on the Edexcel GCSE Chemistry specification (the others being hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and chlorine). Although the procedure itself is short, examiners have flagged it as a topic where candidates routinely lose marks because the wording of the answer must be precise.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Glowing splintA wooden splint that has been lit and then blown out so that the tip is still hot and smouldering (glowing red/orange) but not producing a flame
RelightsThe process of the glowing tip bursting back into a visible flame when exposed to a higher concentration of oxygen than is normally found in air
CombustionA chemical reaction between a fuel and oxygen that releases heat and light; the reason a splint can relight in pure oxygen

The test — exactly what to write

Test: Insert a glowing splint into a sample of the gas (or hold the glowing splint at the mouth of the test tube).

Positive result: The splint relights.

That is the full mark-scheme answer. Both halves are required for the 2 marks typically awarded — the reagent/apparatus (a glowing splint) and the observation (the splint relights).

*Why the splint must be glowing, not flaming*

The wood of the splint is already hot enough to combust; what limits combustion in air is the concentration of oxygen (~21%). Air contains enough O₂ to keep a flame burning, but not enough to relight a smouldering splint on its own. When you push the glowing tip into a tube of pure (or oxygen-rich) gas, the much higher O₂ concentration accelerates the combustion reaction enough that the splint bursts back into flame. If you used a fully lit splint instead, it would already be flaming in air and there would be no observable change — so the test would be useless.

*Why "relights" is the precise word*

Examiners look for the word relights (or "rekindles", or "bursts back into flame"). Answers such as "the splint burns brighter", "the splint catches fire", or "the splint glows more" are not equivalent and may not score. The splint was already glowing; the diagnostic change is that a flame reappears.

Comparison with the other GCSE gas tests

Knowing the test for oxygen in isolation is rarely enough — exam questions often present an unknown gas and ask which gas it is, or list several gases and ask which tests apply. Memorise all four together so you do not confuse them under pressure.

GasTestPositive Result
Oxygen (O₂)Insert a glowing splintSplint relights

*The two splint tests are easy to mix up*

A common candidate error is to write "lit splint" for oxygen or "glowing splint" for hydrogen. Get the pairing right:

  • Oxygen → glowing splint → relights
  • Hydrogen → lit splint → squeaky pop

The logic helps: oxygen supports combustion (so it relights something that was nearly out), while hydrogen is combustible (so a lit flame ignites a tiny pocket of hydrogen and the rapid burn produces the pop sound).

The chemistry behind the test

Combustion of wood (which is largely cellulose, a carbohydrate) is an exothermic reaction with oxygen:

cellulose + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + heat + light

In normal air, a glowing splint has lost enough heat that the rate of combustion has dropped too low to sustain a flame — only smouldering oxidation of the charred wood continues. Increasing the partial pressure of O₂ around the splint pushes the reaction rate up. The released heat raises the local temperature above the ignition point of the wood gases, and a visible flame reappears. This is essentially Le Chatelier-style thinking applied to the kinetics of combustion: more reactant (O₂) → faster reaction → more heat released per second → flame.

You do not need this depth for the GCSE mark, but understanding why the splint relights makes the test impossible to forget — and protects you against muddling it with the hydrogen test.

Practical context — where this test fits in the lab

The oxygen test is most often used to confirm that:

  • A reaction is producing O₂ (for example, the catalysed decomposition of hydrogen peroxide using manganese(IV) oxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂)
  • An electrolysis cell is producing O₂ at the anode (for example, electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid or water)
  • A photosynthesising plant is generating O₂ (for example, gas collected over pondweed in a light experiment)
  • A sample of an unknown gas is oxygen rather than another colourless gas

When you write an answer in a practical-context question, name the apparatus and observation in the same direction the splint moves: insert the glowing splint into the gas (or hold it at the mouth of the test tube), then state that it relights.

*Safety and method tips you may need in a practical question:*

  • The splint must be hot enough to react — light it and only just blow it out so the tip stays orange
  • Insert it quickly; if you wait, the splint cools and the test will give a false negative even when oxygen is present
  • The test does not distinguish between pure O₂ and an oxygen-rich mixture — any gas sample containing significantly more than ~21% O₂ will relight a splint

Greenhouse Gases

Spec 8.24: Describe how various gases in the atmosphere, including carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour, absorb heat radiated from the Earth, subsequently releasing energy which keeps the Earth warm: this is known as the greenhouse effect.

Tier: Foundation + Higher · Qualification: 1CH0 + 1SC0 · Paper: 2

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Greenhouse gasA gas in the atmosphere that absorbs heat (infrared radiation) emitted from the Earth's surface and re-emits it in all directions, including back towards the Earth
Greenhouse effectThe natural process by which greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb heat radiated from the Earth's surface and release energy that keeps the Earth warm
Infrared radiationA form of electromagnetic radiation, often called "heat radiation"; emitted by all warm objects, including the Earth's surface
Re-radiatedRe-emitted as new radiation after being absorbed — the warmed gas releases energy in all directions, not only back towards space
AtmosphereThe thin layer of gases surrounding the Earth, made up mostly of nitrogen (~78%) and oxygen (~21%), with trace amounts of CO₂, methane, water vapour and other gases

The three greenhouse gases named on the spec

The specification names three gases that you must recognise as greenhouse gases:

GasFormulaSource (natural and human)Notes
Carbon dioxideCO₂Respiration, volcanic activity, combustion of fossil fuels, deforestationThe most commonly tested greenhouse gas in 1CH0
MethaneCH₄Wetlands, livestock (cattle), rice paddies, landfill, leaks from natural-gas extractionA much stronger greenhouse gas per molecule than CO₂
Water vapourH₂O (g)Evaporation from oceans, lakes, rivers and from plants (transpiration)The most abundant greenhouse gas, but its concentration depends mostly on temperature, not on human activity

You are not required to memorise relative warming "potencies" at GCSE — only that all three are greenhouse gases and that all three behave in the same general way: they absorb heat that is radiated from the Earth.

How the greenhouse effect works — step by step

The greenhouse effect is a sequence. Get the order right and you will not lose marks.

  1. Short-wavelength radiation from the Sun (visible light, plus some ultraviolet) reaches the Earth.
  2. Most of this radiation passes straight through the atmosphere — greenhouse gases do not absorb visible light.
  3. The radiation is absorbed by the Earth's surface (land, oceans, plants), which warms up.
  4. The warmed Earth emits its own radiation — but because the Earth is much cooler than the Sun, this is longer-wavelength infrared (heat) radiation.
  5. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere — CO₂, methane and water vapour — absorb this infrared radiation.
  6. The greenhouse gases then re-emit energy in all directions, including back towards the Earth's surface.
  7. The energy that is sent back to Earth keeps the Earth warm. Without this process, the average surface temperature of the Earth would be far below 0 °C and the planet would be uninhabitable.

A useful mental model: incoming sunlight is "let in", outgoing heat is "trapped on the way out". Both halves of that statement matter — and the second half is where most marks are won or lost.

*Why this is a natural process, not a problem in itself*

The greenhouse effect is not the same thing as global warming. The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary: it keeps the Earth at a temperature suitable for life. Global warming refers to additional warming caused by human activity increasing the concentration of greenhouse gases — that is covered in the next spec point. Many GCSE candidates conflate the two and lose marks by writing as if all greenhouse effect is bad.

Common misconception — what is actually absorbed?

This is the single biggest source of error on this spec point. The wording of the specification is deliberate:

> "…absorb heat radiated from the Earth…"

It is the heat that leaves the Earth's surface that gets absorbed — not the incoming sunlight. Sunlight passes through the atmosphere relatively easily because greenhouse gases do not absorb short-wavelength visible light. Only the longer-wavelength infrared that the Earth radiates outwards is absorbed.

Incorrect answers (which lose marks):

  • "Greenhouse gases trap heat from the Sun." → too vague and implies sunlight is trapped on the way in.
  • "Greenhouse gases stop heat escaping by reflecting it back to Earth." → reflection is not the mechanism; absorption and re-emission is.
  • "Greenhouse gases form a layer that prevents heat from leaving the atmosphere." → this is a blanket image; the gases are mixed throughout the lower atmosphere, not in a single reflective layer.

Correct answers should include:

  • The Earth's surface absorbs energy from the Sun and warms up.
  • The warm Earth radiates heat (infrared radiation) back outwards.
  • Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb this radiated heat.
  • The gases re-emit energy in all directions, including back towards the Earth.
  • This keeps the surface of the Earth warmer than it would otherwise be.

Why greenhouse gases absorb infrared but not visible light

This is universal subject knowledge that helps you write a clearer answer at Higher tier. You do not need to memorise it, but it helps explain why the sequence in step 5 above is selective.

Molecules absorb radiation when the radiation has the right energy to match a change in the molecule's bonds — for greenhouse gases, the right energies are in the infrared range, where bond vibrations and bending modes can be excited. Triatomic and polyatomic gases such as CO₂, CH₄ and H₂O have several bond-bending modes that match infrared photon energies. Simple diatomic molecules like N₂ and O₂ — the bulk of the atmosphere — do not have suitable modes, so they let infrared pass straight through. This is why the Earth's atmosphere can have a strong greenhouse effect even though greenhouse gases are only a small fraction of the total gas present.

GasGreenhouse gas?Why

Nitrogen (N₂)NoDiatomic; does not absorb infrared at the relevant wavelengths

This table is worth remembering because exam questions sometimes ask you to identify which gas in a list is a greenhouse gas. Nitrogen and oxygen — though the most abundant gases in the atmosphere — are not greenhouse gases.

Why methane matters even though there is far less of it

Even though atmospheric methane is present at much lower concentrations than CO₂ (a few parts per million vs around 420 ppm of CO₂), methane is a much stronger greenhouse gas per molecule because its bond-bending modes absorb infrared more effectively. That is why livestock farming, rice cultivation and landfill emissions matter — they may emit comparatively small masses of methane, but each molecule has a disproportionate warming effect.

You will not be tested on the precise "warming potential" multiplier at GCSE, but you should be able to say that methane is a "more powerful" or "stronger" greenhouse gas than CO₂ if asked.

The role of water vapour

Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas in the atmosphere and is responsible for the largest share of the natural greenhouse effect. However, its concentration in the atmosphere is controlled by temperature: warm air holds more water vapour, cold air holds less. Humans do not directly emit significant water vapour. Instead, water vapour acts as a feedback — if CO₂ raises the temperature, the warmer air holds more water vapour, which causes further warming. You do not need to discuss feedback loops at GCSE, but it is worth knowing that water vapour is named on the spec for a reason.

Evaluating the Evidence

Spec 8.25: Evaluate the evidence for human activity causing climate change, considering the correlation between change in atmospheric CO₂ concentration, fossil-fuel consumption and temperature change; and the uncertainties caused by location of measurements and historical accuracy.

This spec point is one of the few in 1CH0 that asks you to do something genuinely scientific — weigh evidence rather than recall it. Examiners use it to test whether you understand the difference between a pattern in data (correlation) and a confirmed cause-and-effect link (causation), and whether you can recognise the limitations of measurements made over long timescales and in different places.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CorrelationA statistical relationship between two variables — as one changes, the other changes in a predictable way (in the same direction = positive correlation; in opposite directions = negative correlation)
CausationA relationship where a change in one variable directly produces the change in the other
AnomalyThe difference between a measured value and a long-term average (used for global temperature data, e.g. "+1.1 °C above the 1850–1900 mean")
ppm (parts per million)A unit for trace concentrations: 1 ppm means 1 molecule of the gas per million molecules of air

---

What the evidence actually shows

Three datasets are central to the evaluation:

  1. Atmospheric CO₂ concentration over time — measured directly since 1958 at Mauna Loa, Hawaii, and reconstructed back hundreds of thousands of years using ice cores.
  2. Fossil-fuel consumption over time — recorded from industrial and government records since the start of the Industrial Revolution (~1750).
  3. Global mean temperature over time — measured directly using thermometers since around 1850, and reconstructed further back using proxy data.

When these three curves are plotted on the same time axis, they show a strong positive correlation: as fossil-fuel consumption has risen, atmospheric CO₂ has risen, and global mean temperature has risen. CO₂ concentration has increased from a pre-industrial value of around 280 ppm to over 420 ppm today — a rise of around 50% in roughly 270 years. Over the same period, global mean temperature has risen by approximately 1.1 °C.

*Why correlation alone is not proof*

Two variables can be correlated without one causing the other. Both might be caused by a third hidden factor, or the link could be a coincidence over the time range studied. To move from correlation to causation, scientists need a mechanism — a physical explanation of how one variable produces the change in the other.

For CO₂ and temperature, that mechanism exists: CO₂ absorbs infrared radiation emitted by the Earth's surface and re-emits some of it back downwards. This is the enhanced greenhouse effect. Without this mechanism, the correlation would be suggestive but not conclusive; with it, the case for causation is much stronger.

---

Where the historical CO₂ data comes from

Direct measurements of atmospheric CO₂ only go back to 1958. Anything earlier than that has to be reconstructed indirectly.

*Ice cores*

When snow falls on a glacier or ice sheet, air becomes trapped between the snowflakes. As more snow falls on top, the lower layers compact into ice, sealing tiny air bubbles inside. Each bubble is effectively a preserved sample of the atmosphere from the year the snow fell.

Scientists drill long cylinders of ice — ice cores — from places like Antarctica and Greenland. The deeper the ice, the older it is. By extracting the air bubbles and analysing the gas inside, the CO₂ concentration of past atmospheres can be measured. Ice cores from Antarctica have provided CO₂ records stretching back over 800,000 years.

*Why ice cores are reliable but not perfect*

The air trapped in bubbles was not sealed off instantly — it takes decades for snow to compact fully into ice, so the air in any given bubble represents an average of several decades, not a single year. This means short-term spikes in CO₂ may be smoothed out in the record. The age of the ice itself is dated by counting annual layers, similar to tree rings, but dating becomes less precise further down the core.

---

How historical temperatures are reconstructed

Direct temperature measurements began with the spread of mercury thermometers in the 17th–19th centuries, but global coverage only became reasonable from around 1850. For earlier periods, scientists use proxy data:

ProxyWhat it tells usLimitation
Tree ringsWider rings form in warmer, wetter years; narrower rings in cold or dry yearsAffected by rainfall as well as temperature; only useful for the last ~1,000–10,000 years
Ice cores (isotopes)Ratios of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16 in the ice depend on temperature when the snow fellIndirect — requires calibration; gives temperature at the ice site, not globally

Each proxy on its own has limitations, but when several methods agree on the same trend, confidence in the reconstruction increases. This is a key idea in the evaluation — multiple independent lines of evidence pointing the same way is stronger than any single dataset.

---

The Mauna Loa record

In 1958, Charles Keeling began continuous measurements of atmospheric CO₂ at the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii. The resulting graph — known as the Keeling Curve — is one of the most important pieces of climate evidence.

It shows two features clearly:

  • A long-term upward trend: CO₂ has risen from around 315 ppm in 1958 to over 420 ppm today.
  • An annual sawtooth pattern: CO₂ falls slightly in Northern Hemisphere spring/summer (when plants photosynthesise) and rises in autumn/winter (when respiration and decay dominate over photosynthesis).

*Why Mauna Loa was chosen*

Mauna Loa is a high-altitude site in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, far from major industrial sources, cities, and large forests. This means measurements there reflect well-mixed background atmospheric CO₂ rather than local pollution or local plant activity. A measurement taken next to a motorway or downwind of a power station would be much higher than the true global average.

---

Uncertainties — location and historical accuracy

Spec point 8.25(b) requires you to consider two specific sources of uncertainty.

*Location uncertainty*

CO₂ and temperature are not uniform across the planet. CO₂ varies between hemispheres (the Northern Hemisphere has more industry and more vegetation), between altitudes (lower near forests, higher near cities), and even between day and night. A single measurement site cannot represent the whole atmosphere. The solution is a global network of stations, but coverage was patchy for most of the 20th century and remains sparse in parts of Africa, the polar regions, and the open ocean.

Temperature varies even more strongly with location — by latitude, altitude, proximity to sea, and time of day. Land warms and cools faster than oceans, and the Arctic is warming several times faster than the global mean. A global mean temperature is calculated by combining thousands of station readings, weighted by area, and the result depends on how the gaps are filled.

*Historical accuracy uncertainty*

For data more than ~150 years old, almost everything is indirect:

  • Ice-core CO₂ is averaged over decades, not years.
  • Proxy temperatures depend on calibration assumptions.
  • Some proxies (tree rings) respond to rainfall as well as temperature.
  • Dating becomes less precise the further back you go.

Older thermometer records also have problems: instruments were less precise, weather stations changed location, and cities grew up around stations (the urban heat island effect can make a station appear to warm even if the wider region has not). Climate scientists correct for these effects, but the corrections themselves introduce uncertainty.

*Why uncertainty does not mean "we don't know"*

A common misreading of the evidence is to say "because there is uncertainty, the science is not settled". Uncertainty in science means the range of likely values, not "anything could be true". Even at the wide end of every uncertainty range, the trend remains the same — CO₂ is rising, temperature is rising, and the rise is unprecedented in at least 800,000 years.

---

Interpreting a CO₂-vs-temperature graph in the exam

A standard exam stimulus shows two curves on the same time axis — atmospheric CO₂ (ppm) and global temperature anomaly (°C) — typically from 1850 or 1900 to the present.

*How to read it step by step*

  1. Identify the axes — usually time on the x-axis, with two y-axes (one for CO₂ in ppm, one for temperature anomaly in °C).
  2. Describe the overall trend in each variable — e.g. "CO₂ has increased from ~290 ppm to ~420 ppm" and "temperature anomaly has increased from around −0.2 °C to +1.1 °C".
  3. Compare the shapes — both rise, and the rate of rise has accelerated in the most recent decades.
  4. State the type of correlation — positive correlation: as CO₂ rises, temperature rises.
  5. Identify any anomalies — short-term dips (e.g. cooler years following major volcanic eruptions) or temperature pauses despite rising CO₂. These show the climate system has other influences too.
  6. Evaluate — does the data on its own prove human activity caused the warming? Correlation supports the link; combined with the known greenhouse mechanism and the matching rise in fossil-fuel use, the evidence for human causation is strong.

*Worked example — calculating a rate of change*

If atmospheric CO₂ was 315 ppm in 1958 and 420 ppm in 2024:

  • Change in CO₂ = 420 − 315 = 105 ppm
  • Time elapsed = 2024 − 1958 = 66 years
  • Average rate of increase = 105 ÷ 66 ≈ 1.6 ppm per year

For comparison, ice-core data shows that during the last 10,000 years before the Industrial Revolution, CO₂ varied by only about 20 ppm in total — roughly 0.002 ppm per year. The current rate is around 800 times faster than the natural pre-industrial rate, which is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the modern rise is not part of a natural cycle.

---

Building an "evaluate" answer

The command word evaluate requires you to weigh evidence — give points for and against, then reach a justified conclusion. A strong answer typically includes:

  • The positive correlation between CO₂, fossil-fuel use, and temperature
  • The physical mechanism (greenhouse effect) linking CO₂ to warming
  • The rate of recent change compared to natural rates from ice cores
  • Acknowledgement of uncertainty — location coverage, proxy data, historical accuracy
  • A conclusion that the evidence, taken together, strongly supports human activity as the cause, despite individual uncertainties

A weaker answer just describes the graph without addressing the uncertainty part of the spec, or dismisses the link because correlation is not causation, without engaging with the mechanism.

Human Activity & Climate Change

Spec 8.26: Describe (a) the potential effects on the climate of increased levels of carbon dioxide and methane generated by human activity, including burning fossil fuels and livestock farming, and (b) that these effects may be mitigated, considering scale, risk and environmental implications.

This spec point sits on both Foundation and Higher tier papers and on both Separate and Combined Science. It is examined on Paper 2 only.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Climate changeA long-term shift in average weather patterns (especially temperature and rainfall) caused by changes in the composition of the atmosphere
Global warmingThe rise in the average surface temperature of the Earth caused by an enhanced greenhouse effect
Greenhouse gasA gas in the atmosphere that absorbs infrared radiation emitted by the Earth's surface, e.g. carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour
AnthropogenicCaused by human activity
Carbon footprintThe total amount of CO₂ and other greenhouse gases (often expressed as CO₂ equivalents) released over the full life cycle of a product, service or event
MitigationAn action that reduces the rate of climate change or limits its impact
Carbon capture and storage (CCS)The process of trapping CO₂ at the point of emission (e.g. a power station flue) and storing it underground, often in depleted oil or gas fields
AfforestationPlanting new forests on land that was not previously forested, to remove CO₂ from the atmosphere by photosynthesis

Why human activity is changing the climate

Carbon dioxide and methane both absorb infrared (IR) radiation emitted from the Earth's warmed surface. The energy absorbed by these gas molecules is re-emitted in all directions, including back down to the surface. This is the greenhouse effect, and it is a natural process that keeps the Earth warm enough to support life. The problem is not that the greenhouse effect exists — it is that human activity has raised the concentration of greenhouse gases far above pre-industrial levels, making the effect stronger. A stronger greenhouse effect means more IR is trapped, so the lower atmosphere warms further. This is sometimes called the enhanced greenhouse effect.

Main human sources of greenhouse gases

Methane is a much more powerful greenhouse gas than CO₂ on a per-molecule basis, but its concentration in the atmosphere is far lower and it breaks down faster, so the total contribution of CO₂ to warming is still greater. Both gases matter.

Potential effects of climate change on the planet

You need to be able to describe a range of effects — examiners typically want three or four distinct consequences for a higher-mark question, not just "the Earth gets hotter".

  • Rising global temperatures. Average surface temperatures have risen by around 1 °C since the late 19th century, and continue to rise. Local temperature extremes (heatwaves) become more common.
  • Melting of polar ice caps and glaciers. Ice that has accumulated over thousands of years melts faster than it can be replaced. This reduces the planet's reflectivity (its albedo), so more solar energy is absorbed by darker land and ocean, causing further warming — a positive feedback loop.
  • Rising sea levels. Sea levels rise for two reasons: meltwater from land-based ice (Greenland, Antarctica, mountain glaciers) flows into the oceans, and seawater expands as it warms (thermal expansion). Rising seas threaten low-lying coastal cities, small island nations, and important river deltas where many people live and grow food.
  • More frequent and severe extreme weather. A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, so when storms form they can release more rainfall, causing flooding. At the same time, other regions experience longer droughts and more intense heatwaves. Hurricanes and tropical storms can become more powerful.
  • Changes to ecosystems and habitats. Many species are adapted to a narrow temperature range. As local climates shift, some species cannot migrate fast enough, leading to local extinctions. Coral reefs are particularly vulnerable to ocean warming and acidification (caused when CO₂ dissolves in seawater).
  • Shifts in food production. Warmer temperatures and changed rainfall patterns alter where crops can be grown. Some regions become more productive; many become less productive, particularly in already-hot, drought-prone parts of the world.
  • Human health and migration impacts. Heatwaves cause excess deaths. Tropical diseases spread to new regions as their insect carriers (mosquitoes, ticks) move into newly warmed areas. Loss of habitable or farmable land drives migration.

Why these effects are described as "potential"

The Edexcel syllabus uses the word "potential" deliberately. Climate models predict a range of outcomes, and the exact scale and timing of each effect depends on how much greenhouse gas is emitted in the coming decades. Examiners do not want students to write "this will definitely happen by 2050" — write in terms of risk and likelihood instead.

"Mitigation" means taking action to reduce future warming or to reduce CO₂ already in the atmosphere. The spec asks you to weigh up each strategy by considering its scale, risk, and environmental implications.

When you are asked to evaluate a mitigation strategy, write about all three of the spec's criteria — scale, risk, and environmental implications:

  • Scale — Will it make a meaningful difference globally? Switching one bus to electric removes far less CO₂ than converting an entire national electricity grid.
  • Risk — Could it have unintended consequences? CCS could leak; nuclear has waste storage issues; biofuels can drive deforestation.
  • Environmental implications — Does the action have its own footprint? Building wind turbines requires steel and rare-earth metals; planting trees on the wrong land can disrupt local ecosystems.

Why no single strategy is enough

The scale of human CO₂ emissions is enormous — tens of billions of tonnes each year. No one mitigation strategy can solve climate change on its own. A realistic approach combines several actions: decarbonising electricity, cutting methane from agriculture and waste, capturing CO₂ where it cannot yet be avoided, and planting more forests. Examiners marking "Suggest" or "Evaluate" responses are looking for students who recognise this — strong answers acknowledge trade-offs rather than presenting a single strategy as a complete fix.

Common student mistakes

  • Confusing the greenhouse effect with the hole in the ozone layer. These are different problems — ozone depletion is caused by CFCs damaging the upper-atmosphere ozone layer that blocks UV, not by greenhouse gases warming the lower atmosphere.
  • Writing "global warming" when the question asks about "climate change". Climate change is broader — it includes changes in rainfall, storms and sea level as well as temperature.
  • Saying ice melting causes sea level rise without mentioning thermal expansion. Both mechanisms contribute, and a full-mark answer often expects both.
  • Listing strategies without weighing them up. Spec point 8.26b explicitly says "consider scale, risk and environmental implications". A list of bullet points without trade-offs will not score well on an "Evaluate" question.
  • Forgetting methane. CO₂ is the headline gas, but methane from livestock and waste is in the spec by name. Mentioning both gases and both sources (burning fossil fuels and livestock farming) is required for the descriptive half of the spec point.
