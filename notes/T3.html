Defining Acids & Bases

Spec 3.1: Recall that acids in solution are sources of hydrogen ions and alkalis in solution are sources of hydroxide ions. Foundation + Higher tier, examined on Paper 1.

This spec point introduces the ionic definition of acids and alkalis. Earlier in the course you met acids in everyday contexts (vinegar, stomach acid, sulfuric acid in car batteries), but at GCSE you now need to be able to say why a substance behaves like an acid or alkali in terms of the ions it releases when dissolved in water. This single idea underpins almost every reaction in Topic 3 — pH, neutralisation, indicators, acid + metal, acid + carbonate, and titration calculations all rest on it.

What an acid is

An acid is any substance that, when dissolved in water, releases hydrogen ions (H+) into solution. The H+ ion is what gives every acid its characteristic properties — its sour taste, its ability to turn blue litmus red, its low pH, and its reactions with metals, carbonates, and bases.

Take hydrochloric acid as a worked example. Pure hydrogen chloride, HCl, is a covalent molecular gas. When you dissolve it in water, the polar water molecules pull the H–Cl bond apart and the molecule splits into ions:

HCl (aq) → H+ (aq) + Cl− (aq)

The H+ ion is what makes the resulting solution acidic. The chloride ion (Cl−) is a "spectator" — it doesn't take part in the chemistry that makes the solution acidic. If you dissolved a different acid, such as nitric acid, you would still get H+ ions released, but the spectator ion would be different:

HNO₃ (aq) → H+ (aq) + NO₃− (aq)

Strictly, the H+ ion in water does not float around bare. Each H+ attaches itself to a water molecule to form the hydronium (or oxonium) ion, H₃O+. At GCSE you can simply write H+ and refer to "hydrogen ions" — but it is useful to know that when you write H+ (aq), what is really there is H₃O+ (aq). This is why "in solution" or the state symbol "(aq)" matters: the H+ definition only applies once the acid is dissolved in water.

A useful consequence of the H+ definition: an acid only behaves as an acid once it is dissolved in water. Pure, dry HCl gas is a covalent molecule and is not acidic in the everyday chemical sense — it cannot turn litmus paper red until water is present to release the H+ ions. This is why universal indicator paper needs to be damp.

What an alkali is

An alkali is a soluble base. Specifically, an alkali is any substance that, when dissolved in water, releases hydroxide ions (OH−) into solution. The OH− ion is what gives every alkali its characteristic properties — slippery feel, high pH (above 7), ability to turn red litmus blue, and reactions that neutralise acids.

The classic example is sodium hydroxide, NaOH. It is an ionic compound — even in solid form it already contains Na+ ions and OH− ions held together in a lattice. When you dissolve it in water, the lattice breaks up and the ions become free-moving in solution:

NaOH (aq) → Na+ (aq) + OH− (aq)

The OH− ion is what makes the resulting solution alkaline. The Na+ ion is a spectator. Other common alkalis behave the same way:

  • Potassium hydroxide: KOH (aq) → K+ (aq) + OH− (aq)
  • Calcium hydroxide: Ca(OH)₂ (aq) → Ca²+ (aq) + 2OH− (aq)
  • Lithium hydroxide: LiOH (aq) → Li+ (aq) + OH− (aq)

Ammonia is a slightly trickier case. Ammonia, NH₃, doesn't contain any OH− groups in its formula, but it produces OH− ions when it dissolves in water by reacting with water itself:

NH₃ (aq) + H₂O (l) ⇌ NH₄+ (aq) + OH− (aq)

It is still an alkali because the solution that forms contains OH− ions.

The difference between a base, an alkali, and "soluble in water"

This is a distinction many students miss. Every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali.

A base is the broader category: any substance that can neutralise an acid by reacting with H+ ions to form water. Bases include:

  • Metal oxides (e.g. copper(II) oxide, CuO; magnesium oxide, MgO)
  • Metal hydroxides (e.g. sodium hydroxide, NaOH; copper(II) hydroxide, Cu(OH)₂)
  • Ammonia, NH₃

An alkali is the special subset of bases that dissolve in water. Copper(II) oxide is a base — it neutralises sulfuric acid to make copper sulfate — but it is not an alkali, because it doesn't dissolve in water. You can't measure its pH directly because you can't make an aqueous solution of it.

Out of the common metal hydroxides, only the Group 1 hydroxides (NaOH, KOH, LiOH) and ammonia solution are properly water-soluble. Group 2 hydroxides such as calcium hydroxide are only slightly soluble (limewater is a dilute Ca(OH)₂ solution). Transition metal hydroxides (e.g. Cu(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₃) are essentially insoluble — they are bases but not alkalis. This distinction is developed further in 3.1.5.1 Bases.

Neutralisation in ionic terms

The H+/OH− definitions matter most when you write a neutralisation reaction. Combine any acid with any alkali, and the underlying ionic equation is always the same:

H+ (aq) + OH− (aq) → H₂O (l)

That single equation is the chemical heart of the whole topic. It explains:

  • Why mixing equal amounts of acid and alkali gives a neutral solution (pH 7): all the H+ has been used up reacting with all the OH−
  • Why the pH rises when you add alkali to an acid: H+ ions are being removed (used up by reacting with OH−), so there are fewer of them left in solution
  • Why every acid + alkali reaction produces water as one of the products
  • Why the resulting "salt" (e.g. NaCl from HCl + NaOH) is made of the spectator ions — they're the only ones left when the H+ and OH− have combined to make water

When an exam question asks you to "explain in terms of particles why the pH increases when an alkali is added to an acid", the gold-standard answer references H+ ions reacting with OH− ions to form water (or "being neutralised"), not just "there are more OH− ions" or "there are fewer H+ ions". This is exactly the point examiners flagged in June 2019 — see Examiner Report Insights below.

3.1.1.2 The pH Scale F+H ▼

The pH Scale

Spec point 3.2 (Foundation + Higher tier · 1CH0 + 1SC0 · Paper 1): Recall that a neutral solution has a pH of 7 and that acidic solutions have lower pH values and alkaline solutions higher pH values.

The pH scale is a numerical scale used to measure how acidic or alkaline an aqueous solution is. It is one of the most important quantitative tools in chemistry, and it appears throughout the GCSE course — in acid–base reactions, neutralisation, electrolysis, indicators, salt preparation, and the rates and equilibria of reactions involving acids. At Foundation and Higher tier you are expected to recall the basic structure of the scale, identify whether a substance is acidic, neutral, or alkaline from a pH value, and link the pH scale to indicator colour changes and the concept of neutralisation.

Neutral solutionA solution with a pH value of exactly 7 (e.g. pure water, sodium chloride solution)
Alkaline solutionA solution with a pH value higher than 7
AcidA substance that, when dissolved in water, releases hydrogen ions (H⁺) into solution
AlkaliA soluble base that, when dissolved in water, releases hydroxide ions (OH⁻) into solution
IndicatorA substance that changes colour depending on the pH of the solution it is added to

The structure of the pH scale

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14 for the solutions you will meet at GCSE. Each whole number on the scale corresponds to a different level of acidity or alkalinity:

pH valueNature of solutionExamples
0Strongly acidicConcentrated hydrochloric acid, concentrated sulfuric acid
1–3AcidicStomach acid (pH ~2), lemon juice, vinegar
4–6Weakly acidicRainwater, black coffee, milk (slightly acidic)
7NeutralPure water, sodium chloride solution
8–10Weakly alkalineBaking soda solution, seawater (~pH 8)
11–13AlkalineSoap solution, oven cleaner
14Strongly alkalineConcentrated sodium hydroxide solution

The numbers themselves are not arbitrary — they reflect the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in the solution. The lower the pH, the higher the concentration of H⁺ ions, and the more acidic the solution is. The higher the pH, the lower the concentration of H⁺ ions (and the higher the concentration of OH⁻ ions), and the more alkaline the solution is. The detailed link between pH and ion concentration is covered in 3.1.2.1 Hydrogen Ions & pH — for this spec point you simply need to recall the structure of the scale itself.

Why pH 7 is neutral

A neutral solution has equal concentrations of H⁺ ions and OH⁻ ions. Pure water is the standard example: a very small fraction of water molecules naturally split (dissociate) into H⁺ and OH⁻ ions, and because exactly one H⁺ is produced for every one OH⁻, the two cancel out and the solution is neutral.

A neutral solution does not have to be pure water. A salt solution like sodium chloride (NaCl) dissolved in water is also neutral with a pH of 7. This is because when an acid reacts with the corresponding alkali in equal amounts, the H⁺ from the acid and the OH⁻ from the alkali combine to make water, leaving only a neutral salt solution behind. This idea — that acid + alkali → salt + water — is the basis of neutralisation.

This is a point that examiners commonly test by asking students to give the pH of a salt solution. For example:

  • 1CH0/1F — November 2020 Examiner Report (Q1(c)(ii)): Candidates were asked, "Sodium chloride solution is neutral. Give the pH of this solution" (1 mark). The expected answer is simply 7.

If you are asked to give the pH of a neutral solution, the answer is always 7 — not "around 7" or "close to 7". State the exact value.

You must be confident at quickly identifying whether a pH value is acidic, neutral, or alkaline. A common exam style is to give a list of pH values (for example, pH 3, pH 7, pH 10, pH 13) and ask you to pick the most acidic, the alkaline solutions, or the neutral one.

Strong and weak — pH at the extremes

Although you only need to recall that acids have pH below 7 and alkalis have pH above 7, it helps your understanding to recognise the difference between the ends of the scale and the middle:

  • A pH of 0 or 1 indicates a strong, concentrated acid — there is a high concentration of H⁺ ions in solution.
  • A pH of 5 or 6 indicates a weakly acidic solution — there are still more H⁺ than OH⁻ ions, but only slightly.
  • A pH of 8 or 9 indicates a weakly alkaline solution.
  • A pH of 13 or 14 indicates a strong, concentrated alkali — there is a high concentration of OH⁻ ions in solution.

The full distinction between strong acids/alkalis (which fully ionise in water) and weak acids/alkalis (which only partially ionise) is a Higher tier idea covered in 3.1.2.1 Hydrogen Ions & pH. For this spec point you simply need to recall the scale and which end is acidic and which is alkaline.

(Higher tier) At Higher tier you should also know that pH is a logarithmic scale: each step of 1 pH unit represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. A solution at pH 2 has ten times more H⁺ ions than a solution at pH 3, and one hundred times more H⁺ ions than a solution at pH 4. This means the difference between pH 1 and pH 3 is much larger than the gap between the numbers suggests.

Measuring the pH of a solution

There are two main ways to measure the pH of a solution at GCSE:

*1. Indicators*

A chemical indicator changes colour at a specific pH. Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators that produces a continuous colour change across the whole pH scale, from red (strong acid) through orange, yellow, green (neutral), blue, to purple (strong alkali). You add a few drops to the solution (or use universal indicator paper) and compare the colour to a chart printed with the corresponding pH values. The detail of how individual indicators behave is covered in 3.1.1.3 Indicators.

*2. pH probe / pH meter*

An electronic pH probe is dipped into the solution and gives a numerical pH reading directly on a digital display. This is more accurate than using indicators because:

  • It gives an exact numerical value, not a colour you have to interpret.
  • It does not depend on the observer judging shades of colour.
  • It works on coloured solutions where indicators would be hard to read.

The practical work of measuring and comparing pH values is covered in 3.1.3.1 Core Practical: Investigating pH.

3.1.1.3 Indicators F+H ▼

Indicators

Spec point 3.3: Recall the effect of acids and alkalis on indicators, including litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein. Foundation + Higher tier, 1CH0 + 1SC0, Paper 1.

An indicator is a substance that changes colour to show whether a solution is acidic, neutral, or alkaline. Indicators are weak acids or weak bases whose protonated and deprotonated forms have different colours, so the colour you see depends on the concentration of H⁺ (aq) or OH⁻ (aq) in the solution. For GCSE Edexcel you need to recall the colour changes of three named indicators — litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein — plus the broader behaviour of universal indicator when used to estimate pH.

NeutralA solution with a pH of exactly 7 (e.g. pure water) — neither acidic nor alkaline

Universal indicatorA mixture of several different indicators that gives a continuous range of colours from red through green to purple across the pH scale

The three named indicators

Edexcel names three indicators by spec — you must learn each colour change in both directions (acid → alkali and alkali → acid).

A few practical points sit behind that table:

  • Litmus is the only one of the three that gives a distinct intermediate colour (purple) in neutral solution. It is often supplied as litmus paper — either red or blue. Red litmus paper turns blue in alkali; blue litmus paper turns red in acid. Litmus paper is useful for a quick yes/no acidity test but it does not tell you how strong the acid or alkali is.
  • Methyl orange changes from red in acid to yellow in alkali, passing through orange around pH 4. It is therefore well suited to titrations of a strong acid against a strong base, or a strong acid against a weak base — the end-point lies on the acidic side.
  • Phenolphthalein changes from colourless in acid to pink in alkali, with the change happening around pH 9. It is well suited to titrations of a strong acid against a strong base, or a weak acid against a strong base — the end-point lies on the alkaline side.

For Edexcel GCSE the level of detail expected is the colour change itself, not the exact pH at which the change occurs. You should still know that methyl orange and phenolphthalein change at different pH values because that is why two different indicators are used for different titrations.

Why indicators change colour

An indicator molecule exists in two forms — a protonated form (with an extra H⁺) and a deprotonated form (without it). The two forms absorb different wavelengths of visible light, so they look different colours.

  • In an acidic solution there is a high concentration of H⁺ (aq), which pushes the indicator equilibrium toward the protonated form. You see the "acid colour" (red for litmus, red for methyl orange, colourless for phenolphthalein).
  • In an alkaline solution there is a high concentration of OH⁻ (aq) which removes H⁺ from the indicator, pushing the equilibrium toward the deprotonated form. You see the "alkali colour" (blue for litmus, yellow for methyl orange, pink for phenolphthalein).

You do not need to draw the equilibrium for GCSE, but understanding that the colour depends on whether the indicator has picked up or given up an H⁺ explains why the same indicator changes colour in opposite directions when you add acid versus alkali.

Universal indicator and the pH scale

Universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators in a single solution (or impregnated into universal indicator paper). Because each of the component indicators changes at a different pH, the mixture shows a continuous range of colours across the pH scale rather than a single colour change.

Worked examples — describing colour changes in exam answers

Edexcel mark schemes for indicator colour-change questions are strict on wording. You normally need to give both the starting colour and the final colour — saying only "it turns pink" without saying it started colourless will usually not be enough for two marks.

*Worked example 1 — methyl orange in a titration of HCl with NaOH (acid in flask, alkali added from burette).*

Before the alkali is added, the flask contains hydrochloric acid plus methyl orange — the solution is red. As sodium hydroxide is added, the solution becomes neutral and then alkaline at the end-point. The colour change at the end-point is:

red → (orange) → yellow

A 2-mark answer should say: "The solution changes from red to yellow" (or "from red, through orange, to yellow"). A 1-mark answer might only state the final colour.

*Worked example 2 — phenolphthalein in a titration of NaOH with HCl (alkali in flask, acid added from burette).*

Before the acid is added, the flask contains sodium hydroxide plus phenolphthalein — the solution is pink. As hydrochloric acid is added, the solution becomes neutral at the end-point. The colour change is:

pink → colourless

A 1-mark answer is simply "The pink colour disappears" or "It changes from pink to colourless".

*Worked example 3 — adding universal indicator to lemon juice.*

Lemon juice contains citric acid and has a pH of about 2–3. Universal indicator added to lemon juice would turn red or orange, showing that the solution is strongly to moderately acidic. A student answer that says "it would turn red, showing the lemon juice is acidic" gets full marks; an answer that says only "it would turn red" without linking the colour to acidity often loses the explanation mark.

Exam Technique & Traps

A few patterns recur in indicator questions that you should plan for:

  • Give both colours when asked for a colour change. "Colourless to pink" is a full answer; "pink" on its own usually loses a mark.
  • Match the indicator to the right pH range. If the question gives you methyl orange and the answer expects yellow in alkali, do not write "blue" — that is litmus. Cross-contamination of indicator colours is a common slip.
  • Litmus does not measure pH. If an exam question asks how to measure the pH of a solution accurately, the answer is a pH probe (or pH meter). If it asks how to estimate pH, universal indicator is acceptable. Litmus paper only ever tells you acid/neutral/alkali.
  • Universal indicator does not give a sharp end-point. It is a mixture of indicators, so the colour change is gradual rather than sharp. That is why titrations use a single indicator (methyl orange or phenolphthalein), not universal indicator.
  • State the colour in the context of acid/alkali, not just the colour. If asked to explain what the colour tells you, link the colour to the pH or to acid/alkali — e.g. "the solution turned red, so the lemon juice is acidic" rather than just "the solution turned red".

  4. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in alkali, changing around pH 9 — used in titrations where the end-point is alkaline.

3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules F+H ▼

Solubility Rules

Spec points 3.19 and 3.20 require you to recall the five general solubility rules and predict whether mixing two named solutions will produce a precipitate — and if so, to name it. This is a Foundation + Higher tier topic on Paper 1, examined for both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

These rules are deceptively simple to state but underpin a huge amount of practical chemistry at GCSE: they tell you which salt-preparation method to use, they let you write ionic equations for precipitation, and they explain why some reactions appear to "go to completion" while others do not. Get the table below into long-term memory — almost every Paper 1 question on salts or qualitative analysis assumes you know it.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
SolubleA substance that dissolves in water to form an aqueous solution; in equations it is labelled with the state symbol (aq)
InsolubleA substance that does not dissolve in water; in equations it is labelled with the state symbol (s) and will appear as a solid suspended in the liquid
PrecipitateAn insoluble solid that forms when two soluble solutions are mixed; it appears as a suspension or settles out, and is usually written with the (s) state symbol
Precipitation reactionA reaction in which two aqueous solutions combine to form at least one insoluble product (a precipitate) and an aqueous product
Spectator ionsIons that are present in solution before and after the reaction but do not take part in forming the precipitate — they are omitted from the ionic equation

The Five Solubility Rules (central reference table)

Learn this verbatim. Edexcel examiners assume you can recall it without prompting, and several question types require you to apply more than one rule in the same answer.

RuleSolubleInsoluble Exceptions
aAll common sodium (Na⁺), potassium (K⁺) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) salts are solubleNone — no exceptions
bAll nitrates (NO₃⁻) are solubleNone — no exceptions
cCommon chlorides (Cl⁻) are solubleSilver chloride (AgCl) and lead chloride (PbCl₂) are insoluble
dCommon sulfates (SO₄²⁻) are solubleLead sulfate (PbSO₄), barium sulfate (BaSO₄) and calcium sulfate (CaSO₄) are insoluble
eCarbonates (CO₃²⁻) and hydroxides (OH⁻) are insoluble except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium (which are soluble)All other carbonates and hydroxides are insoluble

Memory aid: rules a and b are universal positives (no exceptions). Rule e is inverted — it is a list of insoluble compounds with three soluble exceptions. Most students get caught out on rule e because the polarity is flipped relative to a–d.

How to Use the Rules to Predict a Precipitate (3.20)

When two aqueous solutions are mixed, the cations and anions can swap partners. There are two possible new products. To predict whether a precipitate forms:

  1. Write the two reactants with their state symbols (both aqueous).
  2. Swap the partners to identify the two possible products.
  3. Check each product against the five rules.
  4. If either product is insoluble, that compound is the precipitate.
  5. If both products are soluble, no reaction occurs — the ions remain in solution.

Worked Example 1 — Silver Chloride

Mix: silver nitrate solution + sodium chloride solution.

*Step 1 — write the reactants:*

AgNO₃ (aq) + NaCl (aq) → ?

Step 2 — swap partners. Possible products: silver chloride (AgCl) and sodium nitrate (NaNO₃).

*Step 3 — apply the rules:*

  • AgCl — rule c says chlorides are soluble except silver and lead → AgCl is insoluble
  • NaNO₃ — rule a (sodium salt) and rule b (nitrate) both say soluble → NaNO₃ is soluble

*Step 4 — full balanced equation:*

AgNO₃ (aq) + NaCl (aq) → AgCl (s) + NaNO₃ (aq)

Step 5 — ionic equation (Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectator ions and are cancelled):

Ag⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) → AgCl (s)

The precipitate is silver chloride — a white solid that darkens to grey on exposure to light. This is the basis of the qualitative test for chloride ions.

Worked Example 2 — Barium Sulfate

Mix: barium chloride solution + sodium sulfate solution.

BaCl₂ (aq) + Na₂SO₄ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s) + 2NaCl (aq)

Apply the rules:

  • BaSO₄ — rule d, barium sulfate is one of the three insoluble sulfates → precipitate
  • NaCl — rule a (sodium) and rule c (chloride, not Ag or Pb) → soluble

*Ionic equation:*

Ba²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s)

The precipitate is barium sulfate — a dense white solid. This is the basis of the qualitative test for sulfate ions (acidified barium chloride or barium nitrate is used as the reagent).

Worked Example 3 — Calcium Carbonate

Mix: calcium chloride solution + sodium carbonate solution.

CaCl₂ (aq) + Na₂CO₃ (aq) → CaCO₃ (s) + 2NaCl (aq)

  • CaCO₃ — rule e, carbonates are insoluble except Na/K/NH₄ → precipitate
  • NaCl — soluble (rules a + c)

*Ionic equation:*

Ca²⁺ (aq) + CO₃²⁻ (aq) → CaCO₃ (s)

The precipitate is calcium carbonate — a white solid (the same compound that makes up chalk, limestone and marble). This reaction is one explanation for the "scale" that forms in kettles in hard-water areas.

Worked Example 4 — Copper Hydroxide

Mix: copper sulfate solution + sodium hydroxide solution.

CuSO₄ (aq) + 2NaOH (aq) → Cu(OH)₂ (s) + Na₂SO₄ (aq)

  • Cu(OH)₂ — rule e, hydroxides are insoluble except Na/K/NH₄ → precipitate
  • Na₂SO₄ — soluble (rule a)

*Ionic equation:*

Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2OH⁻ (aq) → Cu(OH)₂ (s)

The precipitate is copper(II) hydroxide — a pale blue gelatinous solid. The colour is itself a clue to the identity of the metal ion, and forms part of the qualitative test for Cu²⁺.

Why These Rules Matter — Choosing a Salt Preparation Method

A frequent exam scenario asks you to choose how to prepare a named salt. The solubility of the salt being made dictates the method:

Target saltSolubilityMethod
Soluble salt of Na, K or NH₄ (e.g. NaCl, KNO₃)Soluble (rule a)Titration of the alkali + acid — no excess base to filter off
Other soluble salt (e.g. CuSO₄, ZnCl₂)SolubleInsoluble-base method — add excess insoluble base/carbonate to the acid, filter, then crystallise
Insoluble salt (e.g. AgCl, BaSO₄, PbI₂)InsolublePrecipitation — mix two soluble solutions, filter the precipitate, wash and dry

You cannot pick the right method without first applying the solubility rules. That is why this SP underpins the whole salt-preparation family: see 3.1.9.1 Prepare a Salt by Titration and 3.1.11.1 Preparing an Insoluble Salt for the practical procedures.

Writing Ionic Equations from Precipitation Reactions

The full equation shows every species. The ionic equation strips out the spectator ions — those that appear unchanged on both sides — to leave just the ions that combine to form the precipitate.

Procedure:

  1. Write the full balanced equation with state symbols.
  2. Split every aqueous compound into its ions (e.g. NaCl (aq) → Na⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq)).
  3. Leave solids (precipitates), liquids and gases as molecules.
  4. Cancel any ion that appears identically on both sides.
  5. What remains is the ionic equation.

For all four worked examples above the spectator ions are the Na⁺ (or K⁺/NH₄⁺) cation and the nitrate, chloride or sulfate anion that came in with it — depending on which soluble reagent you chose. The ionic equation always reduces to the cation + anion of the precipitate combining in their correct ratio.

3.1.11.1 Preparing an Insoluble Salt F+H ▼

Preparing an Insoluble Salt

Spec 3.21 — Describe the method used to prepare a pure, dry sample of an insoluble salt. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry and Combined Science, Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Insoluble saltA salt that does not dissolve in water — when its ions meet in solution, they join to form a solid
PrecipitateAn insoluble solid that forms when two soluble solutions are mixed and their ions combine
Precipitation reactionA reaction between two aqueous solutions that produces an insoluble solid (precipitate) plus a soluble salt
FiltrateThe liquid that passes through the filter paper — contains the soluble products and any unreacted soluble starting material
ResidueThe solid left on the filter paper — for this preparation, the residue is the insoluble salt you want
Distilled waterWater with dissolved ions removed; used for washing so no new ions contaminate the salt

Why precipitation is needed for insoluble salts

Most salts — sodium chloride, potassium nitrate, copper sulfate, magnesium sulfate — are soluble. You can make them by reacting an acid with a metal, a base, or a carbonate, then evaporating the solution to crystallise the salt. That whole route only works because the salt itself dissolves in the reaction mixture and stays in solution until you choose to crystallise it.

For an insoluble salt the moment its two ions meet in water they drop straight out of solution as a solid. There is no acid + base combination that will keep an insoluble salt dissolved long enough to neutralise, evaporate and crystallise. Instead, you take advantage of the fact that the salt is insoluble: you deliberately mix two soluble solutions whose ions combine to give the insoluble product, then physically separate that solid from the surrounding liquid by filtration.

You decide which salts need this method by using the solubility rules from 3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules. Any salt that is listed as insoluble — for example most carbonates, most hydroxides, lead halides, silver halides, barium sulfate — must be made by precipitation.

The four-step method

The standard preparation has the same four steps for every insoluble salt. Learn them as a sequence:

*Step 1 — Mix two soluble solutions that contain the correct ions*

Choose two soluble salts: one supplies the positive ion you want in the final product, the other supplies the negative ion. Both starting solutions must be aqueous so the ions are free to move and meet. Mix them in a beaker, stirring with a glass rod. The insoluble salt forms immediately as a cloudy suspension of fine solid throughout the liquid.

*Step 2 — Filter the mixture*

Set up a filter funnel lined with filter paper over a conical flask. Pour the suspension through. The fine solid (your insoluble salt) is trapped on the filter paper as the residue. The water plus the soluble by-product salt and any excess reactant pass through as the filtrate, which is discarded.

*Step 3 — Wash the residue with distilled water*

The residue on the filter paper is still wet with filtrate, so it carries traces of the soluble by-product and any excess reactant clinging to it. Pour a small volume of distilled water over the residue (while it is still in the filter funnel) and let it drain through. This rinses the soluble contaminants away. You must use distilled water, not tap water, because tap water contains dissolved ions (calcium, magnesium, chloride, hydrogencarbonate) that would contaminate the salt.

*Step 4 — Leave to dry*

Carefully lift the damp filter paper out of the funnel and place it on a watch glass. Allow the residue to dry. Three acceptable drying options:

  • Leave on the watch glass in a warm oven at low temperature
  • Place in a desiccator (sealed container with a drying agent such as anhydrous calcium chloride)
  • Leave on the bench to air-dry at room temperature

The result is a pure, dry sample of the insoluble salt.

Worked example 1 — Barium sulfate (BaSO₄)

Barium sulfate is insoluble (sulfates are generally soluble, but barium sulfate is an exception — see 3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules). To make a pure dry sample:

Reactants chosen: barium chloride solution (supplies Ba²⁺) and sodium sulfate solution (supplies SO₄²⁻). Both are soluble.

*Full balanced equation:*

BaCl₂ (aq) + Na₂SO₄ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s) + 2 NaCl (aq)

Ionic equation (the soluble spectator ions Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are removed):

Ba²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s)

*Procedure:*

  1. Mix equal volumes of barium chloride solution and sodium sulfate solution in a beaker. A white precipitate of barium sulfate forms.
  2. Filter the mixture using a filter funnel and filter paper. The white solid (BaSO₄) collects on the paper; the filtrate contains sodium chloride solution.
  3. Wash the residue with distilled water to remove traces of sodium chloride.
  4. Dry the residue — in a warm oven, a desiccator, or by air-drying.

Worked example 2 — Lead(II) iodide (PbI₂)

Lead iodide is a striking bright-yellow insoluble salt (lead halides are insoluble — see 3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules).

Reactants chosen: lead(II) nitrate solution (supplies Pb²⁺) and potassium iodide solution (supplies I⁻). Both are soluble.

*Full balanced equation:*

Pb(NO₃)₂ (aq) + 2 KI (aq) → PbI₂ (s) + 2 KNO₃ (aq)

*Ionic equation:*

Pb²⁺ (aq) + 2 I⁻ (aq) → PbI₂ (s)

Procedure: identical to the four-step method — mix, filter, wash with distilled water, dry. The residue is pure yellow lead(II) iodide.

Choosing the right reactants

For any insoluble salt, you need two soluble salts that between them provide the right cation and anion. A useful checklist:

  • The cation source must be a soluble compound of that metal. Nitrates are always soluble (a reliable cation source). For sodium, potassium, ammonium, the chloride or sulfate works too.
  • The anion source must be a soluble compound containing that anion. Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts of almost any anion are soluble (a reliable anion source).
  • The two by-product ions (e.g. Na⁺ and NO₃⁻, or K⁺ and Cl⁻) must form a soluble salt that stays in the filtrate.

If you accidentally chose two reactants whose by-product was also insoluble, you would get two precipitates mixed together and could not separate them by filtration alone.

Comparing the three salt-preparation methods

The choice of preparation depends on whether the salt is soluble, and if so, what reactants are available. The table below compares all three methods you need to know:

MethodWhen to useReactantsSeparationFinal step
Acid + insoluble base/metal/carbonate (see 3.1.8.1 Core Practical: Preparing Copper Sulfate)Salt is soluble AND can be made from an insoluble reactantAcid + insoluble base/metal/metal carbonate (use excess solid)Filter off excess solid reactantEvaporate filtrate to crystallise the salt

The key signal that tells you which method to choose is the solubility of the target salt. If the salt is insoluble (as predicted by 3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules), the precipitation method is the only route that works.

Common pitfalls

  • Using tap water to wash the residue. Tap water contains dissolved Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻ ions. These ions stick to the solid as it dries and contaminate the salt. Always specify distilled (or deionised) water in your written method.
  • Skipping the wash step. Without washing, the residue is contaminated by the soluble by-product trapped between particles. The salt looks fine but is impure.
  • Trying to evaporate instead of filter. If you evaporated the mixture instead of filtering, the soluble by-product would crystallise alongside your insoluble salt and you would have a mixture, not a pure sample. Filtration is essential because it physically separates the insoluble solid from everything else.
  • Drying at too high a temperature. Some insoluble salts (especially hydroxides and carbonates) decompose if heated strongly. Gentle warming in an oven, a desiccator, or air-drying are the safe options.
  • Confusing residue and filtrate. For this preparation the desired product is the residue on the filter paper — the opposite of what you do in a soluble-salt preparation, where the desired product is dissolved in the filtrate.

Key Takeaways
  1. An insoluble salt cannot be made by neutralising an acid in solution — you must use a precipitation reaction between two soluble salts.
  2. Identify whether a salt is insoluble using the solubility rules from 3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules — only then choose the precipitation method.
  3. Step 1 — mix two aqueous solutions whose ions combine to give the insoluble salt as a precipitate.
  4. Step 2 — filter the mixture; the precipitate is the residue on the filter paper, the soluble by-product is in the filtrate.
  5. Step 3 — wash the residue with distilled water (not tap water) to remove soluble impurities.
  6. Step 4 — dry the residue in a warm oven, a desiccator, or in air to obtain a pure, dry sample.
  7. Example: BaCl₂ (aq) + Na₂SO₄ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s) + 2 NaCl (aq); ionic equation Ba²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s).
  8. Example: Pb(NO₃)₂ (aq) + 2 KI (aq) → PbI₂ (s) + 2 KNO₃ (aq); ionic equation Pb²⁺ (aq) + 2 I⁻ (aq) → PbI₂ (s).
  9. Choose reactants so that both starting solutions are soluble AND the by-product remains soluble in the filtrate — usually a sodium, potassium, ammonium or nitrate salt is a safe partner.
  10. Distinguish the three salt-preparation routes: acid + insoluble reactant (filter excess, evaporate filtrate); titration (find exact volumes, evaporate); precipitation (filter, wash, dry).
  11. Never use tap water for washing — it contains ions that would contaminate the pure salt.
  12. Typical exam ask: "Describe" question, 2–4 marks, one mark per step (mix / filter / wash / dry), often coupled with obtaining the soluble by-product by evaporation.
3.1.2.1 Hydrogen Ions & pH H only ▼

Hydrogen Ions & pH

Spec 3.4: Recall that the higher the concentration of hydrogen ions in an acidic solution, the lower the pH; and the higher the concentration of hydroxide ions in an alkaline solution, the higher the pH.

Spec 3.5: Recall that as hydrogen ion concentration in a solution increases by a factor of 10, the pH of the solution decreases by 1.

This spec point is Higher tier only and is examined exclusively on Paper 1 (1CH0/1H). It links the qualitative relationship between ion concentration and pH (spec 3.4) with the quantitative factor-of-10 rule that allows you to convert between pH and [H⁺] (spec 3.5).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Hydrogen ion (H⁺)A hydrogen atom that has lost its single electron, leaving a bare proton. The species responsible for acidic behaviour in aqueous solution
Hydroxide ion (OH⁻)A negatively charged ion made of one oxygen and one hydrogen atom. The species responsible for alkaline behaviour in aqueous solution
pHA numerical scale (typically 0–14) that measures how acidic or alkaline an aqueous solution is, based on the concentration of hydrogen ions
ConcentrationThe amount of dissolved substance per unit volume of solution, usually measured in mol/dm³
Acidic solutionA solution in which [H⁺] is greater than [OH⁻]; pH below 7
Alkaline solutionA solution in which [OH⁻] is greater than [H⁺]; pH above 7
Neutral solutionA solution in which [H⁺] equals [OH⁻]; pH equal to 7

Why hydrogen ions control pH

Acids in water release hydrogen ions. For example, hydrochloric acid ionises completely:

HCl (aq) → H⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq)

The hydrogen ions, H⁺ (aq), are what give the solution its acidic character. The more of these ions per dm³ of solution, the more strongly acidic the solution behaves — it tastes more sour, reacts faster with reactive metals, and turns indicators a deeper red. Because we need a compact way to talk about [H⁺] values that span many orders of magnitude (from around 1 mol/dm³ in concentrated acid down to 0.00000000000001 mol/dm³ in concentrated alkali), chemists use the pH scale.

The pH scale is constructed so that:

  • Low pH (close to 0) = high [H⁺] = strongly acidic
  • pH 7 = [H⁺] equal to [OH⁻] = neutral (pure water at room temperature)
  • High pH (close to 14) = low [H⁺] (and high [OH⁻]) = strongly alkaline

This is why the relationship in spec 3.4 is inverse: as [H⁺] goes up, pH goes down. Many students intuitively expect "more ions = bigger number", which is the trap — pH is a measure of acidity, and the more acidic a solution, the smaller the pH value.

Why [OH⁻] also matters

In any aqueous solution, water itself ionises very slightly:

H₂O (l) ⇌ H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq)

Because of this equilibrium, [H⁺] and [OH⁻] are linked. Whenever one goes up, the other goes down. So when you add a base (such as sodium hydroxide) and increase [OH⁻], the [H⁺] in that solution must fall — and the pH rises. This is the second half of spec 3.4: higher [OH⁻] → higher pH.

This explains why the same pH scale describes both acidity and alkalinity: it is really one scale that tracks [H⁺], and because [OH⁻] is tied to [H⁺] through the water equilibrium, knowing one tells you the other.

The factor-of-10 rule (spec 3.5)

The pH scale is logarithmic. Each unit of pH represents a factor of 10 change in hydrogen ion concentration. You do not need to know logarithms or use a log button on your calculator for GCSE — but you must be fluent with the factor-of-10 reasoning.

The rule, in both directions:

  • pH decreases by 1 → [H⁺] increases by a factor of 10 (×10)
  • pH increases by 1 → [H⁺] decreases by a factor of 10 (÷10)
  • A pH change of n units → an [H⁺] change of 10ⁿ
Change in pHFactor change in [H⁺]

Worked example 1 — pH falls from 5 to 2

A solution has its pH lowered from 5 to 2 by adding more acid. By what factor has the hydrogen ion concentration changed?

*Step 1 — Find the change in pH.*

Change = 5 − 2 = 3 units lower.

*Step 2 — Apply the factor-of-10 rule.*

Each pH unit corresponds to a factor of 10 in [H⁺]. Three units lower means [H⁺] has increased by:

10 × 10 × 10 = 10³ = 1000 times

Answer: The hydrogen ion concentration has increased by a factor of 1000.

Worked example 2 — pH rises from 2 to 6

In a neutralisation experiment, the pH of a mixture rises from 2 to 6. By what factor has the hydrogen ion concentration changed?

*Step 1 — Find the change in pH.*

Change = 6 − 2 = 4 units higher.

*Step 2 — Apply the factor-of-10 rule.*

Each pH unit corresponds to a factor of 10 in [H⁺]. Four units higher means [H⁺] has decreased by:

10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10⁴ = 10,000 times

Answer: The hydrogen ion concentration has decreased by a factor of 10,000.

Worked example 3 — comparing two acids

Acid X has pH 1. Acid Y has pH 2. How many times greater is the hydrogen ion concentration in acid X compared with acid Y?

*Step 1 — Find the pH difference.*

Acid X is 1 unit lower in pH than acid Y.

*Step 2 — Apply the factor-of-10 rule.*

1 unit lower = ×10 higher [H⁺].

Answer: The hydrogen ion concentration in acid X is 10 times greater than in acid Y.

Worked example 4 — using numerical [H⁺] values

A solution has [H⁺] = 0.001 mol/dm³ at pH 3. The acid is diluted until its [H⁺] becomes 0.00001 mol/dm³. What is the new pH?

*Step 1 — Find the factor change in [H⁺].*

0.001 ÷ 0.00001 = 100

So [H⁺] has decreased by a factor of 100 = 10².

*Step 2 — Convert factor to pH change.*

A factor of 10² lower [H⁺] = a rise in pH of 2 units.

*Step 3 — Calculate the new pH.*

New pH = 3 + 2 = pH 5.

Why pH is logarithmic (the underlying reason)

You do not need to derive pH at GCSE, but understanding why the scale is built this way helps the factor-of-10 rule stick. Hydrogen ion concentrations in real solutions span an enormous range — from roughly 1 mol/dm³ in 1 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid down to about 10⁻¹⁴ mol/dm³ in concentrated sodium hydroxide. That is 14 orders of magnitude. Writing every solution's acidity as a decimal would be unwieldy ("[H⁺] = 0.0000001 mol/dm³" is harder to compare than "pH 7"). Using a logarithmic scale compresses these huge ranges into single-digit numbers between 0 and 14, where each unit represents a tenfold change.

This is why a small-looking change in pH represents a large chemical change. A solution at pH 1 is ten thousand times more acidic in terms of [H⁺] than a solution at pH 5 — even though the numbers 1 and 5 look close together.

Mathematical Skills

Spec 3.5 maps onto the Edexcel mathematical skill 1c — Use ratios, fractions and percentages. The reasoning needed is the logarithmic factor-of-10 rule:

`` Factor change in [H⁺] = 10^(change in pH) ``

Procedure for a typical question:

  1. Identify the two pH values given in the question (or asked for).
  2. Calculate the difference in pH (subtract one from the other).
  3. Raise 10 to the power of that difference: 10ⁿ where n is the pH change.
  4. Decide the direction: if pH has fallen, [H⁺] has risen by that factor; if pH has risen, [H⁺] has fallen by that factor.
  5. Write the answer with the correct factor and the correct direction.

The questions are typically 1-mark MCQs, so the marker is looking for the correct factor — but the direction matters when the question wording forces it (e.g. "how many times greater").

3.1.3.1 Core Practical: Investigating pH F+H ▼

Core Practical: Investigating pH

Syllabus point 3.6 — Investigate the change in pH on adding powdered calcium hydroxide or calcium oxide to a fixed volume of dilute hydrochloric acid (Foundation + Higher, 1CH0 + 1SC0, Paper 1).

This core practical is examined as a practical skills question: you must know the method well enough to describe steps, identify variables, predict the shape of the graph, plot data, and interpret what is happening at a particle level. The chemistry being tested is straightforward neutralisation — an acid reacting with a base to give a salt and water — but the graph-plotting and explanation aspects carry most of the marks.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AcidA substance that releases H⁺ ions when dissolved in water (a proton donor)
BaseA substance that neutralises an acid, accepting H⁺ ions to form water
AlkaliA soluble base that releases OH⁻ ions in solution
NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base to produce a salt and water
pHA measure of the concentration of H⁺ ions in solution, scaled 0–14
Universal indicatorA mixture of dyes that changes through a range of colours to show the approximate pH of a solution
Spatula measureA small, approximately reproducible amount of solid added using the flat end of a spatula
Line of best fitA smooth line or curve drawn through plotted data that follows the overall trend without joining every individual point

The chemistry behind the practical

Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid that fully ionises in water:

HCl (aq) → H⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq)

The starting solution therefore has a high concentration of H⁺ ions and a low pH (typically around 1).

Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, and calcium oxide, CaO, are both bases. When they are added to the acid they react to neutralise the H⁺ ions:

Ca(OH)₂ (s) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl₂ (aq) + 2H₂O (l)

CaO (s) + 2HCl (aq) → CaCl₂ (aq) + H₂O (l)

Calcium oxide is technically a basic oxide; when added to water (or to aqueous acid) it forms calcium hydroxide first, so once dissolved it behaves the same way. Both reactions produce calcium chloride as the salt and water.

As more base is added, more H⁺ ions are removed from the solution. The concentration of H⁺ ions falls, so the pH rises. Once all the acid has been neutralised, any further base added is in excess: it dissolves only slightly (calcium hydroxide is sparingly soluble) and the pH rises above 7 into the alkaline region, finally levelling off near the pH of a saturated calcium hydroxide solution (around pH 12).

Apparatus

  • 100 cm³ conical flask (or beaker)
  • 25 cm³ measuring cylinder
  • Dilute hydrochloric acid (typically 0.1 mol/dm³ or 1.0 mol/dm³ — exact concentration is stated in the question)
  • Powdered calcium hydroxide or powdered calcium oxide
  • Spatula
  • Glass stirring rod
  • Universal indicator paper (or a pH probe / pH meter)
  • White tile (for colour comparison if using indicator paper)
  • Balance reading to 0.01 g (if measuring mass added rather than spatula measures)
  • Safety goggles

A pH probe gives continuous, more accurate readings to one decimal place. Universal indicator paper is cheaper and gives whole-number pH values by colour matching against a chart.

Method

  1. Use a measuring cylinder to add a fixed volume (e.g. 25 cm³) of dilute hydrochloric acid to a conical flask.
  2. Measure the starting pH of the acid by dipping a glass rod into the solution and touching it onto a piece of universal indicator paper, then comparing the colour to a pH chart. If using a pH probe, dip the calibrated probe directly into the acid and record the reading.
  3. Add one spatula measure (or a fixed mass, e.g. 0.5 g) of powdered calcium hydroxide to the flask.
  4. Stir thoroughly with the glass rod until the solid has reacted as fully as possible.
  5. Measure the pH of the mixture using the same method as in step 2.
  6. Record the pH in a results table against the cumulative number of spatula measures (or cumulative mass) added.
  7. Repeat steps 3–6, continuing to add spatula measures, until the pH no longer changes — usually around 8–10 additions.
  8. Plot a graph of pH (y-axis) against number of spatula measures of base added (x-axis), and draw a smooth line of best fit.

If using calcium oxide, the method is identical — just substitute CaO for Ca(OH)₂.

Expected shape of the graph

When pH is plotted against the mass (or number of spatula measures) of base added, the curve has a characteristic S-shape:

  • Flat low region — at the start, the pH is very low (around 1) and changes only slightly with the first few additions, because the acid is in large excess.
  • Steep middle region — around the equivalence point, each addition removes a large proportion of the remaining H⁺ ions, so the pH climbs sharply through the neutral point (pH 7).
  • Flat high region — once all the acid has been neutralised, further base does little to change the pH; it levels off at around pH 12 (the pH of a saturated calcium hydroxide solution).

The pH never goes above about 12 in this experiment because calcium hydroxide is only slightly soluble — any extra solid simply sits at the bottom of the flask undissolved.

Graph-plotting guidance (Maths skills 4a and 4c)

Graph plotting is worth a significant share of the marks in this practical question, so it is worth knowing the marking criteria. Maths skills 4a (translate information between graphical and numeric form) and 4c (plot two variables from experimental or other data) both apply here.

To gain full marks for plotting:

  1. Axes the right way round — independent variable (mass / number of spatula measures of base) on the x-axis, dependent variable (pH) on the y-axis.
  2. Label each axis with the quantity and the unit (e.g. "Mass of Ca(OH)₂ added / g", "pH" — pH has no unit).
  3. Choose a sensible scale that uses more than half of the available grid in each direction. Avoid awkward scales like 3 small squares = 1 unit; stick to 1, 2, 5 or 10 per large square.
  4. Plot points accurately to within half a small square, using a sharp pencil and either a small cross (×) or a dot inside a circle (⊙).
  5. Draw a smooth curve of best fit through the points. Do not join the points dot-to-dot with straight line segments — the underlying trend is a smooth S-curve.
  6. To read an interpolated value (e.g. "Use the graph to find the mass of base needed to make the mixture pH 7"), draw a faint dashed line from the y-axis value across to the curve, then straight down to the x-axis, and read off the value.

Safety considerations

  • Wear safety goggles throughout — dilute HCl and calcium hydroxide are both irritants, and powdered solids can spray when stirred.
  • Calcium oxide reacts exothermically with water and acid; add it slowly to avoid splashing and excessive heating.
  • Universal indicator solution and paper are flammable when alcohol-based — keep away from open flames.
  • Wash hands after handling powders; never inhale the dust.
  • Dispose of the final neutralised mixture as directed by your teacher; the calcium chloride solution can usually be washed down the sink with plenty of water.

Particle-level explanation of the pH change

Examiners value this kind of reasoning highly: you should be able to explain why the pH rises in terms of particles, not just state that "it goes up because a base is added".

  • The starting acid contains a high concentration of H⁺ (aq) ions.
  • The base supplies OH⁻ ions (from Ca(OH)₂ directly, or via reaction of CaO with water).
  • H⁺ and OH⁻ ions react together to form water: H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l).
  • Each addition of base therefore reduces the concentration of H⁺ ions in the solution.
  • Because pH is a measure of the concentration of H⁺ ions, the pH rises as more base is added.
  • Once all the H⁺ ions have been neutralised, the solution is neutral (pH 7). Any further base raises the OH⁻ concentration above 10⁻⁷ mol/dm³, so the solution becomes alkaline.

3.1.4.1 Acid Strength & Concentration H only ▼

Acid Strength & Concentration

Spec points 3.7 and 3.8: Explain the terms dilute and concentrated (with respect to amount of substances in solution) and explain the terms weak and strong acids (with respect to the degree of dissociation into ions). **Higher tier only**.

This spec point introduces one of the most common discrimination points in GCSE chemistry — the difference between concentration (how much acid is dissolved in a given volume of water) and strength (how readily the acid molecules split apart into hydrogen ions in solution). The two ideas sound similar in everyday English but describe completely different chemical properties. A solution can be dilute and strong, concentrated and weak, or any other combination of the four terms. Getting this distinction watertight is the single most useful thing you can do for the longer-mark questions on acids.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
DiluteA solution containing a small amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solvent (low concentration)
ConcentratedA solution containing a large amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solvent (high concentration)
Strong acidAn acid that fully dissociates (ionises) into ions in aqueous solution, releasing all of its available hydrogen ions
Weak acidAn acid that only partially dissociates (ionises) into ions in aqueous solution, releasing only a small fraction of its available hydrogen ions
DissociationThe splitting of an acid molecule into its ions (including H⁺) when dissolved in water
ConcentrationThe amount of solute (in moles or grams) dissolved per unit volume of solution (mol/dm³ or g/dm³)

Concentration: dilute vs concentrated

Concentration describes how much acid is dissolved per unit volume of water. It says nothing about what kind of acid you have — only how much of it is present.

  • A dilute acid has a small amount of acid dissolved in a large volume of water (low mol/dm³).
  • A concentrated acid has a large amount of acid dissolved in a small volume of water (high mol/dm³).

You can convert any concentrated solution into a dilute one by adding more water, and you can concentrate any dilute solution by evaporating water off. Concentration is a property you can change just by altering how much solvent is present — it is not a fixed property of the acid itself.

Concentration is usually measured in mol/dm³ (moles per cubic decimetre, sometimes written mol dm⁻³) or in g/dm³ (grams per cubic decimetre). A 1.0 mol/dm³ solution and a 0.01 mol/dm³ solution of the same acid contain the same chemical, but the first has 100 times more acid particles in the same volume.

Strength: strong vs weak

Strength describes what fraction of the acid molecules dissociate (split into ions) when the acid dissolves in water. It is a fixed property of the acid itself — you cannot change a weak acid into a strong acid by adding more of it or by removing water.

  • A strong acid dissociates completely in water. Every acid molecule that dissolves splits into ions, releasing all its hydrogen ions (H⁺) into the solution. The reverse reaction (ions recombining into molecules) does not happen to any significant extent.
  • A weak acid dissociates only partially in water. Most of the dissolved molecules stay intact, and only a small fraction split into ions. The dissociation is a reversible reaction, so an equilibrium is set up between the un-ionised molecules and the ions.

*Strong acid (full dissociation, single arrow):*

HCl (aq) → H⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq)

Every HCl molecule that dissolves donates its hydrogen ion to the water. There are essentially no HCl molecules left in solution — only H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.

*Weak acid (partial dissociation, equilibrium arrow ⇌):*

CH₃COOH (aq) ⇌ H⁺ (aq) + CH₃COO⁻ (aq)

The equilibrium lies far to the left. At any moment, only a small percentage (typically around 1% for ethanoic acid at typical concentrations) of the dissolved CH₃COOH molecules have actually split into ions. The rest remain as intact CH₃COOH molecules dissolved in the water.

The double arrow (⇌) is the key visual cue that the acid is weak. If you write a single arrow for ethanoic acid in an exam answer, you have implied full dissociation — which would make it a strong acid.

*Common examples of each:*

Strong acidsWeak acids
Hydrochloric acid, HClEthanoic acid, CH₃COOH
Sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄Citric acid
Nitric acid, HNO₃Carbonic acid, H₂CO₃

Why concentration and strength are independent

This is the heart of the spec point. Because concentration depends on how much acid is dissolved and strength depends on what fraction dissociates, the two are completely independent properties. All four combinations are possible:

Dilute (low mol/dm³)Concentrated (high mol/dm³)
Strong (fully dissociates)Dilute HCl — small amount of acid, but every molecule releases its H⁺Concentrated HCl — large amount of acid, and every molecule releases its H⁺
Weak (partially dissociates)Dilute ethanoic acid — small amount of acid, and only a small fraction releases its H⁺Concentrated ethanoic acid (e.g. glacial ethanoic acid diluted slightly) — large amount of acid, but only a small fraction releases its H⁺

A 0.001 mol/dm³ solution of HCl is dilute and strong. A 5 mol/dm³ solution of ethanoic acid is concentrated and weak. Neither of these contradictions has anything to do with the other — they are describing different things.

The key idea to lock in: strength tells you how willing each individual molecule is to dissociate; concentration tells you how many molecules are present in the first place.

Hydrogen ion concentration and pH

The pH of an acid solution depends on the concentration of hydrogen ions ([H⁺]) actually present in the solution, not on the total amount of acid you started with. This is why strength matters for pH.

  • A strong acid releases all its H⁺ ions into solution. So a 0.1 mol/dm³ solution of HCl has [H⁺] = 0.1 mol/dm³ and a pH close to 1.
  • A weak acid of the same overall concentration releases only a fraction of its H⁺ ions. A 0.1 mol/dm³ solution of ethanoic acid has [H⁺] much lower than 0.1 mol/dm³ (often around 0.001 mol/dm³) and a pH closer to 3.

(Higher tier) Each decrease of one unit on the pH scale corresponds to a tenfold increase in [H⁺]. So if you compare two acid solutions of the same concentration, the strong acid will have a pH around 2 units lower than the weak acid (because it has roughly 100 times more H⁺ ions in solution).

You can also lower the pH of a weak acid by making it more concentrated, but you can never make a weak acid solution as acidic as a strong acid solution at the same concentration — because there are still only a fraction of its molecules dissociating at any given time.

Worked example: comparing four acid solutions

Question: Solutions A, B, C and D each contain an acid in water. Their properties are summarised below. Identify which is most likely to have the lowest pH and explain why.

SolutionAcidConcentration (mol/dm³)
AHCl0.10
BHCl0.01
CCH₃COOH0.10
DCH₃COOH0.01

*Answer:*

  • HCl is a strong acid — fully dissociates → [H⁺] = the concentration of HCl.
  • CH₃COOH is a weak acid — only partially dissociates → [H⁺] is much less than the concentration of CH₃COOH.

Comparing [H⁺] across the four:

  • A: [H⁺] ≈ 0.10 mol/dm³ (full dissociation of 0.10 mol/dm³ HCl)
  • B: [H⁺] ≈ 0.01 mol/dm³ (full dissociation of 0.01 mol/dm³ HCl)
  • C: [H⁺] ≈ 0.001 mol/dm³ (partial dissociation of 0.10 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid)
  • D: [H⁺] ≈ 0.0001 mol/dm³ (partial dissociation of 0.01 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid)

Solution A has the highest [H⁺] and therefore the lowest pH (closest to 1). This is because it combines a high concentration with a strong acid — both factors push [H⁺] up.

Worked example: writing a 4-mark "explain dilute and strong" answer

Question: The dilute hydrochloric acid used in this reaction is a strong acid. Explain the meaning of the terms dilute and strong acid. (4 marks)

A full-mark answer addresses all four ideas:

  1. Dilute — there is a small amount of acid (HCl) dissolved
  2. ...in a large volume of water (or "per unit volume of solution")
  3. Strong acid — the acid fully dissociates (or fully ionises)
  4. ...to release hydrogen ions (H⁺) in aqueous solution

The fourth mark is the one most candidates lose. Saying "fully dissociates" on its own is not enough — you must say what the acid dissociates into. The point of being a strong acid is the production of H⁺ ions, and the marker is looking for that specific term.

Reactions of strong vs weak acids — what looks the same and what differs

Strong and weak acids both undergo the same types of reaction (with metals, carbonates, bases) and produce the same products for a given acid–base pair. But the rates of those reactions differ because the rate depends on [H⁺]:

  • A strong acid reacts faster than a weak acid at the same overall concentration, because there are more H⁺ ions available to react at any given moment.
  • More gas bubbles per second are produced when magnesium reacts with 0.1 mol/dm³ HCl than with 0.1 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid.
  • The eventual amount of product (e.g. total volume of hydrogen gas) is the same if the moles of acid are the same, because as H⁺ ions are used up, more weak-acid molecules dissociate to replace them (Le Chatelier's principle shifts the equilibrium to the right).

This is a common exam trap — students think a weak acid will produce less hydrogen gas overall when reacting with excess magnesium. It will not. It will just produce it more slowly.

Exam technique: the most common ways to lose marks

  • Writing "fully dissociates" without saying into hydrogen ions (H⁺) — this is the single most-cited error in examiner reports for this spec point. Always finish the sentence with "to release H⁺ ions" or "into ions, including H⁺".
  • Confusing strong with concentrated — saying "a strong acid is one with a lot of acid in it" is wrong. Strong refers to dissociation, not amount.
  • Confusing weak with dilute — saying "a weak acid is one with hardly any acid in it" is wrong. Weak refers to dissociation, not amount.
  • Writing a single arrow (→) for a weak-acid dissociation — that implies full dissociation. Use the equilibrium arrow (⇌) for weak acids.
  • Forgetting state symbols — H⁺ (aq) and Cl⁻ (aq) need (aq), not (l) or no symbol at all.
  • Claiming a weak acid produces less product overall when reacting with excess metal or carbonate — it produces the same amount, just more slowly.
Key Takeaways
  1. Concentration describes the amount of acid dissolved per unit volume of solution (mol/dm³ or g/dm³); strength describes the fraction of dissolved acid molecules that dissociate into ions.
  2. Concentration and strength are independent — all four combinations (dilute strong, concentrated strong, dilute weak, concentrated weak) are possible.
  3. A strong acid fully dissociates in water to release all of its hydrogen ions: HCl (aq) → H⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq). Use a single arrow.
  4. A weak acid only partially dissociates in water, setting up an equilibrium: CH₃COOH (aq) ⇌ H⁺ (aq) + CH₃COO⁻ (aq). Use an equilibrium arrow (⇌).
  5. Common strong acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃. Common weak acids: ethanoic, citric, carbonic.
  6. At the same concentration, a strong acid has a lower pH (more H⁺) than a weak acid, because more of its molecules have dissociated to release H⁺.
  7. The pH of any acid solution depends on the concentration of H⁺ ions present, which depends on both the concentration of the acid and its strength.
  8. At the same concentration, a strong acid reacts faster than a weak acid (more H⁺ available), but the eventual amount of product is the same if the moles of acid are equal.
  9. To score full marks on a "strong acid" definition, you must say it fully dissociates AND that it produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) — the second half is where most marks are lost.
  10. To score full marks on a "dilute" definition, you must say a small amount of acid AND in a large volume of water (or per unit volume of solution).
  11. Strength is a fixed property of the acid; concentration can be changed by adding or removing water.
  12. This spec point is Higher tier only and appears on Paper 1 (1CH0/1H), typically as a 2–4 mark "Explain" question.
3.1.5.1 Bases F+H ▼

Bases

Syllabus reference: 3.9 — Recall that a base is any substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only. 3.10 — Recall that alkalis are soluble bases. Examined on Foundation and Higher tier in Paper 1 of both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science).

Bases are one of the two great families of compounds that, together with acids, are responsible for nearly all of the reactions you meet in the Chemical Changes section of the Edexcel GCSE specification. At GCSE level the definition is functional: a base is identified by what it does — it cancels out an acid, producing only a salt and water. The closely-related term alkali is reserved for the subset of bases that dissolve in water. That distinction looks small on the page but examiners test it repeatedly, and candidates who confuse the two terms throw away easy marks.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
BaseAny substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only
AlkaliA soluble base — a base that dissolves in water to release hydroxide ions, OH⁻ (aq)
NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base in which the H⁺ ions from the acid react with the OH⁻ or O²⁻ ions from the base to form water, leaving a salt in solution
SaltThe ionic compound formed when the hydrogen in an acid is replaced by a metal (or ammonium) ion

The core rule: every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali. Sodium hydroxide, NaOH, is both a base (because it neutralises acid) and an alkali (because it dissolves to give a strongly alkaline solution). Copper(II) oxide, CuO, is a base (it neutralises acid to give copper(II) sulfate and water) but it is not an alkali because it is insoluble in water.

Why bases react with acids to give only a salt and water

The reason the products of an acid–base reaction are limited to a salt and water lies in what the reacting particles actually are. An acid in aqueous solution contains H⁺ (aq) ions. A base contains a particle that can accept those H⁺ ions — most commonly the hydroxide ion OH⁻ (in alkalis), the oxide ion O²⁻ (in metal oxides), or the carbonate ion CO₃²⁻ (in metal carbonates, although carbonates also release CO₂, which is why they sit slightly outside the strict "salt and water only" rule). When H⁺ meets OH⁻, the two combine to form a molecule of water:

H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l)

The metal cation from the base (Na⁺, K⁺, Cu²⁺, etc.) is left in solution paired with the anion from the acid (Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, etc.). That pairing is the salt. So the only new substances produced are water and the salt — which is exactly what the spec wording in 3.9 demands.

Types of base — a comparison table

Bases at GCSE come in four main families. Knowing which are soluble (and so count as alkalis) and which are insoluble is essential, because it affects how you write equations, how you choose a preparation method, and how examiners frame questions.

A few things stand out from the table that you should be ready to use in an answer:

  • All Group 1 hydroxides are alkalis. NaOH and KOH are the two you'll see most often, and they dissolve to give strongly alkaline solutions (pH 13–14).
  • Calcium hydroxide is the unusual case. It is only slightly soluble in water, so a saturated solution (limewater) is a weak alkali used for testing for carbon dioxide.
  • Transition-metal oxides and hydroxides are bases but not alkalis. Copper(II) oxide neutralises sulfuric acid to give copper(II) sulfate — but it does this as an insoluble solid, not via dissolved OH⁻ ions.
  • Ammonia is unusual because it doesn't contain a hydroxide ion, yet it forms an alkaline solution. When NH₃ dissolves it reacts slightly with water to form NH₄⁺ and OH⁻, which is why ammonia solution turns red litmus blue and neutralises acids to give ammonium salts (such as NH₄Cl).

The general word equation and what counts as "neutralisation"

Whatever the base, the general pattern of the reaction with an acid is:

*acid + base → salt + water*

This reaction is called neutralisation, and recognising the reaction as neutralisation is something examiners test directly. When an alkali (a soluble base) neutralises an acid, the ionic equation always reduces to the same single line:

H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l)

That is the underlying neutralisation step. The spectator ions — the metal cation from the base and the non-metal anion from the acid — are not part of the chemistry, they just pair up afterwards to become the salt.

Worked example 1 — an alkali (soluble base) reacting with an acid

Hydrochloric acid is neutralised by sodium hydroxide solution.

*Word equation:*

hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water

*Symbol equation (with state symbols):*

HCl (aq) + NaOH (aq) → NaCl (aq) + H₂O (l)

Both reactants are aqueous solutions, the salt (sodium chloride) is also aqueous because it is highly soluble, and water is a liquid. If you needed solid sodium chloride from this reaction you would evaporate the water off at the end. The ionic equation, with the spectator ions Na⁺ and Cl⁻ cancelled, is simply H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l), which makes the neutralisation step explicit.

Worked example 2 — an insoluble base reacting with an acid

Sulfuric acid is neutralised by copper(II) oxide, which is an insoluble black powder.

*Word equation:*

sulfuric acid + copper(II) oxide → copper(II) sulfate + water

*Symbol equation (with state symbols):*

H₂SO₄ (aq) + CuO (s) → CuSO₄ (aq) + H₂O (l)

Notice three differences from worked example 1:

  1. The base is solid, not aqueous — so its state symbol is (s).
  2. The salt produced (copper(II) sulfate) is soluble and stays in solution as (aq), which is why the resulting solution turns blue.
  3. You typically use excess insoluble base when preparing a salt this way, so that all the acid is used up; the leftover unreacted base is then removed by filtration. This is the standard preparation route for the salt of an insoluble base.

This is still a neutralisation reaction. The fact that the base is insoluble does not stop the H⁺ ions from the acid reacting with the O²⁻ ions on the surface of the copper(II) oxide.

Worked example 3 — a metal carbonate reacting with an acid

Metal carbonates also neutralise acids, although they release carbon dioxide as well as forming a salt and water — so they are a slight extension of the strict "salt and water only" definition in 3.9, but they are still classified as bases. For example:

*Word equation:*

nitric acid + calcium carbonate → calcium nitrate + water + carbon dioxide

*Symbol equation:*

2HNO₃ (aq) + CaCO₃ (s) → Ca(NO₃)₂ (aq) + H₂O (l) + CO₂ (g)

You'll see fizzing because of the CO₂ gas. This is the basis of the limewater test and of indigestion remedies that use calcium carbonate.

Worked example 4 — ammonia reacting with an acid

Ammonia is a soluble base, so it counts as an alkali. It reacts with acids to form ammonium salts — there is no water in the strict sense in the gaseous case, but in solution the neutralisation looks like a standard acid–alkali reaction:

NH₃ (aq) + HCl (aq) → NH₄Cl (aq)

Ammonium chloride is the salt formed. Ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃, is the equivalent salt with nitric acid and is one of the most important fertilisers in the world — which is why ammonia is a base you should be able to recognise in an exam context.

The takeaway from this report is that when a question asks you to name or identify a reaction in which an acid and a base (or acid and alkali) react together, the word the examiner is looking for is neutralisation — not "salt formation", not "acid–base reaction", not "displacement", and not "exothermic reaction".

3.1.6.1 Reactions of Acids F+H ▼

Reactions of Acids

Spec points 3.11, 3.13, 3.14 — Foundation + Higher tier · Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0) · Paper 1.

Aqueous acids react in four characteristic ways: with metals, with metal oxides, with metal hydroxides and with metal carbonates. The common product across all four families is a salt — an ionic compound formed when the hydrogen of the acid is replaced by a metal ion. Three of the four reactions are also neutralisations, because metal oxides, metal hydroxides and metal carbonates are all bases. Underneath every acid–alkali neutralisation is the same single ionic process: H⁺ ions from the acid combining with OH⁻ ions from the alkali to make water.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
AcidA substance that produces H⁺ (aq) ions when dissolved in water
BaseA substance that neutralises an acid; usually a metal oxide, metal hydroxide or metal carbonate
AlkaliA soluble base that releases OH⁻ (aq) ions in water
SaltAn ionic compound formed when the H⁺ of an acid is replaced by a metal ion (or ammonium ion)
NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base to produce a salt and water

The four general reactions of acids (spec 3.11)

Every acid reaction you will be asked about at GCSE fits one of these four patterns. Learn the general word equation first, then the balanced symbol equation, then a worked example.

*a) Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen*

A reactive metal (above hydrogen in the reactivity series — magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron) displaces hydrogen from the acid. You see effervescence (fizzing) as hydrogen gas is released, and the metal dissolves to leave a colourless solution of the salt.

General word equation: acid + metal → salt + hydrogen

Worked example — magnesium with dilute hydrochloric acid:

2HCl (aq) + Mg (s) → MgCl₂ (aq) + H₂ (g)

The hydrogen produced gives a squeaky pop when tested with a lighted splint.

*b) Acid + metal oxide → salt + water*

A metal oxide is a base. When you warm it with a dilute acid, the oxide ion accepts H⁺ ions from the acid to form water, and the metal cation combines with the remaining acid anion to form the salt. Many metal oxides are insoluble black or coloured solids that visibly dissolve as the reaction proceeds — a useful clue in practical questions.

General word equation: acid + metal oxide → salt + water

Worked example — copper(II) oxide with dilute sulfuric acid:

H₂SO₄ (aq) + CuO (s) → CuSO₄ (aq) + H₂O (l)

The black copper(II) oxide disappears and the solution turns blue as copper(II) sulfate forms.

*c) Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water*

Soluble metal hydroxides are alkalis (e.g. NaOH, KOH); insoluble ones are still bases (e.g. Cu(OH)₂). In every case the OH⁻ accepts an H⁺ from the acid to make water, and the metal cation pairs with the acid anion. With soluble hydroxides you see no fizzing and no visible solid change — just a temperature rise — so practicals usually rely on an indicator or thermometer to track the reaction.

General word equation: acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water

Worked example — sodium hydroxide with dilute nitric acid:

HNO₃ (aq) + NaOH (aq) → NaNO₃ (aq) + H₂O (l)

*d) Acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide*

Carbonates contain the CO₃²⁻ ion. When this meets an acid, the H⁺ ions strip oxygen and hydrogen away to form water, while the carbon escapes as carbon dioxide gas. This reaction is unmistakable: you see vigorous effervescence and the carbonate solid (often a white powder or a coloured precipitate) dissolves.

General word equation: acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

Worked example — calcium carbonate with dilute hydrochloric acid:

2HCl (aq) + CaCO₃ (s) → CaCl₂ (aq) + H₂O (l) + CO₂ (g)

Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky/cloudy — that is the test you cite if asked to confirm the gas.

Choosing the right salt

Whichever family of base you start from, the anion of the salt comes from the acid:

  • Hydrochloric acid (HCl) → chloride salts (Cl⁻)
  • Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) → sulfate salts (SO₄²⁻)
  • Nitric acid (HNO₃) → nitrate salts (NO₃⁻)

So sodium hydroxide + sulfuric acid gives sodium sulfate; calcium carbonate + nitric acid gives calcium nitrate. Naming the salt correctly — and getting its formula right — is one of the most common places to lose marks in equation questions.

Neutralisation (spec 3.13)

A neutralisation reaction is a reaction between an acid and a base. Three of the four reactions above (with metal oxides, metal hydroxides, metal carbonates) are neutralisations. The reaction of an acid with a reactive metal is not a neutralisation, because a metal is not a base — the metal is being oxidised in a displacement-style redox reaction, not accepting H⁺ ions.

Neutralisation reactions:

  • always produce a salt and water as the principal products
  • give out heat (they are exothermic), so the temperature of the solution rises
  • end at pH 7 if exactly the right amounts of acid and alkali are mixed (an alkali leaves the solution alkaline; excess acid leaves it acidic)

The ionic equation for acid–alkali neutralisation (spec 3.14)

When an acid and an alkali react in solution, the spectator ions (the metal cation from the alkali and the anion from the acid) stay dissolved as separate aqueous ions throughout. The only particles that actually change are the H⁺ from the acid and the OH⁻ from the alkali, which combine to form water:

*H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l)*

This ionic equation is the essential reaction of every acid–alkali neutralisation — it is the same single process whether you mix HCl and NaOH, H₂SO₄ and KOH or HNO₃ and Ca(OH)₂. The salt that you end up with after evaporating the water depends on which spectator ions were present, but the chemical change driving the reaction is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

*Building the ionic equation from a full equation*

Take the reaction of nitric acid with sodium hydroxide:

HNO₃ (aq) + NaOH (aq) → NaNO₃ (aq) + H₂O (l)

Write every aqueous species split into its ions:

H⁺ (aq) + NO₃⁻ (aq) + Na⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → Na⁺ (aq) + NO₃⁻ (aq) + H₂O (l)

Cancel the spectator ions (Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ appear unchanged on both sides):

H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l)

You arrive at exactly the same equation no matter which acid–alkali pair you choose — that is the point. Once you can do this, you can write the ionic equation in a single line straight from the word equation without going through the full balanced equation each time.

Worked example — building a balanced equation from scratch

Question: write a balanced symbol equation, including state symbols, for the reaction of sulfuric acid with potassium hydroxide.

  1. Identify the family — acid + alkali (metal hydroxide), so products are a salt + water.
  2. Identify the salt — sulfate (from H₂SO₄) of potassium → K₂SO₄.
  3. Write the word equation: sulfuric acid + potassium hydroxide → potassium sulfate + water.
  4. Write the unbalanced symbol equation: H₂SO₄ + KOH → K₂SO₄ + H₂O.
  5. Balance: there are 2 K and 4 H on the right needed from 2 KOH → 2 KOH; then there are 4 H on the left and 2 H in H₂O, so 2 H₂O on the right.

Final balanced equation:

*H₂SO₄ (aq) + 2KOH (aq) → K₂SO₄ (aq) + 2H₂O (l)*

Mathematical Skills

The maths skill behind this SP is 1c — use ratios, fractions and percentages. When you balance an equation you are doing exactly that: applying whole-number ratios to make sure the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides.

A reliable procedure when balancing reactions of acids:

  1. Write the unbalanced symbol equation with the correct formula of every reactant and every product.
  2. Count the atoms of each element on each side.
  3. Balance metals first, then non-metals other than H and O, then H, then O last.
  4. Re-count to check every element balances.
  5. Add state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous solution.

3.1.7.1 Test for Hydrogen & Carbon Dioxide F+H ▼

Test for Hydrogen & Carbon Dioxide

Spec 3.12: Describe the chemical test for: a hydrogen, b carbon dioxide (using limewater). Examined at Foundation + Higher on Paper 1, for both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science).

Gas tests are one of the most reliably examined topics on the Edexcel GCSE Chemistry papers. They are short, fact-based, and easy to drop marks on if the wording is sloppy. The two tests on this spec point — hydrogen with a lit splint, and carbon dioxide with limewater — appear regularly as one- or two-mark questions, often slotted into a larger experimental question on acids reacting with metals or carbonates. The Foundation paper tends to use them as standalone recall, while the Higher paper threads them into a longer multi-step problem. Either way, students who confuse them with the tests for oxygen or chlorine lose easy marks.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Lit splintA wooden splint with a small visible flame, used to test for flammable gases such as hydrogen
Glowing splintA wooden splint that is no longer flaming but is still hot and red-ember — used to test for oxygen, not hydrogen
Squeaky popThe characteristic sharp popping sound made when a small volume of hydrogen ignites in air
LimewaterA dilute aqueous solution of calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂ (aq) — colourless when fresh
Cloudy / milkyA description of the white precipitate of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) that forms when CO₂ is bubbled through limewater
Positive test resultThe specific observation that confirms the presence of the gas being tested for

The test for hydrogen

Hydrogen is a colourless, odourless gas that is highly flammable. The test relies on burning a small volume of the gas in air, where it reacts explosively with oxygen to form water:

2H₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2H₂O (l)

In a small test tube, this rapid combustion produces an audible "squeaky pop" — the sound is the small pressure wave from the fast exothermic reaction. The sharper and louder the pop, the purer the hydrogen sample.

StepMethodObservationConclusion
1Collect the unknown gas in a small test tube(No specific observation)—
2Hold a lit (burning) splint at the open mouth of the test tubeA "squeaky pop" sound is heardThe gas is hydrogen

Why a lit splint and not a glowing splint: Hydrogen needs an ignition source — an actual flame — to begin combusting. A glowing splint (just a hot red ember) is the test for oxygen, where the hot ember catches fire (relights) because oxygen supports combustion. If you swap the two, the chemistry of the result no longer matches:

  • A glowing splint placed near hydrogen typically just stays as a glowing splint — there is no flame to ignite the gas, so no pop is heard.
  • A lit splint plunged into pure oxygen burns more brightly, but it does not relight from an unlit ember state, so the "relight" wording does not apply.

This is exactly the trap that examiner reports flag for Edexcel candidates (see Examiner Report Insights below). The method is part of the marking point — getting the apparatus right is non-negotiable.

Safety note: Only test small volumes of hydrogen. Larger volumes mixed with air form an explosive mixture, which is the reason this test is always done on tiny samples collected in a test tube.

The test for carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is a colourless, odourless gas that is slightly denser than air and slightly soluble in water. Crucially for this test, CO₂ is an acidic oxide — it reacts with alkaline solutions. Limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) is mildly alkaline, and when CO₂ is bubbled through it, an insoluble white precipitate of calcium carbonate forms. The fine suspension of CaCO₃ scatters light, which is what makes the solution turn cloudy/milky.

The balanced equation is:

Ca(OH)₂ (aq) + CO₂ (g) → CaCO₃ (s) + H₂O (l)

StepMethodObservationConclusion
1Bubble the unknown gas through a small volume of limewater in a test tube (often using a delivery tube)Limewater starts colourless—
2Observe the limewater as the gas passes throughLimewater turns cloudy / milky (a white precipitate forms)The gas is carbon dioxide

Why limewater goes cloudy: The Ca²⁺ ions from the dissolved Ca(OH)₂ combine with carbonate ions (formed when CO₂ dissolves and reacts with the OH⁻ ions in the alkaline solution) to make calcium carbonate. Calcium carbonate is insoluble in water — the tiny solid particles stay suspended, giving the cloudy/milky appearance.

Acceptable wording on a mark scheme: "Cloudy" and "milky" both score. "Turns white" is risky on its own — examiners want you to convey that the change is a suspension, not a colour change of the solution itself.

Extension (not needed for the mark but useful for understanding): If you keep bubbling CO₂ through limewater after it has turned cloudy, the precipitate eventually re-dissolves to form soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate, and the solution goes clear again:

CaCO₃ (s) + CO₂ (g) + H₂O (l) → Ca(HCO₃)₂ (aq)

This is exam-board content for A Level rather than GCSE — but it explains why the test is observed over a short bubbling time and not indefinitely.

Why these two tests are not interchangeable with others

The Edexcel specification names four gas tests at GCSE: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and chlorine. The four are easy to confuse under exam pressure, and most lost marks on this spec point come from picking the wrong splint or the wrong reagent. The comparison table below lays them out together — the two on this spec point are bolded, and the other two are included so you can see exactly how they differ.

GasTest apparatus / reagentPositive observation
HydrogenLit splint at the mouth of the test tube"Squeaky pop" sound
OxygenGlowing splint inserted into the gasSplint relights
Carbon dioxideBubble through limewaterLimewater turns cloudy / milky (white precipitate)

Common confusions to avoid:

  • Glowing splint for hydrogen — wrong; that is the oxygen test, and the result (no pop) does not match.
  • Lit splint for carbon dioxide — wrong; CO₂ does not burn. In fact, CO₂ extinguishes a flame, which is sometimes given as a secondary observation but is not the marking-point test on this spec point.
  • Limewater for oxygen — wrong; oxygen does not react with limewater.
  • Damp litmus for CO₂ — wrong; CO₂ is acidic so it would weakly turn blue litmus red, but this is not the named test, and you would lose the mark for not naming limewater.

Worked example: writing a textbook answer

A typical 2-mark Foundation question reads: "A student carries out a reaction. They believe the gas produced is hydrogen. Describe the test the student should carry out to confirm that the gas is hydrogen."

A full-mark answer should contain two clearly separated marking points — the method and the positive result:

  1. Method (1 mark): "Hold a lit splint at the mouth of the test tube containing the gas."
  2. Result (1 mark): "A squeaky pop is heard."

Wording traps to avoid in this kind of answer:

  • Do not write "burning splint" alone — "lit splint" is the safe, standard wording.
  • Do not write "the gas explodes" — this is too vague; the marking point is the squeaky pop.
  • Do not say "the splint goes out" — that is the secondary observation for CO₂ (carbon dioxide extinguishes a flame), and it does not score on the hydrogen test.

A typical 1-mark carbon dioxide question reads: "What is the chemical test for carbon dioxide?"

A full-mark answer needs the reagent and the positive observation in one sentence:

  • "Bubble the gas through limewater; it turns cloudy / milky."

If you only name limewater without describing the colour change, you usually lose the mark — examiners want both the reagent and the observation.

3.1.8.1 Core Practical: Preparing Copper Sulfate F+H ▼

Core Practical: Preparing Copper Sulfate

Syllabus reference 3.17: Investigate the preparation of pure, dry hydrated copper sulfate crystals starting from copper oxide including the use of a water bath. Syllabus reference 3.15: Explain why, if soluble salts are prepared from an acid and an insoluble reactant, (a) excess of the reactant is added, (b) the excess reactant is removed, (c) the solution remaining is only salt and water. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 1.

This Core Practical brings together the reaction of an acid with an insoluble base and the laboratory techniques used to recover a pure, dry, crystalline salt. The salt produced is hydrated copper sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O, which forms blue crystals as water of crystallisation is incorporated into the lattice. You are expected to know both the practical procedure (what you do at each step) and the reasoning behind every step (why you do it that way).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
BaseA substance that neutralises an acid; metal oxides and metal hydroxides are bases
Insoluble baseA base that does not dissolve in water (e.g. copper(II) oxide, CuO)
SaltAn ionic compound formed when the H⁺ ions of an acid are replaced by metal ions (or NH₄⁺)
NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base to form a salt and water only
Water bathA vessel of hot water used to gently and evenly heat another container placed inside it
FiltrationA separation technique that removes an insoluble solid from a liquid using filter paper and a funnel
CrystallisationA separation technique in which a solute is recovered from solution as solid crystals by allowing the solvent to evaporate slowly
Water of crystallisationWater molecules built into the structure of an ionic crystal (e.g. the "·5H₂O" in CuSO₄·5H₂O)
Hydrated saltA crystalline salt that contains water of crystallisation

The reaction taking place

Copper(II) oxide is a typical insoluble metal oxide that behaves as a base. When it is added to warm dilute sulfuric acid it neutralises the acid to give a soluble salt — copper(II) sulfate — and water:

CuO (s) + H₂SO₄ (aq) → CuSO₄ (aq) + H₂O (l)

Two visible changes signal that the reaction is happening:

  1. The black copper oxide powder disappears as it reacts.
  2. The colourless dilute sulfuric acid turns blue, because copper(II) ions (Cu²⁺) are blue in solution.

If you keep adding copper oxide a little at a time, the solid will eventually stop disappearing — black specks remain suspended in the blue liquid. This is the visual signal that all the acid has been used up.

Full step-by-step method

1. Warm the acid in a water bath. Place a beaker containing about 25 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid inside a larger beaker (or trough) of hot water — this is the water bath. A water bath is used rather than heating the acid directly with a Bunsen burner because hot dilute acid can spit, and direct heat gives uneven temperatures that risk boiling the solution dry. The water bath warms the acid gently and evenly to around 60–70 °C, which speeds up the reaction without driving off liquid.

2. Add copper(II) oxide in small portions, stirring. Using a spatula, add a small amount of black copper oxide powder to the warm acid and stir with a glass rod. The black solid gradually disappears and the solution turns blue. Continue adding copper oxide a little at a time.

3. Keep adding until copper oxide is in excess. Stop adding when no more copper oxide will dissolve — you should see unreacted black solid sitting on the bottom of the beaker even after stirring. This confirms that all the acid has reacted. Adding excess insoluble base is the deliberate choice that guarantees no acid is left over (see "Why excess reactant?" below).

4. Filter to remove the excess copper oxide. Fold a piece of filter paper, place it in a filter funnel resting in a conical flask, and pour the warm mixture through it. The unreacted copper oxide stays on the filter paper as a black residue; the blue copper sulfate solution passes through as the filtrate. The filtrate now contains only copper sulfate and water — no acid and no excess solid.

5. Heat the filtrate to concentrate it (do not evaporate to dryness). Transfer the blue filtrate into an evaporating basin and heat gently — using the water bath again, or a tripod-and-gauze setup with a low Bunsen flame. The aim is to evaporate some of the water so the solution becomes saturated. You can tell the solution is concentrated enough when small crystals start to form at the edges of the basin, or when a glass rod dipped in produces a film of crystals on cooling. Stop heating at this point — do not boil the solution dry. Evaporating all the water destroys the water of crystallisation and gives a powdery, anhydrous solid instead of well-formed blue crystals.

6. Leave to cool and crystallise slowly. Set the evaporating basin aside (or place it somewhere warm) so the saturated solution cools slowly. As it cools, the solubility of copper sulfate falls and the dissolved salt comes out of solution as large, regular, blue crystals of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄·5H₂O. Slow cooling gives bigger, purer crystals than rapid cooling — fast cooling traps impurities and produces small, irregular crystals.

7. Separate and dry the crystals. Once crystallisation is complete, pour off the remaining liquid (the mother liquor) or filter it off. Pat the crystals dry between two sheets of filter paper, or leave them in a warm place (or a desiccator) until no liquid remains on the surface. The final product is a pile of pure, dry, blue crystals.

Why each step is done that way (spec 3.15)

This is where Edexcel often pulls the marks — you need to know the reasoning, not just the recipe.

*a) Why is excess copper oxide added?*

Excess insoluble base is added to ensure that all of the acid reacts. If only a small amount of CuO were added, some unreacted acid would remain in the solution; when that solution was crystallised, the product would be contaminated with sulfuric acid (and would be corrosive). Using excess CuO drives the neutralisation to completion. Because CuO is insoluble, any extra that does not react simply remains as a solid that you can see and remove — so adding too much causes no harm to the final product.

*b) Why is the excess reactant removed?*

The excess CuO is removed by filtration. If it stayed in the mixture, the final crystals would be contaminated with black, gritty unreacted copper oxide. Because CuO is an insoluble solid and copper sulfate is soluble in water, filtration is a clean way to keep the salt in solution (in the filtrate) and trap the unreacted oxide (in the residue).

*c) Why is the solution remaining only salt and water?*

After filtration the only things in solution are:

  • the salt (copper sulfate), formed by the neutralisation, and
  • water, which was the solvent for the original acid and which is also produced by the neutralisation.

There is no leftover acid (excess base used it all up) and no other product (acid + metal oxide gives only salt + water). That is why you can safely crystallise the filtrate to obtain a pure salt — anything else dissolved in the solution would end up trapped in the crystal lattice and ruin the purity.

Why use a water bath?

The spec specifically requires you to know that a water bath is used. Three reasons matter:

  1. Safety. Dilute sulfuric acid heated directly on a Bunsen flame can spit hot acid droplets out of the beaker. A water bath keeps the temperature well below 100 °C, so spitting is avoided.
  2. Even heating. Water transfers heat smoothly to the inner beaker. Direct Bunsen heating can cause local boiling and bumping, which throws solution out of the container and can crack glassware.
  3. Temperature control. The boiling point of water (100 °C) acts as a built-in temperature limit. You cannot accidentally overheat the acid or boil it dry.

Note that during step 5 (concentrating the filtrate), a low Bunsen flame on a tripod-and-gauze is also acceptable — but many specifications and exam mark schemes still favour a water bath for this stage too, for the same safety reasons.

Safety considerations

  • Dilute sulfuric acid is irritant — wear safety goggles throughout and rinse skin with water if any is spilled.
  • Hot glassware and apparatus — use tongs to move the evaporating basin, and allow it to cool on a heatproof mat.
  • Eye protection is essential whenever an acid is being heated, however dilute.
  • Avoid breathing in dust when handling copper(II) oxide powder.
  • Do not evaporate to dryness over a roaring flame — apart from destroying the water of crystallisation, the basin can crack from thermal shock.

How Preparing Copper Sulfate Appears in Exams

This Core Practical is regularly tested on Paper 1, usually as a structured short-answer or extended-response question. The command word is almost always "Describe", and tariffs range from 2 marks (just the crystallisation step) up to 6 marks (the full filtration-plus-crystallisation procedure).

  • 1CH0/1H — November 2020, Q9(b)(ii): "Describe how you would obtain a solution of copper sulfate from the mixture and how you would obtain pure, dry copper sulfate crystals from this solution" (6 marks).
  • 1CH0/1H — June 2022, Q6(b): "Describe what should be done to obtain copper sulfate crystals from this copper sulfate solution" (2 marks).

If you are asked the 6-mark version, you need to cover roughly:

  1. Filter the mixture to remove the excess solid,
  2. Collect the filtrate (the copper sulfate solution),
  3. Heat the filtrate to concentrate it (or to the point of crystallisation),
  4. Stop heating before the solution evaporates to dryness,
  5. Leave the concentrated solution to cool slowly so crystals form,
  6. Dry the crystals (between filter paper or in a warm place / desiccator).

If you are asked the 2-mark version focused just on getting crystals from the solution, the two key marking points are usually:

  • Heat to concentrate / evaporate some of the water (do not evaporate to dryness), and
  • Leave to cool / crystallise slowly.

  1. The reaction is CuO (s) + H₂SO₄ (aq) → CuSO₄ (aq) + H₂O (l) — an insoluble metal oxide neutralising a dilute acid to give a soluble salt plus water.
  2. The acid is warmed gently using a water bath for safety, even heating, and temperature control — direct Bunsen heating risks spitting and bumping.
  3. Copper(II) oxide is added a little at a time and stirred until it is in excess — visible as unreacted black solid in the bottom of the beaker.
  4. Adding excess insoluble base guarantees that all the acid reacts, so no acid contaminates the final product.
  5. The excess unreacted copper oxide is removed by filtration; the blue copper sulfate solution passes through as the filtrate.
  6. Because acid + metal oxide → salt + water, and the excess oxide has been filtered out, the filtrate contains only salt and water — nothing else to contaminate the crystals.

  9. Dry the crystals between filter paper or in a warm place — do not dry them in an oven, as this can drive off the water of crystallisation.

3.1.9.1 Prepare a Salt by Titration F+H ▼

Prepare a Salt by Titration

Syllabus 3.18 — describe how to carry out an acid-alkali titration using a burette, pipette and a suitable indicator to prepare a pure, dry salt. Syllabus 3.16 — explain why a titration must be used when the reactant is soluble. Foundation + Higher, Paper 1, both 1CH0 and 1SC0.

This spec point is the method for making a soluble salt when both reactants are themselves soluble in water — typically an acid plus an alkali. The classic examples are sodium hydroxide reacting with hydrochloric acid to make sodium chloride, or ammonia solution reacting with sulfuric acid to make ammonium sulfate. You will not be asked to do the titration calculation itself (moles, concentration, c = n/v) on Paper 1 — those skills belong to Separate-only spec points 5.9C and 5.10C — but you will be asked to describe, step-by-step, how to use a burette, pipette, and indicator to end up with crystals of a pure dry salt.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
TitrationA technique in which one solution of known volume is added to another, drop-wise from a burette, until the reaction is just complete — used to find the exact volume needed for neutralisation
BuretteA long, graduated glass tube with a tap, used to add an acid (or alkali) drop-by-drop and measure the exact volume added
PipetteA narrow glass tube that delivers a single, fixed volume of liquid very accurately (e.g. 25.0 cm³)

NeutralisationThe reaction between an acid and a base (or alkali) producing a salt and water

Why titration must be used when the reactant is soluble (spec 3.16)

When you make a salt from an acid and an insoluble base — for example, copper(II) oxide added to sulfuric acid — you simply add the solid base in excess until no more reacts, then filter off the unreacted solid. That method is covered in 3.1.8.1 Core Practical: Preparing Copper Sulfate.

That approach cannot be used when the base (or carbonate) is soluble, such as sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, or ammonia. The reasons are exactly what syllabus 3.16 lists:

a) You need to add the exact volume of one reactant to neutralise the other. With a soluble reactant you cannot simply tip in an excess — there is no excess solid to filter away at the end, so any unreacted alkali would stay dissolved and contaminate your salt. A titration finds the precise volume needed for neutralisation, so neither reactant is in excess.

b) The acid and the soluble reactant must be mixed in the correct proportions. This follows from (a). Once you know — from the titration — exactly how much acid neutralises a fixed volume of alkali, you can mix those same volumes again to produce a solution that is purely salt and water.

c) The solution remaining after reaction is only salt and water. Because the volumes are exact, there is no leftover acid and no leftover alkali — the entire solution can be evaporated and the only solid left behind is the pure salt.

A typical worked example reaction:

NaOH (aq) + HCl (aq) → NaCl (aq) + H₂O (l)

or for ammonium sulfate (the salt featured in the June 2019 Foundation question):

2 NH₃ (aq) + H₂SO₄ (aq) → (NH₄)₂SO₄ (aq) + (water from the acid)

The two-stage method (spec 3.18)

This is the part candidates most often get wrong. You must describe both stages — Stage 1 finds the volume, Stage 2 produces the clean salt. Examiners specifically flag that lower-scoring responses cover only one stage and not the other (see Examiner Report Insights below).

Stage 1 — the titration (find the exact volume)

  1. Use a pipette and pipette filler to transfer a known volume (commonly 25.0 cm³) of the alkali into a clean conical flask. Place the flask on a white tile.
  2. Add 2–3 drops of a suitable indicator (phenolphthalein or methyl orange) to the alkali in the flask.
  3. Fill a clean burette with the acid, using a small funnel. Remove the funnel and record the initial burette reading to the nearest 0.05 cm³, reading the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  4. Open the tap and add the acid to the alkali in the conical flask, swirling the flask continuously. As you approach the end-point, add the acid drop-by-drop.
  5. Stop adding acid as soon as the indicator changes colour permanently — that is the end-point. Record the final burette reading.
  6. Calculate the volume of acid used (final reading − initial reading). This is the titre.
  7. Repeat the titration until you have at least two concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other) and take the mean.

This stage tells you exactly how much acid is needed to neutralise that volume of alkali — but the resulting solution is not usable as a salt source, because it contains the indicator, which would contaminate the crystals.

Stage 2 — the salt preparation (make the pure salt)

  1. Rinse out the conical flask thoroughly.
  2. Using the pipette again, transfer the same volume of alkali into the clean flask — but this time add no indicator.
  3. From the burette, add exactly the mean titre of acid recorded in Stage 1. The flask now contains a solution of pure salt and water, with no indicator and no excess of either reactant.
  4. Transfer the solution into an evaporating basin.
  5. Heat the basin gently using a water bath (or on a tripod and gauze over a low Bunsen flame) until about half the water has evaporated and the solution is saturated. Look for crystals beginning to form at the edge of the liquid — this is the point of crystallisation.
  6. Stop heating. Leave the basin to cool slowly so the salt crystallises out of solution.
  7. Filter the crystals from any remaining solution, or carefully decant the liquid.
  8. Pat the crystals dry between two pieces of filter paper, or leave them in a warm oven or desiccator until fully dry.

The salt is now pure (because there was no indicator and no excess reactant) and dry (because of the final drying step).

Why no indicator in Stage 2

A common student question. Indicators like phenolphthalein and methyl orange are themselves dyed organic compounds. If you crystallise a salt solution that contains indicator, those dye molecules end up trapped in the crystals — your "pure" sodium chloride would be faintly pink or orange, and would not be chemically pure. So you use the indicator once to find the right volume, then repeat without it for the clean prep.

Why the salt must be left to crystallise rather than evaporated to dryness

Some soluble salts — including many ammonium salts and salts of hydrated metals — decompose or lose their water of crystallisation if heated too strongly. Evaporating to the point of crystallisation and then allowing slow cooling produces well-formed crystals of the correct hydrate. Heating an evaporating basin to dryness on a hot Bunsen flame would risk thermal decomposition (especially for ammonium salts, which can sublime or release ammonia gas) and would not give clean crystals.

3.2.1.1 Electrolysis F+H ▼

Electrolysis

Spec points 3.22, 3.23, 3.24 — Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 1.

Electrolysis is the process by which an electric current is used to break down an ionic compound into simpler substances — usually into its constituent elements. It is the gateway concept for the whole of Topic 3.2: every later spec point on electrolysis (aqueous solutions, molten compounds, half-equations, industrial extraction) depends on the three foundational ideas in this SP. Get the charges, the names of the electrodes, and the direction of ion movement correct here, and the rest of the topic falls into place.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ElectrolyteAn ionic compound that is either molten or dissolved in water, allowing its ions to move freely and conduct electricity
ElectrolysisA process in which electrical energy from a direct current (d.c.) supply decomposes an electrolyte into simpler substances
ElectrodeA solid conductor (often graphite or an unreactive metal such as platinum) that carries the current into and out of the electrolyte
CathodeThe negative electrode — connected to the negative terminal of the d.c. supply
AnodeThe positive electrode — connected to the positive terminal of the d.c. supply
CationA positively charged ion (e.g. Na⁺, Cu²⁺, H⁺) — migrates to the cathode
AnionA negatively charged ion (e.g. Cl⁻, O²⁻, OH⁻) — migrates to the anode
DecomposeTo break a compound down into simpler substances (here, by electrical energy)
Direct current (d.c.)A current that flows in one direction only, so each electrode keeps a fixed positive or negative charge throughout the experiment

Why solid ionic compounds do not conduct, but molten or aqueous ones do

Every ionic compound — sodium chloride, lead bromide, copper sulfate — is built from a regular three-dimensional lattice of positive and negative ions held together by strong electrostatic forces (see 1.3.2.2 Ionic Lattices). In the solid state these ions are locked into fixed positions in the lattice. They can vibrate slightly about those positions, but they cannot move from one place to another. Because electrical conduction requires charged particles to move through the substance, and the ions are immobile, a solid ionic compound is an electrical insulator.

When that same compound is melted, the regular lattice collapses and the ions break free of their fixed positions. They are still charged, but now they can move. Apply a potential difference across the molten liquid and the ions drift towards the electrode of opposite charge — conduction (and decomposition) becomes possible.

When the compound is dissolved in water, water molecules pull the ions apart and surround them. The ions are again free to move through the solution. As long as the compound is ionic (so it actually contains separable cations and anions), the aqueous solution will conduct electricity and can be electrolysed.

This single idea — ions can only move when free, and only moving ions conduct — explains the whole definition of an electrolyte.

The set-up: what an electrolysis cell looks like

A standard electrolysis cell has three essential parts:

  1. A direct-current (d.c.) power supply, usually a battery or a low-voltage d.c. power pack. The d.c. supply is essential because the two electrodes must keep fixed charges throughout the experiment — an alternating current would swap them every cycle, and no net decomposition would occur.
  2. Two electrodes dipping into the electrolyte. Inert electrodes (graphite or platinum) are used when you only want to study the electrolyte itself, because inert electrodes do not react with the products.
  3. The electrolyte — either a molten ionic compound or an aqueous solution of an ionic compound.

The electrode connected to the positive terminal of the d.c. supply is the anode. The electrode connected to the negative terminal of the d.c. supply is the cathode. The names never swap, no matter what is in the cell.

The PANIC mnemonic — the cure for the most common exam mistake

The single biggest source of marks lost in this topic is candidates labelling the electrodes the wrong way round. Examiners have specifically commented on this (see the Examiner Report Insights below). The fix is the mnemonic:

Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode

So:

ElectrodeChargeWhich ions migrate to it
AnodePositive (+)Anions (negative ions, e.g. Cl⁻, Br⁻, O²⁻, OH⁻)
CathodeNegative (−)Cations (positive ions, e.g. Na⁺, K⁺, Cu²⁺, Pb²⁺, H⁺)

A second helpful cross-check: opposites attract. A positive ion is attracted to the negative cathode; a negative ion is attracted to the positive anode. If you ever blank on PANIC in the exam, fall back on this rule — but in either case, take a second to label both electrodes on the diagram before you answer.

Why ions move where they do — the physics underneath

Once the d.c. supply is switched on, the anode is held at a higher (more positive) potential than the cathode. A potential difference exists across the electrolyte. Each free ion in the liquid experiences an electrostatic force:

  • A cation (positive) is pushed away from the positive anode and pulled towards the negative cathode.
  • An anion (negative) is pushed away from the negative cathode and pulled towards the positive anode.

So ions drift in opposite directions through the electrolyte, and this drift is the electric current inside the liquid. (In the external wires the current is carried by electrons, but in the electrolyte the charge carriers are the ions themselves — a crucial distinction.)

When a cation reaches the cathode, it gains electrons (it is reduced) and is discharged as a neutral atom. When an anion reaches the anode, it loses electrons (it is oxidised) and is also discharged. The overall result is that the electrolyte is broken apart — decomposed — into simpler substances, which is the definition of electrolysis from spec point 3.23. The redox detail is developed further in 3.2.4.1 Electrolysis & Redox.

Picturing ion migration — a description of the diagram

Imagine a beaker containing molten lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂. Two graphite rods dip into the liquid. The left-hand rod is wired to the negative terminal of a battery (so it is the cathode); the right-hand rod is wired to the positive terminal (so it is the anode).

  • In the bulk of the liquid you have Pb²⁺ ions (cations) and Br⁻ ions (anions) jumbled together.
  • The Pb²⁺ ions all drift leftwards, towards the negative cathode. Arrows on a typical exam diagram point from the bulk of the liquid towards the cathode.
  • The Br⁻ ions all drift rightwards, towards the positive anode. Arrows point from the bulk of the liquid towards the anode.

At the cathode, each Pb²⁺ ion picks up two electrons and becomes a neutral lead atom — molten lead collects at the bottom. At the anode, two Br⁻ ions each give up an electron and combine to form Br₂, which leaves as a brown vapour.

The same picture works for any electrolyte — just swap the ions. Cations always go to the cathode (negative electrode); anions always go to the anode (positive electrode). Once you can sketch and label that ion-migration diagram cleanly, the rest of Topic 3.2 becomes much easier.

Worked example — predicting ion movement

> A student passes a direct current through molten potassium iodide, KI, using two graphite electrodes. > (a) Name the cation in the electrolyte and state which electrode it migrates to. > (b) Name the anion in the electrolyte and state which electrode it migrates to.

*Answer:*

(a) The cation is the potassium ion, K⁺. K⁺ is positively charged, so it is attracted to the negatively charged electrode — the cathode.

(b) The anion is the iodide ion, I⁻. I⁻ is negatively charged, so it is attracted to the positively charged electrode — the anode.

Notice how the answer hooks each step to the charge of the ion and the charge of the electrode. Examiners want to see that reasoning, not just the destination.

Common misconceptions to avoid

  • "Electrons move through the electrolyte." No — in the electrolyte, the charge carriers are the ions themselves. Electrons only travel through the external metal wires.
  • "Solid ionic compounds conduct electricity." Only if molten or dissolved. The solid lattice has no mobile charges.
  • "The anode is negative." It is not. The anode is the positive electrode. PANIC is the fix.
  • "Cations have a negative charge." Cations are positive. They are attracted to the negative cathode (catiONS to the cathODE — same vowel-pair if it helps you remember).
  • "You can use a.c. for electrolysis." Only direct current works. With a.c. the electrodes would swap charge every cycle and no net decomposition would occur.
  • "Covalent compounds can be electrolysed." Only ionic compounds (molten or in solution) are electrolytes. Covalent compounds have no ions to discharge.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Aqueous solutionA solution in which the solvent is water; indicated by the state symbol (aq)

CathodeThe negative electrode; positive ions (cations) move to it and are reduced (gain electrons)

Why aqueous electrolysis is different from molten electrolysis

When you electrolyse a molten ionic compound, there are only two types of ion in the liquid — the metal cation and the non-metal anion — so the products are always just the metal at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode (see 3.2.3.1 Electrolysis of Molten Compounds).

In an aqueous solution there are four ions to think about, not two, because water itself partly ionises:

  • From the dissolved compound: a metal cation (e.g. Na⁺, Cu²⁺) and a non-metal anion (e.g. Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻)
  • From the water: H⁺ ions and OH⁻ ions

This means there is always a competition. At the cathode, the dissolved metal cation competes with H⁺ from water for the electrons. At the anode, the dissolved anion competes with OH⁻ from water to be discharged. The two rules below decide which species wins each competition.

The two key rules for inert-electrode aqueous electrolysis

*Cathode rule (which positive ion is discharged?)*

  • If the metal in the solution is more reactive than hydrogen (e.g. Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe), the hydrogen ions are discharged and hydrogen gas (H₂) is produced at the cathode. The metal ion stays in solution.
  • If the metal is less reactive than hydrogen (e.g. Cu, Ag), the metal ion is discharged and the metal is deposited as a solid coating on the cathode.

*Anode rule (which negative ion is discharged?)*

  • If a halide ion (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) is present in reasonable concentration, the halogen is produced at the anode (Cl₂, Br₂ or I₂).
  • If there is no halide (e.g. sulfate, nitrate, carbonate), the hydroxide ions from water are discharged and oxygen gas (O₂) is produced at the anode.

These two rules account for all four named electrolytes in spec 3.25.

Working through each electrolyte

*a) Copper chloride solution, CuCl₂ (aq), inert electrodes*

Ions in solution: Cu²⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.

  • Cathode: Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so Cu²⁺ is preferred and is discharged. A pinkish-brown layer of solid copper builds up on the cathode.

- Half-equation: Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu (s)

  • Anode: Chloride is a halide and is present in reasonable concentration, so Cl⁻ is preferred over OH⁻. Pale yellow-green chlorine gas bubbles off.

- Half-equation: 2Cl⁻ (aq) → Cl₂ (g) + 2e⁻

  • Overall: CuCl₂ (aq) → Cu (s) + Cl₂ (g)

The blue colour of the solution fades as Cu²⁺ ions are removed from the solution. This is also the system used in the core practical of electrolysing copper(II) sulfate with copper (non-inert) electrodes, where the anode reaction is different (see 3.2.5.1 Core Practical: Electrolysis of Copper(II)Sulfate).

*b) Sodium chloride solution, NaCl (aq), inert electrodes*

Ions in solution: Na⁺, Cl⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.

  • Cathode: Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so H⁺ ions are preferred. Hydrogen gas bubbles off and the Na⁺ ions remain in solution.

- Half-equation: 2H⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂ (g)

  • Anode: Chloride is a halide, so Cl⁻ is preferred over OH⁻. Chlorine gas is produced.

- Half-equation: 2Cl⁻ (aq) → Cl₂ (g) + 2e⁻

  • Overall: Na⁺ and OH⁻ are left in solution, so the electrolyte gradually becomes sodium hydroxide, NaOH (aq). The industrial version of this process is the chlor-alkali process, used to manufacture chlorine, hydrogen and sodium hydroxide from brine (concentrated NaCl solution).

*c) Sodium sulfate solution, Na₂SO₄ (aq), inert electrodes*

Ions in solution: Na⁺, SO₄²⁻, H⁺, OH⁻.

  • Cathode: Sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so H⁺ is preferred. Hydrogen gas is produced.

- Half-equation: 2H⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂ (g)

  • Anode: Sulfate is not a halide. The sulfate ion is very stable (S is already in its highest oxidation state) and is not oxidised in this competition. OH⁻ ions from water are discharged instead, producing oxygen gas.

- Half-equation: 4OH⁻ (aq) → O₂ (g) + 2H₂O (l) + 4e⁻

  • Overall: Hydrogen is produced twice as fast as oxygen (by volume), because each H₂ molecule needs 2 electrons but each O₂ molecule needs 4. The sodium sulfate is not used up — the net effect is the electrolysis of water:

- 2H₂O (l) → 2H₂ (g) + O₂ (g)

  • The Na₂SO₄ acts only as a charge-carrier, allowing the solution to conduct.

*d) Water acidified with sulfuric acid (dilute H₂SO₄)*

Ions in solution: H⁺ (lots, from the acid and from water), SO₄²⁻, OH⁻.

  • Cathode: Only positive ion available for discharge is H⁺ — hydrogen gas is produced.

- Half-equation: 2H⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂ (g)

  • Anode: Same logic as the sodium sulfate case — sulfate is too stable to be oxidised, so OH⁻ from water is discharged and oxygen is produced.

- Half-equation: 4OH⁻ (aq) → O₂ (g) + 2H₂O (l) + 4e⁻

  • Overall: Net electrolysis of water: 2H₂O (l) → 2H₂ (g) + O₂ (g)
  • The H₂SO₄ is not used up — it is there to provide ions so that pure water (which conducts very poorly) can carry a current. As in (c), you collect twice the volume of hydrogen as oxygen.

Why the cathode discharge rule depends on reactivity

Reduction at the cathode is the gain of electrons. The less reactive a metal, the more readily its cation accepts electrons and reverts to the elemental metal — because a less reactive metal is less stable as an ion and more stable as the atom. The more reactive a metal, the more stable it is as the cation, so it resists being reduced. In an aqueous solution there is always an alternative — H⁺ from water — that can take the electrons instead, so hydrogen is discharged whenever the dissolved metal is more reactive than it.

This is why copper (low on the reactivity series, below hydrogen) is deposited from CuCl₂ (aq), but sodium (high on the reactivity series, well above hydrogen) is not deposited from NaCl (aq) — hydrogen gas comes off instead. The same logic explains why magnesium, calcium, aluminium and zinc cannot be electrolysed out of aqueous solution either; they have to be extracted from molten compounds (see 3.2.3.1 Electrolysis of Molten Compounds and 4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals).

Why the anode discharge rule prefers halides over OH⁻

Oxidation at the anode is the loss of electrons. Halide ions (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) part with their electrons fairly easily because the resulting halogen molecules (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂) are stable diatomic species. Sulfate, nitrate and carbonate ions, on the other hand, are polyatomic ions where the central atom is already in its highest common oxidation state — so they hold on to their electrons very tightly and are not oxidised under exam conditions.

When no halide is present, the only remaining anion that can be oxidised is OH⁻ (from water), and oxygen gas is produced. This is why sodium sulfate and dilute sulfuric acid both give oxygen at the anode, even though they look like very different electrolytes — once you remove the halide from the equation, the water decides the outcome.

Testing the products at each electrode

You're often asked to identify gases produced. Standard tests:

  • Hydrogen — collect a sample in a test tube, place a lighted splint at the mouth; a squeaky pop confirms hydrogen.
  • Chlorine — pale yellow-green gas; damp blue litmus paper turns red, then bleaches white (the red-then-bleach sequence is what distinguishes chlorine from other acidic gases).
  • Oxygen — relights a glowing splint.
  • Copper at the cathode — visible as a pinkish-brown solid deposit on the electrode; the blue colour of the original solution fades.

3.2.3.1 Electrolysis of Molten Compounds F+H ▼

Electrolysis of Molten Compounds

Spec 3.25e: Explain the formation of the products in the electrolysis, using inert electrodes, of molten lead bromide (demonstration).

Spec 3.26: Predict the products of electrolysis of other binary, ionic compounds in the molten state.

Foundation + Higher tier · Paper 1.

Electrolysis of a molten ionic compound is the simplest type of electrolysis you will meet at GCSE. Because there is no water present, only two types of ion are free to move: the metal cation and the non-metal anion. This makes predicting the products straightforward — the metal is always deposited at the cathode and the non-metal is always produced at the anode. Once you can describe what happens in the demonstration of molten lead bromide, you can apply the same logic to any other binary ionic compound.

Why a molten compound conducts but a solid does not

An ionic solid, such as lead bromide PbBr₂, is built from a giant lattice of Pb²⁺ and Br⁻ ions held in fixed positions by strong electrostatic attractions. Because the ions cannot move, the solid does not conduct electricity and cannot be electrolysed.

When the compound is heated above its melting point, the lattice breaks apart and the ions become free to move. The molten liquid now contains mobile charged particles, so it can:

  • Conduct an electric current (charge is carried by the moving ions)
  • Be broken down by that current into its elements

This is why electrolysis only works on ionic compounds that are molten or dissolved — the ions must be free to migrate to the electrodes.

Why molten electrolysis is simpler than aqueous electrolysis

In 3.2.2.1 Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions you have to consider four species — the metal cation, the non-metal anion, and the H⁺ and OH⁻ ions from water — and decide which gets discharged at each electrode using the reactivity series. In a molten compound there is no water, so only two ions exist:

The molten lead bromide demonstration (Spec 3.25e)

Lead bromide, PbBr₂, is the classic school demonstration of molten electrolysis. It is chosen because lead bromide has a relatively low melting point (around 370 °C, easily reached with a Bunsen burner) and gives a clearly visible product at each electrode.

*Apparatus and procedure*

  • A small amount of solid PbBr₂ is placed in a crucible
  • Two inert graphite electrodes are dipped into the solid and connected to a d.c. power supply
  • The crucible is heated with a Bunsen burner until the lead bromide melts
  • Once liquid, the lamp in the circuit lights and electrolysis begins
  • The demonstration is carried out in a fume cupboard because the bromine produced is toxic and gives off an orange, choking vapour

*Why graphite electrodes?*

Graphite (a form of carbon) is inert at these temperatures and conducts electricity well. An unreactive electrode means the products you see are the products of the electrolyte itself — the electrode does not take part in the reaction.

*Observations*

  • A shiny silvery bead of molten lead forms at the bottom of the crucible (the cathode is dipped near the base) — this is the metal deposited at the negative electrode
  • An orange/brown gas with a pungent smell is given off at the anode — this is bromine vapour

*Half-equations*

At the cathode (reduction — gain of electrons):

Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb

At the anode (oxidation — loss of electrons):

2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻

*Overall equation*

PbBr₂ (l) → Pb (l) + Br₂ (g)

*Explaining the result*

When the PbBr₂ melts, the Pb²⁺ ions and Br⁻ ions become free to move. The Pb²⁺ ions are attracted to the negative cathode, where each gains two electrons to become a neutral lead atom. The Br⁻ ions are attracted to the positive anode, where each loses one electron to form a bromine atom; pairs of bromine atoms then bond covalently to give Br₂ gas. The overall effect is that lead bromide has been decomposed into its two elements — exactly the definition of electrolysis.

Predicting the products of any binary ionic compound (Spec 3.26)

For 3.26, you must be able to predict the products of electrolysis of any binary, ionic compound in the molten state — not just lead bromide. Because there are only two ions involved, the prediction follows the same two-step rule every time:

  1. Identify the metal cation — the metal ion goes to the cathode and is discharged as the metal element.
  2. Identify the non-metal anion — the non-metal ion goes to the anode and is discharged as the non-metal element.

If the non-metal anion is a single atom (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻), it pairs up to form a diatomic molecule (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂). If the anion is oxide (O²⁻), it forms O₂ gas.

Why some metals are extracted this way

Reactive metals such as sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium and aluminium are too reactive to be extracted by reduction with carbon. Their ores must be melted and electrolysed instead, because the electric current provides the energy to force the metal ions to gain electrons. The molten electrolysis you study at GCSE is therefore the same chemistry as industrial extraction in a smelter — only on a smaller scale and using a different compound.

For example, aluminium is extracted industrially from molten aluminium oxide (mixed with cryolite to lower its melting point). The cathode and anode reactions are exactly the ones in the table above:

  • Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al
  • Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻

Safety considerations for the demonstration

  • Bromine vapour is toxic and corrosive — the demonstration is done in a fume cupboard, and a teacher (not a student) carries it out.
  • Molten lead bromide is hot (around 370 °C); the crucible must be left to cool on a heat-resistant mat.
  • Lead and lead compounds are toxic, so the residue is disposed of as hazardous waste.
  • Goggles and gloves are essential.

These practical points are often the focus of 1-mark recall questions on the demonstration.

Common student errors

*Confusing the electrode names with their charge*

The cathode is the negative electrode (attracts positive cations); the anode is the positive electrode (attracts negative anions). A useful memory aid is that PANDA — Positive Anode, Negative cathode — or "ions go to the opposite charge".

*Forgetting the diatomic non-metals*

Bromine, chlorine, iodine, hydrogen and oxygen are all diatomic. At the anode you do not get Br atoms — you get Br₂ molecules. The half-equation must show 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻, not Br⁻ → Br + e⁻.

*Writing the wrong charge on the anode half-equation*

Electrons are released on the right-hand side when an anion is oxidised: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻. A common slip is to write the electrons on the left (which would describe reduction, not oxidation).

*Confusing molten with aqueous*

Always read the question carefully: "molten" or "(l)" means only the two ions of the compound are present; "aqueous" or "(aq)" means water is also present and you may get hydrogen or oxygen instead. Mixing these up is one of the easiest ways to lose marks on this topic.

3.2.4.1 Electrolysis & Redox H only ▼

Electrolysis & Redox

Spec points 3.27, 3.28 and 3.29: write half-equations for reactions at the anode and cathode; explain oxidation and reduction in terms of loss or gain of electrons; recall that reduction occurs at the cathode and oxidation at the anode in electrolysis reactions. **Higher tier only** — Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).

Electrolysis is, at heart, a redox process driven by electricity. The d.c. power supply pulls electrons off one side of the circuit and pushes them onto the other, forcing ions to gain or lose electrons that they would never exchange spontaneously. To answer Higher tier questions on this topic you must be able to: (i) classify what is happening at each electrode in terms of electron transfer, (ii) write balanced half-equations, and (iii) link the right label (oxidation/reduction) to the right electrode (anode/cathode).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
OxidationLoss of electrons by a species
ReductionGain of electrons by a species
Redox reactionA reaction in which both oxidation and reduction take place at the same time
CathodeThe negative electrode in electrolysis — the site of reduction
AnodeThe positive electrode in electrolysis — the site of oxidation
CationA positively charged ion; migrates to the cathode
AnionA negatively charged ion; migrates to the anode
Half-equationA balanced equation showing the change at one electrode only, including the electrons gained or lost
OILRIGA mnemonic for redox: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)

Oxidation and reduction in terms of electrons (OILRIG)

The Higher tier definitions of oxidation and reduction at GCSE are purely about electron transfer. Older definitions involving oxygen or hydrogen still hold true, but the electron definition is more general and is the one Edexcel will reward in half-equation questions.

  • Oxidation Is Loss of electrons
  • Reduction Is Gain of electrons

A useful way to remember which way round the labels go is that an ion becoming a metal atom gains electrons (it has to — a positive ion has to neutralise its charge), so it is reduced. A negative ion losing electrons to become a neutral molecule is oxidised. Every electrolysis you study at GCSE has reduction at the cathode and oxidation at the anode — without exception.

Why reduction happens at the cathode and oxidation at the anode

The cathode is connected to the negative terminal of the d.c. supply, so it is rich in electrons. Positive ions (cations) are attracted to it and accept those electrons — they are reduced to neutral atoms or molecules. The anode is connected to the positive terminal, so it is electron-poor. Negative ions (anions) are attracted to it and surrender their extra electrons — they are oxidised.

A neat mnemonic to keep cathode/anode straight is:

  • PANIC — Positive is ANode, Negative Is Cathode
  • or remember CAt (Cathode → Cation) and AN (Anode → Anion)

The take-home rule is that the electrons always travel through the external circuit from anode to cathode, while the ions themselves move through the electrolyte to the oppositely charged electrode.

Writing a half-equation — a step-by-step walkthrough

A half-equation has three components on either side of the arrow: the species before the change, the species after the change, and the electrons that account for the gain or loss. Follow this procedure:

  1. Start with the ion — write the symbol and the correct charge, e.g. Cu²⁺ or Cl⁻.
  2. Decide which side it is on. If the ion is being discharged at the cathode (gaining electrons), put it on the left. If it is being discharged at the anode (losing electrons), it also starts on the left — what changes is which side the electrons appear.
  3. Write the product of the discharge on the right. For cations this is usually the neutral metal atom or a neutral gas. For anions it is usually a neutral non-metal molecule (often diatomic, e.g. Cl₂, Br₂, O₂, H₂).
  4. Balance the atoms first — diatomic gases need a coefficient of 2 on the ion side.
  5. Balance the charge by adding electrons (e⁻) to whichever side needs more negative charge. Add electrons to the left for reduction (cathode); add electrons to the right for oxidation (anode).
  6. Check that both atoms and charge balance.

Worked example — cathode reduction of aluminium ions

You are asked to write the half-equation for the reduction of aluminium ions at the cathode during electrolysis of molten aluminium oxide.

  • Start with the ion: Al³⁺
  • Product: a neutral aluminium atom, Al
  • Atoms balance (1 Al on each side).
  • Charge: left side is +3, right side is 0. To balance, add 3 electrons to the left:

$$\text{Al}^{3+} + 3\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Al}$$

Both atoms and charge balance, and the electrons appear on the side of the cation (reduction). This is Mathematical Skill 1c — use of ratios, fractions and percentages applied to stoichiometric coefficients and electron number.

Worked example — anode oxidation of bromide ions

  • Start with the ion: Br⁻
  • Product: bromine gas, Br₂ (diatomic)
  • Atoms: you need 2 Br⁻ on the left to make one Br₂ on the right:

$$2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2$$

  • Charge: left side is −2, right side is 0. Add 2 electrons to the right (oxidation = loss of electrons):

$$2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$$

The five named pitfalls from that report are worth memorising, because they are the same mistakes that lose marks every year:

  • The reverse reaction — students wrote Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ instead of Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn. The clue is in the command word: reduction means electrons are gained, so they must appear on the left. If your half-equation has electrons on the right, you have written an oxidation.

  • Writing "Zn2" instead of Zn²⁺ — the superscript and the positive sign matter. Zn2 is meaningless; Zn²⁺ is a zinc ion with a +2 charge.
  • Subtracting electrons instead of adding them — some scripts had Zn²⁺ − 2e⁻ → Zn. Electrons are never subtracted in chemistry half-equations; they are added to whichever side balances the charge. In a reduction, that means adding them to the same side as the cation.

If you can avoid these five errors, you will almost certainly secure the marks on a half-equation question.

Mathematical Skills

This SP draws on Mathematical Skill 1c — use of ratios, fractions and percentages. You apply it whenever you balance the stoichiometric coefficients and the number of electrons in a half-equation. The key principle is that the ratio of electrons to ions must match the size of the charge being neutralised.

`` Number of electrons in half-equation = |charge of ion| × (number of ions in the equation) ``

Worked procedure for any half-equation:

  1. Identify the charge on the ion (e.g. Al³⁺ → |+3| = 3).
  2. Multiply by the coefficient of the ion in the equation (1 Al³⁺ → 1 × 3 = 3 electrons).
  3. Place the electrons on the correct side (cathode → left; anode → right).
  4. Confirm that atoms and charge both balance.

For 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻: charge on Br⁻ is |−1| = 1, coefficient is 2, so 1 × 2 = 2 electrons. They go on the right because this is an oxidation.

For 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻: charge on OH⁻ is |−1| = 1, coefficient is 4, so 4 electrons; placed on the right (oxidation). Typical tariff for half-equation questions: 1–2 marks.

Core Practical: Electrolysis of Copper(II)Sulfate

Syllabus 3.31: Investigate the electrolysis of copper sulfate solution with inert electrodes and copper electrodes. Examined at Foundation and Higher tier on Paper 1 (1CH0/1F and 1CH0/1H) for both Separate Chemistry and Combined Science (1SC0).

This core practical asks you to electrolyse the same electrolyte — aqueous copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄ (aq) — using two different sets of electrodes, and compare what happens. The contrast between the two setups is the whole point of the experiment: identical solution, different electrodes, very different observations. You then take measurements (the mass change at each electrode), plot a graph and analyse the relationship between mass change and the variable you controlled (usually current or time).

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ElectrolysisThe breakdown of an ionic compound (the electrolyte), when molten or in solution, using electricity
ElectrolyteThe liquid or solution that conducts electricity in electrolysis because it contains mobile ions
CathodeThe negative electrode; positive ions (cations) move towards it and gain electrons (are reduced)
AnodeThe positive electrode; negative ions (anions) move towards it and lose electrons (are oxidised) — or, with reactive electrodes, the metal of the electrode itself loses electrons

Apparatus

The same basic setup is used for both parts of the practical:

  • 100 cm³ beaker containing copper(II) sulfate solution (typically 0.5 mol/dm³ or 1.0 mol/dm³ — a clear blue solution)
  • Two electrodes held vertically in the solution by a lid or clamp so they do not touch each other

- Part 1 — inert electrodes: two carbon (graphite) rods, or two platinum electrodes - Part 2 — copper electrodes: two strips of pure copper

  • Low-voltage DC power supply (typically 4–6 V)
  • Connecting wires with crocodile clips
  • Ammeter in series (to read the current; allows you to keep current constant)
  • Variable resistor (optional, to set the current)
  • Balance reading to at least 0.01 g, for weighing the electrodes before and after
  • Distilled water, paper towels and a hairdryer or warm oven for drying electrodes
  • Eye protection

The circuit is wired up so that the power pack pushes a steady current through the cell. The two electrodes must be the same depth in the solution each time, and they must not touch.

Important — do not draw an a.c. supply: the supply must be direct current (DC), so that one electrode is consistently the cathode (–) and the other consistently the anode (+). If a.c. is used, the polarity reverses 50 times per second and no net deposition occurs.

Method — Part 1: Inert (Carbon) Electrodes

  1. Pour approximately 50 cm³ of copper(II) sulfate solution into the beaker. Note the colour (deep blue).
  2. Clean two carbon rods with emery paper, rinse them with distilled water and dry them.
  3. Weigh each electrode separately on the balance and record the initial mass (m₁ for the cathode, m₁ for the anode).
  4. Set up the circuit: connect the two carbon rods to the power supply via the ammeter and variable resistor, with both rods dipping into the solution but not touching.
  5. Switch on the power supply and adjust the variable resistor so that a steady current flows (typically 0.5 A).
  6. Run for a fixed time (e.g. 10 minutes). Observe what is happening at each electrode and to the solution.
  7. Switch off, carefully remove each electrode, rinse gently in distilled water (so that solid is not knocked off the cathode), dry without rubbing, and reweigh. Record the final mass (m₂) of each electrode.
  8. Calculate the change in mass at the cathode and at the anode.
  9. Repeat the experiment at different currents (e.g. 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 A) for the same time, to investigate how current affects the mass deposited.

Variables, fair test and reliability

  • Independent variable (the one you change): usually current, sometimes time
  • Dependent variable (the one you measure): change in mass of the cathode (or the anode)

Change in mass at cathode = 5.78 g − 5.62 g = 0.16 g

Explaining the Electrolysis of Copper(II)Sulfate

Spec point 3.30: Explain the formation of the products in the electrolysis of copper sulfate solution, using copper electrodes, and how this electrolysis can be used to purify copper. Foundation + Higher tier; both 1CH0 and 1SC0; Paper 1.

The practical setup and what you actually see is covered in 3.2.5.1 Core Practical: Electrolysis of Copper(II)Sulfate. This note is the explanation half — what is happening in terms of ions and electrons, why the mass of each electrode changes the way it does, why the blue colour of the solution does not fade, and how exactly the same process is scaled up in industry to purify copper.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ElectrolysisThe breakdown of an ionic compound (molten or in solution) by passing an electric current through it
ElectrolyteThe ionic liquid that conducts the current — here, aqueous copper(II) sulfate, CuSO₄ (aq)
CathodeThe negative electrode; positive ions (cations) move to it and are reduced (gain electrons)
AnodeThe positive electrode; negative ions (anions), or in this case copper atoms themselves, are oxidised (lose electrons)
Inert electrodeAn electrode (e.g. graphite or platinum) that does not take part in the reaction

The Half-equations You Must Know

Copper(II) sulfate solution contains four kinds of ion: Cu²⁺ (aq), SO₄²⁻ (aq), H⁺ (aq) and OH⁻ (aq). With copper electrodes, only the copper ions and copper atoms take part — the sulfate, hydrogen and hydroxide ions stay as spectators.

*At the cathode (negative electrode):*

Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu (s)

Copper(II) ions in the solution gain two electrons and are deposited as a thin pink-brown layer of copper metal on the cathode. The cathode therefore gains mass. This is reduction (gain of electrons).

*At the anode (positive electrode):*

Cu (s) → Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻

A copper atom on the surface of the anode loses two electrons and goes into the solution as a Cu²⁺ ion. The anode therefore loses mass. This is oxidation (loss of electrons).

Why the Solution Stays Blue

The blue colour of copper sulfate solution is caused by Cu²⁺ ions. With copper electrodes, Cu²⁺ ions are being added at the anode at the same rate as they are being removed at the cathode. Because the two rates are equal (every electron of charge that flows through the circuit removes one Cu²⁺ at the cathode for every Cu²⁺ added at the anode), the concentration of Cu²⁺ in the solution is constant — and so is the depth of the blue colour.

This is exactly the point Edexcel examiners highlight as the make-or-break idea: students who can say "the rate at which Cu²⁺ ions are added at the anode equals the rate at which they are removed at the cathode" pick up the mark; students who just say "the colour doesn't change" do not.

With inert electrodes (graphite or platinum) the rules for aqueous electrolysis from 3.2.2.1 Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions apply: at the anode water/hydroxide ions are discharged rather than sulfate, because sulfate is too stable to lose electrons. Using This Reaction to Purify Copper Industrially

Copper extracted from its ore is too impure to use in high-quality electrical wiring (impurities raise the electrical resistance). Electrolysis with copper electrodes is used to purify it. The chemistry is identical to the small-scale experiment — only the scale and the choice of starting material are different.

*The industrial setup:*

  • Impure copper (often called blister copper, around 99% pure) is cast into thick slabs and used as the anode.
  • Thin sheets of pure copper are used as the cathode.
  • The electrolyte is copper(II) sulfate solution, usually acidified with sulfuric acid to increase its conductivity.
  • A low voltage DC current is passed through the cell.

*What happens to each kind of metal in the impure anode:*

  • Copper atoms lose two electrons and dissolve into the electrolyte as Cu²⁺ ions, then plate out as pure copper on the cathode. This is the main reaction.
  • More reactive impurities (commonly zinc, iron, nickel) lose electrons more easily than copper, so they also dissolve into the solution as ions. But they are more difficult to discharge at the cathode than Cu²⁺, so they stay in solution as ions and do not plate out on the pure-copper cathode.
  • Less reactive impurities (silver, gold, platinum) do not lose electrons easily — they cannot dissolve at the anode under these conditions. As the surrounding copper dissolves away, they simply fall off the anode and collect at the bottom of the tank as anode sludge.

The reactivity logic here is the same as in 4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals and 4.1.2.1 Displacement Reactions & Redox — more reactive metals lose electrons more readily; less reactive metals resist losing electrons.

*Why the anode sludge matters economically:*

The anode sludge is not waste and it is not impure copper — a common misconception flagged in the examiner reports below. It is mostly silver, gold and other precious metals that were trapped in the original ore. The sludge is collected, processed, and sold separately. For many copper refineries this byproduct is what makes the whole process profitable.

Final purity: the copper that builds up on the cathode is typically over 99.99% pure — clean enough for high-conductivity wiring and electronics.

Worked Example — 3-mark "Explain in terms of ions" Question

This is the style used on 1CH0/1H June 2022 Q5(c)(i).

Question: Explain, in terms of ions, the changes in mass of the two electrodes when copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed using copper electrodes. (3 marks)

*Model answer:*

> At the cathode, Cu²⁺ ions in solution gain electrons (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu) and are deposited as solid copper, so the cathode gains mass. (1) At the anode, copper atoms lose electrons (Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻) and go into solution as Cu²⁺ ions, so the anode loses mass. (1) For every Cu²⁺ ion removed at the cathode, another Cu²⁺ ion is added at the anode, so the concentration (and blue colour) of the solution does not change. (1)

*Mark-by-mark logic:*

  • M1: correct cathode process — Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu and cathode gains mass. The half-equation is not strictly required at this tariff, but naming the ion gaining electrons is.
  • M2: correct anode process — Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ and anode loses mass.
  • M3: link the two rates to explain why concentration/colour stays constant.

A common dropped-mark version simply states the observation ("the anode loses mass") without explaining it in terms of ions — examiners will not award the mark for this.

Worked Example — Mass-difference Arithmetic and Graph Reading

Mathematical skills 1a, 4a, 4b, 4c and 4d are all in scope here. A typical follow-up part asks you to find a mass difference, or to plot/read a graph of mass change against time or current.

Question: A student electrolysed copper(II) sulfate solution with copper electrodes. After 20 minutes the cathode mass had increased from 1.84 g to 2.16 g, and the anode mass had decreased from 4.50 g to 4.18 g.

(a) Calculate the change in mass of the cathode. (b) Calculate the change in mass of the anode. (c) Compare your answers and explain what you notice.

*Working:*

`` (a) Δm(cathode) = 2.16 − 1.84 = +0.32 g (gained) (b) Δm(anode) = 4.18 − 4.50 = −0.32 g (lost) (c) The mass gained at the cathode equals the mass lost at the anode. Every copper atom that dissolves from the anode (Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻) is deposited on the cathode (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu), so the mass transfers one-for-one across the cell. ``

Graph version (math skills 4a–4d): If you are given a table of cathode mass vs time and asked to plot it, expect a straight line through the origin (assuming a constant current). The gradient is the rate of mass deposition in g/min or g/s. To find it, pick two well-separated points on the line:

gradient = (Δ mass) / (Δ time)

A steeper line means a higher current (more charge per second means more electrons, so more Cu²⁺ reduced per second).

Examiner Report Insights

The examiner reports for this spec point flag four recurring problems. Quote each one back to yourself when you revise so you know not to fall into them.

  • 1CH0/1F — June 2019 Examiner Report: "Some gained credit for saying that both electrodes increased in mass, the thought often being that the cathode gained pure copper while the anode gained impure copper. Many thought that the solid appearing under the anode was copper, often impure copper... Very few correctly described why the solution does not change in colour. It was very clear that very few candidates indeed had any real understanding of what was going on in this process even though this is a Core practical."
  • 1CH0/1H — November 2021 Examiner Report: "Explaining what happens in electrolysis continues to be a weak area for candidates. Very few marks were awarded here with confusion between electrons and ions, and no apparent understanding of why the concentration of the solution does not change during electrolysis with copper electrodes. There was also a misconception that the loss and gain of electrons at the electrodes are the cause of the changes in mass."

*What these tell you:*

  1. Do not say both electrodes gain mass. The cathode gains, the anode loses. Saying "both gain" is one of the most common dropped marks.
  2. The solid at the bottom of the cell (industrial process) is not copper. It is anode sludge, mostly silver and gold — the less reactive impurities. Calling it "impure copper" is wrong.
  3. Be precise with electrons vs ions. Electrons flow round the external circuit (the wires). Ions move through the solution. The change in mass is caused by Cu²⁺ ions being deposited as Cu atoms (cathode) or Cu atoms going into solution as Cu²⁺ ions (anode) — not by electrons themselves having mass. Electrons are essentially massless on this scale.
  4. Explain why concentration is constant. "Cu²⁺ ions are removed at the cathode at the same rate as they are added at the anode" — examiners want this rate-balance idea, not just "it stays the same".

Key Takeaways
  1. With copper electrodes, copper(II) sulfate solution undergoes electrolysis in which copper transfers from the anode to the cathode through the solution.
  2. Cathode half-equation: Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu (s) — the cathode gains mass as copper is deposited (reduction).
  3. Anode half-equation: Cu (s) → Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ — the anode loses mass as copper atoms dissolve (oxidation).
  4. The mass gained by the cathode equals the mass lost by the anode — copper transfers one-for-one across the cell.
  5. The blue colour of the solution does not fade, because Cu²⁺ ions are removed at the cathode at the same rate as they are added at the anode.
  6. This is fundamentally different from electrolysis with inert electrodes (graphite/platinum), where O₂ is produced at the anode, the blue colour fades, and the solution becomes acidic.
  7. The same process is used industrially to purify copper: impure copper is the anode, pure copper is the cathode, the electrolyte is copper(II) sulfate (acidified).
  8. More reactive impurities (Zn, Fe, Ni) dissolve into the solution but stay there as ions — they are not deposited on the cathode.
  9. Less reactive impurities (Ag, Au, Pt) do not dissolve. They drop from the anode and collect as anode sludge at the bottom of the cell — this is not impure copper, and is valuable enough to be sold separately.
  10. The refined copper at the cathode is over 99.99% pure, suitable for electrical wiring.
  11. Common exam errors flagged by examiners: claiming both electrodes gain mass; calling the anode sludge "impure copper"; confusing electrons and ions; failing to explain why the solution colour stays the same.
  12. Electrons flow through the external wires; ions move through the solution. The mass changes are caused by ions, not by the electrons themselves.
