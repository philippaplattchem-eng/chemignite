The Three States of Matter

Spec 2.1: Describe the arrangement, movement and the relative energy of particles in each of the three states of matter: solid, liquid and gas. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate + Combined Science, Paper 1.

Every substance you meet at GCSE is one of three states at a given temperature: solid, liquid or gas. The state a substance is in is decided by two things — how much kinetic energy its particles have, and how strong the forces are pulling those particles together. The particle model gives you a simple but powerful way to picture and explain the differences. Get this spec point right and you have the foundation for changes of state (2.1.1.2), interconversions (2.1.1.3), gas pressure, dissolving, diffusion and almost everything that follows in Section 2.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
ParticleA general term used in the particle model for the smallest repeating units of a substance — atoms, molecules or ions
State of matterOne of three physical forms in which a substance can exist: solid, liquid or gas
ArrangementHow the particles are positioned relative to each other — regular and touching, irregular but touching, or far apart and random
MovementHow the particles move — vibrating in fixed positions, sliding past one another, or moving freely in straight lines
Relative energyA comparison of the kinetic energy of the particles in each state, from lowest (solid) to highest (gas)
Kinetic energyThe energy a particle has because it is moving (or vibrating). The hotter the substance, the more kinetic energy its particles have
Forces of attractionThe forces holding particles together. Strongest in a solid, weaker in a liquid, almost negligible in a gas

Particle-model diagrams

You should be able to draw and recognise simple particle diagrams for all three states. Examiners frequently ask for these or test them via multiple-choice.

  • Solid: Draw a regular square or hexagonal close-packed arrangement of identical touching circles, lined up neatly in rows and columns. The circles all touch their neighbours and there are no gaps. Add small arrows or "vibration" symbols (short double-headed arrows) to show that the particles vibrate in place but do not move around.
  • Liquid: Draw the same circles still mostly touching, but in an irregular arrangement — not lined up in rows. Some circles touch many neighbours, some touch fewer. There may be the occasional small gap. Use longer arrows in random directions to show the particles slide past each other.
  • Gas: Draw the circles spread far apart, with large gaps between them, in completely random positions. Use long arrows in random directions to show fast straight-line motion. The particles should occupy only a small fraction of the box.

These three pictures (regular and touching, irregular and touching, far apart and random) are the visual shorthand examiners expect you to recognise. Whenever you sketch one, label the state and show movement with arrows so the mark scheme can see you have applied the model correctly.

Why the particles behave differently in each state

It is not enough to memorise the table — Paper 1 questions reward students who can explain why the differences exist. The explanation comes down to a competition between two things: the kinetic energy of the particles (which makes them move around and spread out) and the forces of attraction between them (which pull them together).

Why solids hold their shape and volume. In a solid, the particles do not have much kinetic energy. The forces of attraction between them are strong enough to lock each particle into a fixed position within a regular lattice. The particles still move — they vibrate about their fixed positions — but they cannot break free from their neighbours. Because the particles cannot move around or slide past each other, a solid keeps a fixed shape and a fixed volume. Because the particles are touching, you cannot squash them any closer together, which is why solids cannot be compressed.

Why liquids take the shape of their container. When a solid is heated, the particles gain kinetic energy. Eventually they have enough energy to break out of the regular lattice positions and slide past each other, although the forces of attraction are still strong enough to keep them in contact. The result is a substance whose particles can flow past each other (so the liquid takes the shape of its container) but which still has a fixed volume (because the particles are still mostly touching). Liquids are only very slightly less dense than the corresponding solid because the particles are almost as close together.

Why gases fill the whole container. Heating a liquid gives its particles even more kinetic energy. When the particles have enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction completely, they break apart from each other and move freely in random directions. Because the forces of attraction are essentially negligible, nothing keeps the particles close together — they spread out to fill any container they are put in. The large gaps between particles also mean a gas can be compressed by squashing those gaps shut.

Why "relative energy" is the key idea. Notice that you never say exactly how much energy the particles have — you only say which state has more or less than another. The spec wording is "relative energy" because the absolute kinetic energy depends on temperature: a solid at 200 °C has more kinetic energy than a gas at –200 °C in absolute terms. What matters is that within a given substance at a given temperature, particles in the gas state have more kinetic energy than particles in the liquid state, which in turn have more than particles in the solid state.

A note on "strength of forces" vs "type of bonding"

A common confusion: are we talking about the strong covalent bonds inside molecules, or the weaker forces between molecules? For state-of-matter questions, we always mean the forces between particles, not the bonds inside them. When ice melts, the covalent bonds inside each H₂O molecule are not broken; only the weaker forces between the water molecules are overcome. That is why simple molecular substances (with weak intermolecular forces) tend to be liquids or gases at room temperature, while ionic and giant covalent substances (with very strong forces between every particle) are solids with high melting points. This link to bonding is developed properly in 1.5.1.1 Classifying Substances.

Limitations of the particle model

The simple particle model used at GCSE is deliberately simplified. You should be aware of three limitations because they sometimes appear as 1–2 mark questions on later spec points:

  1. The particles are drawn as solid spheres, but real atoms and molecules are not solid spheres.
  2. The model shows particles as identical circles, ignoring differences in size and shape between different atoms and molecules in real substances.
  3. The model does not show the forces between particles or the gaps between them at the correct scale.

For the purposes of 2.1 these limitations do not matter — the model is good enough to predict arrangement, movement and relative energy. The full limitations discussion lives in 1.5.7.1 Limitations of Models.

Mathematical Skills

The mathematical skill listed for 2.1 is 5b: Visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms. This is the skill used when you sketch or interpret particle-model diagrams.

*How to apply skill 5b on this spec point:*

  1. Read the question carefully — is it asking for a 2D sketch, a description, or recognition of a labelled diagram?
  2. Identify the state being represented (look for clues: "regular arrangement", "vibrating", "fixed positions" all point to solid).
  3. For a sketch, draw particles as identical circles. Use regular rows/columns for a solid, irregular but touching circles for a liquid, and widely spaced random circles for a gas.
  4. Add motion arrows: short double-headed arrows on solid-state particles to show vibration; longer arrows in random directions on liquid and gas particles to show movement.
  5. Label the diagram with the state (solid / liquid / gas) and any features the question asks for (e.g. "particles touching", "gaps").

Exam technique for this spec point

A few simple rules will earn the marks reliably:

  • When asked to read state from a data table, compare the temperature in the question to the melting point and boiling point of the substance. Below melting point → solid. Between melting and boiling point → liquid. Above boiling point → gas.
  • When asked about arrangement, use the three keywords: regular and touching (solid), irregular but touching (liquid), far apart and random (gas).
  • When asked about movement, use the three keywords: vibrate in fixed positions (solid), slide past each other (liquid), move freely in random directions (gas).
  • When asked about relative energy, say which state has more or less energy than another — do not quote any numerical value.
  • When asked to draw a particle diagram, label the state, show all particles as identical circles, and add arrows to show movement.
Key Takeaways
  1. The particle model describes substances as being made of tiny particles whose arrangement, movement and relative energy determine the state of matter.
  2. In a solid, particles are in a regular arrangement, all touching, vibrating about fixed positions; the forces between them are strongest and their relative energy is lowest.
  3. In a liquid, particles are in an irregular arrangement, mostly touching, sliding past each other; the forces are weaker than in a solid and the relative energy is greater than in a solid but less than in a gas.
  4. In a gas, particles are far apart in a random arrangement, moving quickly in random directions in straight lines; the forces between them are negligible and the relative energy is highest.
  5. Solids have a fixed shape and fixed volume because the particles cannot move from place to place; liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of the container; gases have neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume.
  6. The state a substance is in at a given temperature is decided by comparing the temperature with the melting and boiling points: below MP = solid, between MP and BP = liquid, above BP = gas.
  7. Particle-model diagrams must show particles as identical circles — regular and touching for a solid, irregular but touching for a liquid, far apart and random for a gas — with arrows indicating movement.
  8. The simple particle model is a useful but simplified picture: real particles are not solid spheres, they are not all the same size, and the gaps and forces are not drawn to scale (limitations developed in 1.5.7.1 Limitations of Models).
  9. State-of-matter questions almost always appear at the start of Foundation tier Paper 1 (e.g. 1CH0/1F June 2019 Q1(c) — identify the solid at 20 °C from a melting/boiling point table) and use the command words Give, Name, Use and Predict.
  10. When discussing the forces broken on changing state, you are always talking about the forces between particles, not the bonds inside them — covalent bonds inside molecules do not break on melting or boiling.
  11. Always describe energy in relative terms (greater than / less than another state), never as an absolute number — the spec wording is "relative energy".
  12. Examiners noted in the 1CH0/1F June 2019 report that candidates need to practise interpretation of heating and cooling curves, a linked skill developed in 2.1.1.2 Changes of State.
2.1.1.2 Interconversions F+H ▼

Interconversions

Spec 2.2 + 2.3: Recall the names used for the interconversions between the three states of matter, recognising that these are physical changes contrasted with chemical reactions; and explain the changes in arrangement, movement and energy of particles during these interconversions. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry + Combined Science, Paper 1 only.

The three states of matter — solid, liquid and gas — can be converted from one to another by heating or cooling. Every one of these changes of state is a physical change, not a chemical reaction: the substance itself stays the same, only its arrangement and energy change. This single distinction is what makes the topic so important — examiners use it as the first sorting question of the paper to check that you understand the difference between a physical and a chemical change. You need to know the six interconversion names, the direction each one goes in, what happens to the particles, and how to read these changes off a heating or cooling curve.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
MeltingA solid changes to a liquid
FreezingA liquid changes to a solid
Evaporating / boilingA liquid changes to a gas (evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point)
CondensingA gas changes to a liquid
SublimationA solid changes directly to a gas without melting first
DepositionA gas changes directly to a solid without first becoming a liquid
Physical changeA change in which no new substance is made and the change can be reversed by changing the conditions (e.g. heating or cooling)
Chemical changeA change in which one or more new substances are made, usually very difficult to reverse
Melting pointThe temperature at which a pure substance changes from solid to liquid (also the temperature at which the liquid freezes back to a solid)
Boiling pointThe temperature at which a pure substance changes from liquid to gas throughout the bulk of the liquid

The six interconversions

There are three states of matter (solid, liquid and gas) and six named changes between them — one in each direction for each pair of states. Knowing these names is the very first thing examiners check, often as a 1-mark "Give the name…" question on the front page of the Foundation paper.

Notice the symmetry: every "heating" change has a "cooling" partner that does the reverse. Examples you should be able to give:

  • Melting: ice → water at 0 °C; iron melts at around 1538 °C.
  • Freezing: liquid water → ice at 0 °C; molten iron solidifies when poured into a mould.
  • Evaporating / boiling: water → steam at 100 °C; puddles disappearing on a warm pavement (evaporation, below the boiling point).
  • Condensing: steam → water; water droplets forming on a cold window.
  • Sublimation: solid carbon dioxide ("dry ice") → carbon dioxide gas at –78 °C; solid iodine when warmed gently.
  • Deposition: water vapour in air → ice crystals on a cold surface (frost); iodine vapour cooling on a cold flask.

Why interconversions are physical changes — not chemical reactions

This is the conceptual heart of the spec point and the reason 2.2 ends with the words "contrasted with chemical reactions that result in chemical changes". A change of state is physical for three linked reasons:

1. No new substance is made. When ice melts to water, the substance is still H₂O. When water boils to steam, it is still H₂O. The chemical identity of the substance is unchanged — only how its particles are arranged and how much energy they have. Compare that with a chemical reaction such as burning magnesium in oxygen: magnesium and oxygen disappear and a completely new substance, magnesium oxide, is made. That is a chemical change.

2. Mass is conserved with no rearrangement of atoms. In a physical change the same particles are still there in the same numbers — they have just spread out, packed together, or started moving faster. In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged into new combinations and new chemical bonds are made and broken.

3. The change can be reversed by reversing the conditions. Cool water and you get ice back; cool steam and you get water back. The reverse of a chemical change is usually much harder (you cannot un-burn the magnesium by cooling it down). The reversibility of changes of state is one of the simplest pieces of evidence that no new substance was made.

A useful sanity check in the exam: if you can undo the change just by changing the temperature, it is physical. If you would need a completely different chemical to undo it, it is chemical.

Arrangement, movement and energy of particles during each change

Spec 2.3 asks you to explain the changes in arrangement, movement and energy of particles during interconversions. The summary below pulls together the particle picture for each of the six changes — this is what the 5b Mathematical Skill (visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms) is testing.

Melting (solid → liquid). Heat is given to the solid. Particles vibrate more strongly about their fixed positions until the energy is enough to overcome the forces holding them in the lattice. The regular arrangement breaks down: particles can now slide past one another, although they are still touching. Arrangement: changes from regular and tightly packed to irregular and still close-packed. Movement: from vibrating about fixed positions to moving past each other. Energy: gained by particles.

Freezing (liquid → solid). Heat is taken away from the liquid. Particles slow down until the forces of attraction can lock them into a regular lattice. Arrangement: from irregular liquid to a regular solid. Movement: from sliding past one another to vibrating about fixed positions. Energy: lost by particles.

Evaporating / boiling (liquid → gas). Heat is given to the liquid. Particles speed up until they have enough energy to break free from the attractions of the other liquid particles and escape into the gas phase. Arrangement: from close-packed and irregular to far apart and random. Movement: from sliding past each other to moving very quickly in all directions. Energy: gained by particles, especially during the boiling plateau when the forces between particles are being broken.

The distinction between evaporation and boiling is small but worth knowing for higher-mark answers. Evaporation happens at the surface of a liquid at any temperature — only the most energetic particles escape. Boiling happens throughout the bulk of the liquid at the boiling point — bubbles of gas form inside the liquid because every particle has enough energy to break free.

Condensing (gas → liquid). Heat is taken away. Gas particles slow down, lose energy and the forces of attraction between them become significant again. They come close together and start sliding past each other rather than flying apart. Arrangement: from far apart and random to close-packed and irregular. Movement: from very fast and random to slower and sliding. Energy: lost.

Sublimation (solid → gas). Heat is given. Particles in the solid gain so much energy that they break free from the lattice and escape as a gas without ever forming a liquid. Common GCSE example: solid carbon dioxide ("dry ice") sublimes at –78 °C at atmospheric pressure. Arrangement: from regular solid to widely spaced gas. Movement: from vibrating about fixed points to flying around randomly. Energy: gained.

Deposition (gas → solid). Heat is taken away. Gas particles lose energy fast enough that they go straight from random gas movement to locking into a lattice — without spending time as a liquid. Common example: water vapour in air forming frost on a cold surface. Arrangement: from far apart and random to regular solid. Movement: from very fast and random to vibrating about fixed positions. Energy: lost.

Heating and cooling curves — how to read them

A heating curve (or cooling curve) is a graph of temperature against time as a substance is steadily heated (or cooled). The shape of the curve is the most powerful single piece of evidence for what is happening to the particles, and the spec point 2.3 explicitly expects you to be able to interpret one. The 5b Mathematical Skill (visualising 2D representations) maps directly onto reading these curves.

*The shape of a heating curve for a pure substance:*

  • Slope 1 (rising): Solid being heated. Temperature rises. Particles vibrate more strongly.
  • Plateau 1 (flat): Solid melting. Temperature stays constant at the melting point even though heat is still being added.
  • Slope 2 (rising): Liquid being heated. Temperature rises again. Particles move past each other faster.
  • Plateau 2 (flat): Liquid boiling. Temperature stays constant at the boiling point.
  • Slope 3 (rising): Gas being heated. Temperature rises again, often more steeply.

A cooling curve is the mirror image: the substance cools down, with flat plateaus at the boiling point (gas condensing) and melting point (liquid freezing).

Why the plateaus are flat — the most-asked exam question. During a plateau, heat energy is still being added (or removed), but the temperature does not change. The energy is being used to break the forces between the particles (on a heating curve) or letting those forces re-form (on a cooling curve), not to make the particles move faster. Temperature only rises again once every particle has changed state. This is the single key idea that students miss most often: the temperature is constant during a change of state because the energy is going into rearranging the particles, not into raising the temperature.

Worked example — interpreting a cooling curve. A pure substance is cooled steadily from 120 °C. The temperature falls smoothly from 120 °C to 80 °C, then stays flat at 80 °C for several minutes, then falls smoothly again to 20 °C, then stays flat at 20 °C, then falls to 0 °C.

Step 1: identify the sloping sections — these are stages where one state of matter is cooling. Step 2: identify the flat sections — these are changes of state. Step 3: read off the temperatures of the plateaus — these are the boiling and melting points.

Reading the curve: from 120 °C to 80 °C the substance is a gas cooling down. The plateau at 80 °C is the gas condensing to a liquid, so the boiling point of the substance is 80 °C. From 80 °C to 20 °C the liquid cools down. The plateau at 20 °C is the liquid freezing to a solid, so the melting point of the substance is 20 °C. From 20 °C to 0 °C the solid cools down.

You should be able to do the reverse on a heating curve: the first plateau is melting (at the melting point), the second plateau is boiling (at the boiling point).

Energy considerations — heating and cooling

Two simple rules cover energy changes during interconversions:

  • All "heating" changes (melting, evaporating/boiling, sublimation) absorb energy because energy is needed to overcome the forces between particles.
  • All "cooling" changes (freezing, condensing, deposition) release energy because the forces of attraction re-forming between particles release energy to the surroundings.

This is why steam at 100 °C scalds skin worse than water at 100 °C — the steam first condenses on the skin, releasing the energy that was used to vaporise it, before then cooling down as liquid water. It is also why an ice cube cools a drink: as the ice melts it absorbs energy from the drink.

Mathematical Skills

Key Takeaways
  1. There are six named interconversions between the three states of matter: melting (solid → liquid), freezing (liquid → solid), evaporating / boiling (liquid → gas), condensing (gas → liquid), sublimation (solid → gas), and deposition (gas → solid).
  2. Every change of state is a physical change — no new substance is made, mass and atoms are conserved, and the change can be reversed by reversing the temperature.
  3. A chemical change makes one or more new substances and is much harder to reverse — burning magnesium in oxygen is a chemical change; melting magnesium is a physical change.
  4. Heating changes (melting, evaporating/boiling, sublimation) absorb energy so that particles can overcome the forces holding them together. Cooling changes (freezing, condensing, deposition) release energy as those forces re-form.
  5. During melting and boiling the arrangement of particles becomes more disordered, the movement becomes faster and less restricted, and the energy of the particles increases.
  6. On a heating curve, the temperature rises during each sloping section (where one state is being heated) but stays constant during each flat plateau (where the substance is changing state).
  7. The plateaus are flat because the heat energy supplied is being used to break the forces between particles, not to make the particles move faster — so the temperature does not rise until the change of state is complete.
  8. The first plateau on a heating curve is melting (at the melting point); the second is boiling (at the boiling point). On a cooling curve, the first plateau is condensing and the second is freezing.
  9. Sublimation (solid → gas without melting) and deposition (gas → solid without first condensing to a liquid) are the two "skip the liquid" changes — examples are dry ice subliming and frost forming on a cold window.
  10. The June 2019 1CH0/1F examiner report explicitly recommends practising interpretation of heating and cooling curves — examiners want you to read off melting and boiling points from plateaus, not just recite the six names.
  11. Spec 2.3 maps onto Mathematical Skill 5b — being able to visualise and represent particle arrangements in 2D/3D and read off temperatures and states from a labelled heating or cooling curve.
  12. Questions on this spec point appear almost exclusively at the front of Foundation Paper 1 (Q1 or Q2) as 1-mark Give/Name questions — easy marks if the six names and their directions are learned cold.
2.1.1.3 Predicting Physical State F+H ▼

Predicting Physical State

Spec 2.4: Predict the physical state of a substance under specified conditions, given suitable data. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) + Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 1 only.

This is a small spec point on paper but it is one of the highest-frequency easy-mark earners in Paper 1. You are given a table of substances with their melting points and boiling points, plus a temperature (often room temperature, 20 °C or 25 °C), and asked which substance is a solid, which is a liquid, which is a gas — or to give the letter of the one that matches a particular state. Nothing about the chemistry of the substance is involved; the whole question is about reading numbers off a table and applying three simple rules consistently.

The skill is conceptually trivial once you have the rules in your head, but in real exams a surprisingly large number of marks are lost here through carelessness — reversing the rule, mixing up melting point with boiling point, or forgetting what happens exactly at the melting or boiling point. The aim of this note is to nail the three rules, work through several examples until they are automatic, and flag the traps that cost marks.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Melting point (mp)The temperature at which a substance changes from solid to liquid (and the same temperature at which a liquid freezes to a solid)
Boiling point (bp)The temperature at which a substance changes from liquid to gas throughout its bulk (and the same temperature at which a gas condenses to a liquid)

The three-rule decision — how to predict the state

The whole spec point boils down to comparing the specified temperature, T, with the melting point and the boiling point of the substance. There are exactly three cases:

  1. If T is less than the melting point (T < mp), the substance is a solid. The temperature is too low for the particles to break free of their fixed positions — they only vibrate.
  2. If T is between the melting point and the boiling point (mp < T < bp), the substance is a liquid. There is enough energy to overcome the forces holding the particles in fixed positions, but not enough to separate them into a gas.
  3. If T is greater than the boiling point (T > bp), the substance is a gas. There is enough energy for the particles to overcome the forces holding them close together, so they move freely.

A useful mental picture: imagine a temperature axis running from very cold on the left to very hot on the right. The melting point and boiling point are two vertical lines on that axis. The space to the left of the melting point is solid, the space between the two lines is liquid, and the space to the right of the boiling point is gas. Plotting the specified temperature on that axis and noting which zone it falls into gives the state immediately.

What happens at exactly the melting point or boiling point

Two edge cases need attention because exam questions occasionally exploit them.

At exactly the melting point, the substance is in the process of changing state. Strictly speaking, it can be either solid or liquid (or a mixture of both) because the temperature stays constant while the change happens. In most GCSE exam contexts, T = mp is treated as "just melting" — answers of either solid or liquid are usually accepted, but the question is rarely phrased to land on this knife edge. If it does, follow the wording of the question. "At which temperature does substance X begin to melt?" points to the melting point itself; "At 20 °C, what state is substance X?" with mp also 20 °C is usually accepted as either.

At exactly the boiling point, the substance is changing from liquid to gas. The same reasoning applies — liquid or gas would both be defensible, but the question will normally place the temperature clearly above or below the boiling point to avoid the ambiguity.

The most reliable strategy is to read the temperature carefully against the table and apply rule 1, 2 or 3 above without overthinking. Edge cases at exactly the mp or bp are uncommon — most exam tables are designed so that the answer is unambiguous.

Common pitfalls

Reversing the rule. The most common error is to write that T < mp means the substance is a liquid (because it is "above" the value), or T > bp means it is a solid. Burn the order in: cold = solid, warm = liquid, hot = gas. The melting point is the boundary between solid and liquid (because that is the temperature at which a solid melts to a liquid); the boiling point is the boundary between liquid and gas.

Mixing up mp and bp columns. Look very carefully at column headings. Most tables list melting point first, then boiling point, but some are reversed. Many questions are now drawn from real-data sets that include values like –183 °C and 1538 °C in the same column — checking that the smaller number is the melting point is a fast sanity check, because mp < bp always.

Ignoring the negative sign. With values like –114 °C and –183 °C, students sometimes treat the larger-magnitude number as the bigger value. It is not. –183 is colder than –114, because –183 is further from zero on the cold side. Always compare on a number line: –219 < –183 < –114 < –7 < 0 < 25 < 78 < 100 < 801.

Forgetting that mercury is liquid at room temperature. Mercury and bromine are the two famous "liquid at room temperature" exceptions on the periodic table. A table that lists mercury at room temperature should give a liquid answer — and the student who instinctively says "metal, so solid" without checking the data will lose the mark.

Not reading the temperature stated in the stem. Most questions specify a temperature — usually 20 °C or 25 °C, but sometimes a higher or lower one. Read it. The whole answer depends on which temperature you compare with.

Giving the wrong type of answer. If the question says "Give the letter of the substance that is a solid at 20 °C", the answer is a single letter (e.g. E) — not the name of the substance, not a sentence. Read the command word carefully.

Mathematical Skills

The mathematical skills called up by spec 2.4 are 1d — make estimates of the results of simple calculations and 4a — translate information between graphical and numeric form.

In practice this means two things.

The "estimate" wording covers the fact that you do not have to know the exact temperature; you just need to know which side of the mp and bp boundaries it lies.

Skill 4a — translate between graphical and numeric form. Sometimes the data is given as a graph (heating curve, cooling curve) rather than a table of numbers. The same logic applies, but you are now reading values off plateau regions of the curve:

`` Heating curve plateau 1 (flat region) = melting point Heating curve plateau 2 (flat region) = boiling point ``

The procedure on a heating curve is:

  1. Identify the first flat region on the curve — that temperature is the melting point.
  2. Identify the second flat region — that temperature is the boiling point.
  3. Find the stated temperature on the vertical axis.
  4. Apply the three-rule decision: below first plateau = solid, between plateaus = liquid, above second plateau = gas.

The numeric and graphical versions of this skill are the same problem dressed up differently.

Pure Substance vs Mixture

Spec 2.5: Explain the difference between the use of 'pure' in chemistry compared with its everyday use and the differences in chemistry between a pure substance and a mixture. Spec 2.6: Interpret melting point data to distinguish between pure substances which have a sharp melting point and mixtures which melt over a range of temperatures. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate + Combined Science, Paper 1.

The single biggest trap on this spec point is that the word pure means something different in chemistry from how it is used in everyday English. Get the definition right, and the rest of the topic — including the melting point graphs that show up in Paper 1 — falls into place very quickly.

The everyday meaning of "pure" vs the chemistry meaning

In everyday language, "pure" usually means natural, unprocessed, or with nothing added. Adverts talk about "pure orange juice" or "pure milk" — meaning the juice has not had sugar, water or preservatives added to it. The label "pure spring water" is meant to tell you the water hasn't been treated with chemicals. In this everyday sense, the orange juice is "pure" because it is just orange juice, with no other ingredients.

In chemistry the word pure is much stricter. A pure substance contains only one chemical species — either one element (e.g. pure copper, pure oxygen, pure diamond) or one compound (e.g. pure water, pure sodium chloride, pure ethanol). Anything else is a mixture.

That means that orange juice — even from a freshly squeezed orange with absolutely nothing added — is not a pure substance in chemistry. Orange juice contains water, sugars, citric acid, vitamin C, plant pigments and dozens of other compounds. The everyday label "pure" really means "unadulterated", but to a chemist it is a mixture.

Other useful contrasts:

  • Sea water (everyday: "natural") — chemistry classifies it as a mixture of water, sodium chloride, magnesium sulfate and many other salts.
  • Air — a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour, even though it looks like a single colourless gas.
  • Steel — a mixture (alloy) of iron with carbon and sometimes other metals; not a pure substance.
  • Pure copper — a chemistry-pure substance, containing only copper atoms.
  • Distilled water — chemistry-pure, because the distillation process leaves behind dissolved salts and gases.
  • Tap water — not chemistry-pure, because it contains dissolved minerals and treatment chemicals.

The everyday/chemistry distinction is the most common 2-mark explain question in this part of the spec. If you are asked "Explain the difference between the everyday use of the word 'pure' and the chemistry use of the word 'pure'", your answer needs both sides: the everyday meaning (nothing added / unadulterated) and the chemistry meaning (a single element or single compound).

What counts as a pure substance and what counts as a mixture

A pure substance is either a single element or a single compound. Both count as pure, because in each case the sample contains only one type of chemical species:

  • A pure element is made of one kind of atom only. Examples: a sample of pure copper contains only copper atoms; pure neon contains only neon atoms; a diamond is pure carbon.
  • A pure compound contains only one kind of compound, with a fixed ratio of atoms set by the formula. Pure water is always H₂O — two hydrogen atoms to every oxygen atom — and never anything else.

A mixture, by contrast, has two key features:

  1. It contains two or more elements or compounds.
  2. Those elements or compounds are not chemically combined — there are no chemical bonds between them, and they keep their own individual properties.

A third, very useful feature: the components of a mixture can be present in any proportion. Sea water from one ocean can have a different salt concentration from sea water from another, but both are still sea water. Bronze made with 12% tin behaves slightly differently from bronze made with 10% tin, but both are still bronze. By contrast, a compound has a fixed composition set by its chemical formula — pure water is always 11.2% hydrogen and 88.8% oxygen by mass, and nothing else counts as water.

Because there are no chemical bonds between the components of a mixture, mixtures can usually be separated by physical methods — filtration, evaporation, distillation, chromatography. Compounds can only be split apart by chemical reactions that break the bonds inside them, which usually takes a lot more energy.

Interpreting melting point data to spot pure substances and mixtures

Spec point 2.6 is where the topic becomes a data-handling skill. You will be given a table of melting points — sometimes the temperature at which melting started and finished, sometimes a literature value compared to a measured value — and asked to decide whether each sample is pure or impure.

The underlying rule is simple:

  • A pure substance melts at one fixed temperature — a sharp melting point.
  • A mixture (or a substance with impurities in it) melts over a range of temperatures, usually starting below the melting point of the pure version and finishing close to it.

The reason is that in a pure solid every particle is identical and held in the lattice by the same forces, so all the particles need the same amount of energy to overcome those forces and start moving. The whole sample reaches its melting point at once. In a mixture, the particles are different, the forces between them are not uniform, and some particles start to break free at lower temperatures than others. Melting therefore happens gradually across a temperature range rather than at one fixed value. Adding impurities to a pure substance also tends to lower the start of the melting range — which is why salt is spread on icy roads (the salt-water mixture stays liquid below 0 °C).

Worked example — interpreting a melting point table

A student measures the melting points of three white solid samples and compares them with the literature value for pure benzoic acid, which has a sharp melting point of 122.4 °C.

SampleMelting behaviour observedConclusion
AMelts sharply at 122.4 °CPure benzoic acid — the sample melts at one fixed temperature that matches the literature value
BMelts gradually between 105 °C and 121 °CImpure / a mixture — melting occurs over a range, not at a single temperature, and the range starts below the literature value, indicating impurities are present
CMelts sharply at 98.6 °CPure, but not benzoic acid — the melting point is sharp (so the sample is pure) but it does not match benzoic acid, so it must be a different pure substance

The third row is the trap. A sharp melting point only tells you the sample is pure — it does not prove the substance is the one you think it is. To identify an unknown pure substance you have to compare its sharp melting point to a known literature value. If the values match, the substance is identified; if they do not match, the substance is pure but is a different compound.

When you write up an answer to a "State whether each of these samples is pure or impure and justify your answer" question, link the observation to the rule every time:

  • "Sample A is pure because it has a sharp melting point at one fixed temperature."
  • "Sample B is impure because it melts over a range of temperatures."

Mark schemes are built around that observation-justification pair.

Heating curves

The same idea can be drawn on a heating curve (temperature plotted against time as the sample is heated). For a pure substance the curve has a flat horizontal plateau at the melting point — the temperature stays constant while all the energy goes into breaking the lattice. For a mixture, the temperature slopes upwards gradually during melting, because different particles are melting at slightly different temperatures.

A flat plateau on a heating curve is therefore another fingerprint of a pure substance, and a sloping line during the change of state is a fingerprint of a mixture.

Why this matters in chemistry

The reason chemists are so strict about the word "pure" is that the properties of a substance only have fixed, reproducible values when the substance is pure. Pure water always boils at 100 °C at atmospheric pressure; pure sodium chloride always melts at 801 °C. If you can confirm by experiment that a sample boils at exactly 100 °C with a sharp transition, you have strong evidence that the substance is pure water.

That is why melting and boiling points are used as a quick check on the purity of a substance in the lab. A pharmaceutical chemist preparing aspirin will measure its melting point: if the sample melts sharply at 135 °C (the literature value), the aspirin is pure; if it melts over a range from 120 °C to 134 °C, it still contains impurities and needs to be recrystallised again. This is a real, practical use of the chemistry meaning of "pure", and a good example to use if an exam question asks why pure substances are important.

2.2.2.1 Simple Distillation F+H ▼

Simple Distillation

Spec 2.7a: Explain the experimental techniques for separation of mixtures by simple distillation. Spec 2.8: Describe an appropriate experimental technique to separate a mixture, knowing the properties of the components of the mixture. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate (1CH0) and Combined (1SC0), Paper 1.

Simple distillation is the technique used to separate a dissolved solute from a solvent — for example, pure water from sea water, or pure water from a solution of copper sulfate. It works whenever one component of the mixture is a liquid with a much lower boiling point than the other (the dissolved solid does not boil at all in this range). The principle is a two-stage physical change: the liquid is evaporated by heating, then the vapour is condensed back to a pure liquid in a different part of the apparatus. The dissolved solid is left behind in the original flask because it never enters the gas phase.

This is one of the most reliably examined separation techniques on Paper 1. Questions tend to be diagram-based and they almost always test whether you understand why each piece of apparatus is where it is — particularly the thermometer and the condenser — rather than just whether you can name the parts.

The apparatus — a labelled description

A simple distillation set-up is a single connected sequence of glassware. From left to right (or bottom to top):

  1. A heat source — a Bunsen burner on a tripod with a gauze, or an electric heating mantle — heats the mixture.
  2. A round-bottomed (distillation) flask holds the mixture being separated. The round bottom allows the flask to be heated evenly.
  3. A few anti-bumping granules are placed in the flask before heating. They cause smooth, controlled boiling — without them, the liquid would superheat and erupt in violent bubbles that could blow apart the apparatus.
  4. The flask is closed with a bung or stopper that holds a thermometer. The bulb of the thermometer must be positioned at the side-arm exit, not down in the boiling liquid — this point is critical and is explained below.
  5. A side-arm angles out from the top of the flask and carries the vapour into the condenser.
  6. The condenser is a long glass tube with an outer water jacket. Cold water flows through the jacket counter-current to the vapour: water enters at the lower end (nearest the collection beaker) and exits at the upper end (nearest the flask). The vapour is cooled as it passes through the inner tube, and condenses back into a liquid.
  7. The condensed liquid drips into a collection beaker at the open end of the condenser. This liquid is the distillate — the pure separated solvent.

Everything has to be open to the atmosphere at the collection end. If the apparatus were sealed, the pressure inside would build up as the liquid boiled, and the glassware could shatter. Students sometimes draw a stopper on the receiving flask in their exam diagrams — that loses a mark and is a recurring weakness on diagram-correction questions.

Why the thermometer must be at the side-arm exit

This is the single most heavily examined detail of simple distillation, and the place where the most candidates lose marks. The bulb of the thermometer must sit level with the opening of the side-arm, so that it measures the temperature of the vapour that is about to leave the flask and pass into the condenser — not the temperature of the boiling liquid below it.

The reason is that you need to know which substance is currently being collected at the condenser end. The thermometer reads the boiling point of whichever liquid is in vapour form at the side-arm. While pure water (boiling point 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure) is distilling over, the thermometer holds steady at about 100 °C. If you lower the bulb into the boiling liquid, you instead read the temperature of the bulk liquid (which can be hotter than its boiling point if it contains dissolved solid), and you lose the ability to identify what is being collected.

This is exactly the misconception flagged in the 1CH0/1F — June 2019 Examiner Report, in which examiners noted that very few candidates really understood the purpose of the thermometer. It is also the most common reason a setup diagram is described as "flawed" in a diagram-correction question — see the June 2024 example in the Exam Appearance section below.

Why the condenser uses counter-current water flow

The condenser has cold water flowing through its outer jacket so that the hot vapour passing through the inner tube is cooled below its boiling point and condenses back to a liquid. The water flows counter-current to the vapour — in at the bottom (cold end, near the receiving beaker) and out at the top (warm end, near the flask).

Counter-current flow has two advantages. First, the coldest water always meets the already-partially-cooled vapour at the receiving end, making sure the last of it condenses before it can escape. Second, the water jacket stays full at all times: if water flowed in at the top, gravity would let the jacket drain partly empty and the cooling would be much less efficient. Examiners often ask students to label "water in" and "water out" correctly on a condenser diagram — getting these the wrong way round is a common error.

Step-by-step procedure (using sea water as the example)

The most exam-friendly worked example is recovering pure water from sea water. The steps below give you a generic procedure that can be adapted to almost any "obtain the pure solvent from a solution" question.

  1. Set up the apparatus as described above — distillation flask with thermometer at the side-arm, side-arm connected to a condenser with cold water flowing counter-current, condenser outlet directed into a clean dry collection beaker.
  2. Half-fill the distillation flask with the sea water and add a few anti-bumping granules.
  3. Heat the flask gently with a Bunsen burner (or heating mantle). The sea water begins to boil and water vapour rises up the flask.
  4. The water vapour reaches the side-arm and passes into the condenser. The thermometer reads approximately 100 °C while pure water is distilling over.
  5. In the condenser, the water vapour is cooled by the cold water in the surrounding jacket. It condenses back into liquid water and drips into the collection beaker as distillate.
  6. The salt (and any other dissolved solid) is left behind in the original distillation flask, because its boiling point is far higher than the heating temperature and it cannot enter the gas phase.
  7. Stop heating once enough pure water has been collected, or before the flask runs dry — heating to dryness can crack the glass.

The end result: the collection beaker contains pure water; the distillation flask contains a concentrated (or eventually dry) salt residue.

Why simple distillation works — the underlying physics

Simple distillation exploits a very large difference in boiling point between the liquid solvent and the dissolved solute. Water boils at 100 °C; sodium chloride only melts at 801 °C and boils far higher again. At any temperature at which water is boiling, the salt cannot enter the gas phase, so the vapour rising in the flask is pure water. Once that pure water vapour is condensed back to a liquid in the condenser, you have separated the two components by a single physical change.

This is what "knowing the properties of the components" in spec 2.8 means: you choose simple distillation specifically because the boiling points of the two components are very different, and because you want to recover the liquid (the solvent) rather than the dissolved solid. If you wanted to recover the dissolved salt instead, simple distillation would still work (it would be the residue in the flask) — but the more usual technique for that goal is crystallisation, which is faster and uses less apparatus.

When to use simple distillation versus other techniques

This connects directly to spec 2.8. The technique you pick depends on what you have and what you want out:

Soluble solid in a liquid, want to recover the solid (e.g. salt from rock salt solution)CrystallisationThe solvent is evaporated off and the solid recrystallises in the dish
Soluble solid in a liquid, want to recover the liquid (e.g. pure water from sea water)Simple distillationThe solvent evaporates and is condensed back; the solid stays in the flask

2.2.2.2 Fractional Distillation F+H ▼

Fractional Distillation

Spec 2.7(b): Explain the experimental techniques for separation of mixtures by fractional distillation. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 1.

Fractional distillation is the method used to separate a mixture of two or more liquids that are mixed together (miscible) but have different boiling points. The classic GCSE examples are separating ethanol from water, separating the components of liquid air (nitrogen, oxygen and argon), and — most importantly for Paper 1 — separating the hydrocarbon fractions in crude oil. The technique is closely related to simple distillation, but it adds an extra piece of apparatus, the fractionating column, which makes it possible to separate liquids whose boiling points are close together.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Fractional distillationA separation technique used to separate a mixture of two or more miscible liquids with different boiling points by repeatedly evaporating and condensing them along a temperature gradient
Miscible liquidsLiquids that mix completely in any proportion to form a single solution (e.g. ethanol and water)

The apparatus and how it is set up

The set-up looks much like simple distillation, with one important addition. From bottom to top, the apparatus is:

  • A round-bottomed flask containing the liquid mixture, sitting on a heat source (Bunsen burner with gauze and tripod, or an electric heating mantle). Anti-bumping granules are added to the flask so the liquid boils smoothly rather than in violent bursts.
  • A fractionating column clamped vertically above the flask. The column is typically packed with glass beads (or has horizontal glass plates) to provide a large surface area for vapour to condense on and re-evaporate.
  • A thermometer at the top of the column, with its bulb level with the side-arm where the vapour exits.
  • A condenser clamped at a downward slope from the top of the column, with cold water flowing in at the lower end and out at the upper end (counter-current cooling).
  • A collection vessel (a conical flask or beaker) at the open end of the condenser to receive the distillate.

When you describe the apparatus in an exam answer, the two features that distinguish fractional distillation from simple distillation are the fractionating column and the glass beads (or plates) packed inside it. Mentioning these is what tells the examiner you understand which technique is being used.

Why the fractionating column works — the temperature gradient

The crucial idea is that the fractionating column is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Vapour rising from the boiling flask gradually loses heat as it travels up the column. The temperature falls steadily from the flask end to the thermometer end, producing a continuous temperature gradient.

This gradient does two important jobs at once:

  1. It lets the lower-boiling-point liquid travel further up the column before it cools enough to condense — because its boiling point is lower, it stays as a vapour at temperatures that are already too cold for the higher-boiling liquid to remain as a vapour.
  2. It forces the higher-boiling-point liquid to condense lower down the column and drip back into the flask, because the column cools below its boiling point quite quickly above the flask.

The glass beads (or plates) packed inside the column give the rising vapour a huge surface area to land on. Each time vapour touches a cool bead, it condenses; the heat of nearby vapour then re-evaporates it; it rises a little further; condenses again; and so on. This cycle of evaporating and condensing happens many times as the vapour climbs the column. Each cycle is effectively a mini-distillation, so by the time the vapour reaches the top of the column it is almost pure in the lower-boiling-point substance.

This is the single key idea that separates fractional from simple distillation. Simple distillation does one round of evaporation and condensation, which works only if the boiling points are very far apart (e.g. separating water from dissolved salt — one boils at 100 °C, the other does not boil at all). Fractional distillation does many rounds in series, which lets you separate two liquids whose boiling points may only be a few tens of degrees apart.

Step-by-step procedure

The standard procedure for separating a mixture of two miscible liquids (using ethanol/water as the worked example):

  1. Pour the mixture of ethanol and water into a round-bottomed flask and add a few anti-bumping granules.
  2. Set up the fractionating column packed with glass beads above the flask, with a thermometer at the top, a condenser sloping down from the side-arm, and a collection flask at the end of the condenser.
  3. Turn on the cold water flowing through the condenser jacket (water in at the bottom, out at the top — this counter-current flow keeps the inside of the condenser cold along its whole length).
  4. Heat the flask gently. Both liquids start to evaporate, but the one with the lower boiling point evaporates more rapidly.
  5. Watch the thermometer. When the temperature reaches roughly the boiling point of the lower-boiling component (78 °C for ethanol), the vapour reaching the top of the column is almost pure ethanol. The temperature reading stays close to 78 °C while ethanol is being collected.
  6. The ethanol vapour passes into the condenser, where it cools, condenses to a liquid and drips into the collection flask. This is the first fraction.
  7. When all the ethanol has distilled over, the thermometer reading rises sharply. The collection flask is changed.
  8. The temperature climbs to roughly 100 °C, at which point water begins to distil over. The water can be collected as a separate fraction.

The thermometer reading is therefore the key piece of data — it tells you which liquid is currently distilling over and signals when to change the collection flask.

Worked example — separating ethanol from water

Imagine a mixture that is 50% ethanol and 50% water. Ethanol boils at 78 °C and water boils at 100 °C. The 22 °C gap is small enough that simple distillation gives a poor separation: too much water evaporates alongside the ethanol. Fractional distillation handles it cleanly.

As the flask is heated, both liquids begin to evaporate. The vapour above the surface of the mixture is richer in ethanol than the original liquid because ethanol has the lower boiling point and evaporates more readily. This ethanol-rich vapour rises into the column. Near the bottom of the column it is still hot enough for both ethanol and water vapour to coexist. A little higher up, the column has cooled to about 90 °C — too cold for water vapour, so water condenses on the beads and drips back down. The ethanol vapour, still well above its own boiling point, keeps rising. By the time the vapour reaches the thermometer at the top, almost all the water has dropped back, and the thermometer reads close to 78 °C. The ethanol vapour passes into the condenser, cools below 78 °C, condenses to a liquid and is collected. The first fraction is nearly pure ethanol.

Once the ethanol has distilled over, only water remains in the flask. The thermometer reading rises and eventually settles near 100 °C as water boils and travels through the apparatus. Swap the collection flask, and the second fraction is water.

In an exam, a 3-mark "explain how fractional distillation separates ethanol from water" answer might read:

"The mixture is heated. Both liquids evaporate, but ethanol has the lower boiling point (78 °C) so it evaporates faster (1). The vapour rises up the fractionating column, where the temperature falls from the bottom to the top. Water condenses lower down and drips back into the flask, while ethanol vapour continues up the column and is collected at the top once the thermometer reaches 78 °C (1). The ethanol vapour passes into the condenser, condenses back to a liquid and is collected as the first fraction (1)."

Industrial application — crude oil

The most important real-world use of fractional distillation at GCSE is the separation of crude oil into useful fractions. Crude oil is a mixture of many hydrocarbons (compounds of carbon and hydrogen only), each with a different boiling point depending on the length of its carbon chain. Short-chain hydrocarbons have low boiling points; longer-chain hydrocarbons have higher boiling points because there are more intermolecular forces to overcome.

In an oil refinery, crude oil is heated until it is almost completely vaporised, then fed into the bottom of a tall fractionating column. The column is hot at the bottom and cool at the top — the same temperature gradient as the lab apparatus, just on an industrial scale. As the vapour rises, each hydrocarbon condenses at the level of the column where the temperature equals its boiling point. Short-chain gases (refinery gases, petrol) reach the cool top and are collected there; long-chain fractions (diesel, fuel oil, bitumen) condense lower down where it is still hot. The fractions are tapped off at the appropriate levels. The principle is identical to the lab-scale ethanol/water example — only the number of fractions and the scale change. (Crude oil and its fractions are covered in detail in the Section 7 organic chemistry SPs.)

2.2.2.3 Filtration F+H ▼

Filtration

Spec 2.7(c): Explain the experimental technique for separation of mixtures by filtration. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate + Combined.

Filtration is the simplest and most heavily examined of the separation techniques in Topic 2. It is the technique you use whenever a mixture contains an insoluble solid sitting in a liquid — whether the liquid is just a solvent on its own or a solution of a different soluble solid. The point of filtration is to physically trap the insoluble particles while letting the liquid (and anything dissolved in it) pass through. Get the apparatus, the labels and the names "filtrate" and "residue" right and this is one of the highest-yield marks in Paper 1.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
FiltrationA separation technique in which a mixture of an insoluble solid and a liquid is passed through filter paper; the solid is trapped and the liquid passes through
FiltrateThe liquid (with any dissolved solute) that passes through the filter paper into the receiving container
ResidueThe insoluble solid trapped on the filter paper
InsolubleA substance that does not dissolve in a given solvent (typically water at GCSE)
SolubleA substance that dissolves in a given solvent to form a solution

Why filtration works — the underlying chemistry

Filter paper is not a perfectly solid sheet; under a microscope it is a tangled mesh of cellulose fibres with tiny pores between them. Those pores are large enough to let molecules of water (and any dissolved ions or small molecules) slip through, but far too small to let solid particles such as grains of sand, lumps of calcium carbonate, or a precipitate of barium sulfate pass. So when you pour a mixture of insoluble solid and liquid into the filter paper, gravity pulls the liquid through the pores and into the conical flask underneath, while the solid particles collect on top of the paper as the residue.

A common student misconception is that filter paper somehow "knows" what to keep and what to let through. It does not — it is a size filter. Anything dissolved in the liquid (a solute) is in the form of individual ions or molecules, which are far smaller than the pores in the filter paper and so pass straight through with the solvent. That is why you cannot use filtration to separate a soluble solid from a solvent: sodium chloride dissolved in water will go straight through with the water as part of the filtrate. To recover sodium chloride from salty water you need to use evaporation or crystallisation instead — see 2.2.2.4 Crystallisation.

This also means filtration is the right choice when:

  • You have an insoluble solid suspended in a liquid (e.g. sand stirred into water).
  • You have a mixture of two solids in which one is soluble and the other is insoluble in water (e.g. sand and salt) — you add water to dissolve the salt, then filter to remove the sand.
  • You have made an insoluble product by a chemical reaction (e.g. a precipitation reaction such as silver chloride forming when silver nitrate is mixed with sodium chloride solution) and need to collect it.

And it is the wrong choice when:

  • Both substances in the mixture are soluble — no solid is present to trap.
  • You are trying to separate two miscible liquids — filtration cannot separate liquids from each other. Use fractional distillation instead.

The apparatus — what each piece does and why

The apparatus for filtration is small, but every part has a job. Below is a text description of the correctly assembled apparatus, with what every label needs to say and the common errors that cost marks.

  • Filter funnel. A glass or plastic funnel with a wide opening at the top and a narrow stem at the bottom. The funnel must be open at the top — students who draw a closed dome over the funnel lose marks because liquid cannot enter a closed funnel.
  • Filter paper. A circular sheet of porous paper, folded into a cone and placed inside the funnel so that it rests against the inner walls. The fold runs from the centre of the paper, so the cone has four overlapping layers on one side and two on the other. The paper must clearly be drawn and clearly be labelled as "filter paper" — not as "paper" and definitely not as "filter" (the funnel and the paper are two different things; labelling the funnel as "filter" is one of the most common errors).
  • Conical flask (or beaker). Placed underneath the funnel to catch the filtrate. The stem of the funnel should rest just inside the neck of the flask so that drops do not splash out.
  • The mixture. Poured slowly into the cone of filter paper — never above the rim of the paper, or unfiltered mixture spills over the top and contaminates the filtrate.

The procedure step by step:

  1. Fold the filter paper into a quarter — fold it in half, then in half again — and open it out into a cone so that one half of the cone has three layers and the other has one.
  2. Place the cone into the filter funnel with the point of the cone pointing down into the stem.
  3. Wet the paper with a little of the solvent (usually water). This makes the paper stick to the walls of the funnel and stops air gaps forming behind it.
  4. Stand the funnel in a conical flask (or in a beaker), with the stem of the funnel resting just inside the neck so that filtrate drops down cleanly.
  5. Pour the mixture slowly into the cone, keeping the level below the top of the filter paper at all times. Use a stirring rod against the lip of the beaker to guide the flow if necessary.
  6. Wait for the liquid to drip through — the liquid that collects in the flask is the filtrate; the solid left on the paper is the residue.
  7. If you want to recover a clean residue, wash it with a little fresh solvent while still on the paper to remove any traces of the filtrate, then leave it to dry.

Worked example — separating sand and salt

This is the classic exam scenario. You are given a mixture of sand (insoluble in water) and salt (sodium chloride, soluble in water) and asked how to separate them so that both substances are recovered pure.

  1. Add water and stir. The sodium chloride dissolves to form a salt solution; the sand is insoluble and stays as solid grains suspended in the liquid.
  2. Filter the mixture. Set up a funnel and filter paper over a conical flask. Pour the sand/salt solution into the cone. The salt solution passes through as the filtrate; the sand is trapped on the filter paper as the residue.
  3. Recover the sand. Wash the residue on the filter paper with a small volume of distilled water (to remove any salt solution still clinging to the sand), then leave it to dry in an oven or in air.
  4. Recover the salt. Pour the filtrate (the salt solution) into an evaporating basin and heat it gently. The water evaporates and salt crystals are left behind — this is crystallisation (see 2.2.2.4 Crystallisation).

Notice that filtration on its own only ever separates one stage of this problem — the sand from the salt solution. The salt itself is recovered by a follow-up technique (evaporation or crystallisation). Examiners often write questions in two halves: a "describe filtration" half worth 2 marks, then a "how would you obtain pure salt from the filtrate" half worth 2 more marks. The two techniques are partners.

2.2.2.4 Crystallisation F+H ▼

Crystallisation

Spec 2.7(d): Explain the experimental technique for separation of mixtures by crystallisation. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate (1CH0) and Combined (1SC0), Paper 1.

Crystallisation is the technique you use to recover a soluble solid from its solution in a form that is pure, dry and crystalline. It is the natural partner to filtration in nearly every salt-preparation experiment you meet on Paper 1: filtration removes unreacted insoluble material, and crystallisation then recovers the dissolved product from the filtrate. The key skill the exam tests is not just naming the steps — it is showing you understand why the solution is heated only until it is concentrated, and why the crystals are then allowed to form slowly as the solution cools.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
CrystallisationA separation technique in which a soluble solid is recovered from its solution by evaporating some (not all) of the solvent and then allowing the saturated solution to cool so that pure crystals form
Saturated solutionA solution that contains the maximum amount of a solute that will dissolve in the solvent at that temperature — any extra solute will not dissolve
SolubilityThe maximum mass of a solute that will dissolve in a given volume of solvent at a given temperature; for most solids, solubility decreases as temperature decreases
CrystalA solid in which the particles are arranged in a regular, repeating pattern — formed when ions or molecules come out of solution slowly
Evaporating basinA shallow open dish used to heat solutions so the solvent can escape as vapour over a wide surface area
FiltrateThe liquid that has passed through filter paper; in a salt preparation, the filtrate is the solution that goes on to be crystallised
Mother liquorThe remaining concentrated solution left behind after the first batch of crystals has been removed

Why crystallisation works

A solid solute (such as copper sulfate or ammonium sulfate) dissolves in water because its particles spread out and become surrounded by water molecules. As long as the solvent is present, those particles stay in solution. If you remove some of the solvent by gentle heating, the particles are forced closer together — the solution becomes more concentrated. Once the solution is saturated, any further loss of solvent (or any drop in temperature) means there is no longer enough water to keep all of the solute dissolved, and the excess comes out of solution as a solid. If this happens slowly enough, the particles arrange themselves into the regular pattern of a crystal lattice; this gives crystals their characteristic shape and purity. If it happens too quickly, you get a fine powder instead of well-formed crystals, and the powder often traps impurities.

Crystallisation works because most ionic solids are less soluble in cold water than in hot water. Heating the filtrate concentrates the solution (by driving off water) and a saturated hot solution then deposits crystals as it cools, because the cold water cannot hold as much dissolved solute.

When to use crystallisation

Use crystallisation when:

  • The mixture contains a soluble solid dissolved in a liquid — for example, copper sulfate in water, sodium chloride in water, or ammonium sulfate in water
  • You want to recover the solid in a pure, dry, crystalline form (rather than recover the solvent — for that, use simple distillation)
  • The solute does not decompose when the solution is heated gently in an evaporating basin
  • You have already removed any insoluble impurities by filtration, so the filtrate going into crystallisation is a clear solution

You should not use crystallisation if the dissolved substance breaks down on heating, or if both components of the mixture are liquids (use distillation), or if the solid is insoluble in the solvent (use filtration directly).

Heating to concentrate is not the same as evaporating to dryness

This is the single most heavily examined misconception on this spec point and the place candidates most often lose marks.

If you heat the solution gently until all the water has evaporated, you do not get pure crystals — you get a layer of dried-out powder fused to the bottom of the evaporating basin. The particles have had no time to arrange themselves into a regular lattice, water of crystallisation is driven off, and any soluble impurities that were dissolved in the last drop of water are deposited along with the product. The resulting solid is not pure and is not crystalline.

The correct procedure is to heat the solution only until about half the water has evaporated — until you can just see the first crystals starting to form at the edge of the basin, or until you can see crystals forming on a cold glass rod dipped into the solution. At that point the solution is saturated (or close to it). You then stop heating and allow the solution to cool slowly. As it cools, the solubility of the solute falls, and the dissolved solute is forced to leave the solution and grow onto existing crystal nuclei. Because this is slow, the crystals are well-formed, regular and pure. Any soluble impurities stay behind in the mother liquor.

If you are asked about crystallisation in an exam, the words "heat the solution to concentrate it" or "heat until crystals just begin to form" should appear somewhere in your answer. The words "evaporate all the water" or "boil the solution dry" should not.

The crystallisation procedure (step by step)

The standard procedure for recovering a soluble salt from its solution by crystallisation is:

  1. Pour the solution (typically the filtrate from a previous filtration) into an evaporating basin.
  2. Place the basin on a gauze and tripod over a Bunsen burner, or place it on top of a water bath / beaker of boiling water for gentler heating. A water bath is safer for solutes that decompose at higher temperatures.
  3. Heat the solution gently so that the water evaporates. Stir occasionally with a glass rod to prevent bumping.
  4. Continue heating until the solution is concentrated — you can test this by dipping a clean glass rod into the solution; when the first small crystals form on the rod as it cools, the solution is saturated. Alternatively, stop heating when you see the first crystals appearing at the edge of the basin.
  5. Stop heating. Do not heat the solution to dryness.
  6. Leave the saturated solution to cool slowly, either by leaving the basin on the bench, by placing it somewhere warm to slow the cooling further, or — for the slowest crystal growth — by covering it with a watch glass and leaving it for several hours or overnight. Slow cooling gives larger, purer crystals.
  7. Once the crystals have formed, separate them from the remaining mother liquor by filtration (or by carefully decanting the liquid).
  8. Dry the crystals by pressing them gently between two sheets of filter paper, or by leaving them in a warm oven at a low temperature. The aim is to drive off the surface water without driving off any water of crystallisation that is part of the crystal structure.

Apparatus you should be able to name in a labelled-diagram question:

  • Evaporating basin
  • Gauze and tripod (or water bath)
  • Bunsen burner
  • Heat-resistant mat
  • Glass rod (for stirring and for testing saturation)
  • Filter funnel, filter paper and conical flask (for separating crystals from the mother liquor at the end)

Worked example — recovering pure copper sulfate crystals

You are given a solution of copper sulfate that also contains a small amount of dissolved sulfuric acid as an impurity. Describe how you would obtain pure, dry copper sulfate crystals.

  1. Pour the copper sulfate solution into an evaporating basin.
  2. Place the basin on a gauze and tripod over a Bunsen burner (a water bath is fine here too).
  3. Heat the solution gently to evaporate some of the water and concentrate the solution.
  4. Stop heating when the first crystals just begin to form on a glass rod dipped into the solution (the solution is now saturated).
  5. Leave the saturated solution to cool slowly — the cooler solution cannot hold as much dissolved copper sulfate, so blue copper sulfate crystals come out of solution and grow.
  6. When crystallisation is complete, separate the crystals from the remaining liquid (the mother liquor, which contains the soluble acid impurity) by filtration.
  7. Dry the crystals by patting them gently between filter papers or by leaving them in a warm oven on a low setting.

The product is pure, dry, blue copper sulfate crystals. The soluble acid impurity stays behind in the mother liquor and is discarded.

2.2.2.5 Paper Chromatography F+H ▼

Paper Chromatography

Specification points 2.7e and 2.9 — Foundation and Higher tier — Paper 1 only (1CH0/1F, 1CH0/1H, 1SC0/1F, 1SC0/1H).

Paper chromatography is a separation technique used to split up a mixture of soluble substances — for example, the coloured dyes in an ink, the pigments in a food colouring, or the components of a mixture of amino acids. Each substance in the mixture travels across the paper at its own characteristic rate, so by the end of the experiment they have been pulled apart into separate spots that can be identified.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
Mobile phaseThe solvent that moves through the paper, carrying the dissolved substances with it. Typically water or an organic solvent such as ethanol
Stationary phaseThe chromatography paper itself, which stays still. The molecules of each substance interact with the paper to different extents
ChromatogramThe finished piece of paper, showing the separated spots after the solvent has run through
Solvent frontThe furthest point the solvent has travelled up the paper. Must be marked in pencil before the paper dries
Baseline (start line)The pencil line near the bottom of the paper on which the original sample spots are placed
Pure substanceA substance that produces only one spot on a chromatogram in every solvent used

Why substances separate — the principle behind the technique

Paper chromatography works because every soluble substance has its own balance between two competing tendencies:

  1. How strongly it is attracted to the mobile phase — i.e. how soluble it is in the solvent that is moving up the paper. The more soluble a substance is, the more it dissolves into the moving solvent and the further it travels.
  2. How strongly it is attracted to the stationary phase — i.e. how much it adsorbs onto (sticks to) the paper. The more strongly it sticks to the paper, the more it is held back and the shorter the distance it travels.

Different substances have different molecular structures, so they interact with the solvent and with the paper by different amounts. A substance that is highly soluble in the solvent and only weakly attracted to the paper will travel a long way up the chromatogram. A substance that is poorly soluble in the solvent and strongly attracted to the paper will barely move from the baseline. Everything else sits in between.

The result is that, by the time the solvent has nearly reached the top of the paper, every component of the original mixture has been carried a different distance — and the mixture has been physically separated into a row of distinct spots.

Why the substances must be soluble — paper chromatography relies on the substances dissolving in the mobile phase so that the solvent can carry them up the paper. If a substance is not soluble in the chosen solvent, it will simply stay where it was placed at the baseline. This is why the choice of solvent matters: water will not move oil-based pigments, and a non-polar solvent will not move highly polar dyes.

The chromatography paper as the stationary phase

The exam specification (2.9) is careful to state that the paper contains the stationary phase, not that the paper is the stationary phase. This is a subtle but exam-relevant point: the paper is made of cellulose fibres, and the stationary phase is actually a thin film of water trapped within and on the surface of those fibres. When the mobile phase (a different solvent, or sometimes also water) rises through the paper, the substances in the mixture partition between the moving solvent and this fixed water layer.

For Foundation tier, the simpler statement that "the paper is the stationary phase" is acceptable and reflects the wording most candidates will use. Higher tier candidates should still describe it as the spec does: the paper contains the stationary phase.

Step-by-step procedure

The standard Edexcel GCSE method for separating a coloured mixture (e.g. the dyes in an ink) by paper chromatography is as follows:

  1. Draw a baseline in pencil near the bottom of a piece of chromatography paper, roughly 1–2 cm from the bottom edge.
  2. Place a small spot of the mixture on the baseline using a capillary tube or fine glass rod. If running known reference substances alongside the unknown, place each on the same baseline, spaced apart.
  3. Allow the spots to dry, then reapply them once or twice to build up enough sample.
  4. Pour a shallow depth of solvent into the bottom of a chromatography tank (or a beaker with a watch-glass lid). The depth of solvent must be less than the height of the baseline.
  5. Hang the paper in the tank so the bottom edge dips into the solvent but the baseline itself is above the solvent surface. A pencil or rod across the top of the tank with the paper clipped or folded over it works well.
  6. Seal the tank with a lid to stop the solvent evaporating and to keep the atmosphere inside the tank saturated.
  7. Leave the experiment to run until the solvent front has travelled most of the way up the paper, but not all the way to the top.
  8. Remove the paper and immediately mark the solvent front in pencil before it dries and the front becomes invisible.
  9. Allow the chromatogram to dry, then observe the spots.

Why each step matters

Every detail in the method has a reason, and Edexcel routinely tests these reasons as 1- or 2-mark "explain" or "suggest" parts.

  • Why the baseline is drawn in pencil — pencil is made of graphite, which is insoluble in water and in organic solvents. If the baseline were drawn in ink, the ink itself would dissolve in the mobile phase and travel up the paper, contaminating the chromatogram and producing additional spots that did not come from the sample.
  • Why the spots are placed above the level of the solvent — if the baseline dipped into the solvent, the sample spots would simply dissolve directly into the solvent in the tank rather than being carried up the paper. The separation would not occur and the spots would be lost.
  • Why the solvent depth is shallow — same reason: to keep the baseline above the solvent.
  • Why the tank is sealed — to keep the air inside saturated with solvent vapour. If the air is unsaturated, solvent evaporates from the paper as well as travelling up it, which makes the solvent front uneven and the spots distorted.
  • Why the solvent must not reach the top — once the solvent front reaches the top edge, it stops moving, but slower-moving substances may not yet have finished separating. The result is a compressed, hard-to-interpret chromatogram.
  • Why the solvent front is marked immediately — once the paper dries, the wet boundary disappears and you cannot measure the distance travelled by the solvent. Without the solvent-front mark, R_f values cannot be calculated.

Worked example — interpreting a chromatogram

Suppose you run a sample of black ink (unknown mixture) alongside three reference dyes — A, B, C and D — in the same solvent on the same piece of paper. The finished chromatogram shows:

  • Reference dye A — one spot, travelled 4.2 cm
  • Reference dye B — one spot, travelled 6.5 cm
  • Reference dye C — one spot, travelled 2.1 cm
  • Reference dye D — one spot, travelled 5.8 cm
  • Unknown black ink — three spots, travelled 4.2 cm, 5.8 cm and 6.5 cm
  • Solvent front — 8.0 cm above the baseline

*Conclusions you can draw:*

  • The black ink is a mixture, because it produces more than one spot.
  • The three components of the black ink match dyes A, D and B — they have travelled the same distances as the reference dyes in the same solvent.
  • Dye C is not present in the black ink — none of the ink's spots lined up with it.

This kind of "which references are present in the unknown?" comparison is one of the most common forms in which chromatography is examined at GCSE. The technical R_f-value calculation that goes with it is covered in detail in 2.2.3.1 Interpreting Chromatograms — at this SP you only need to recognise that two substances with the same R_f value in the same solvent are the same substance, and that this is what allows the comparison to be made.

A pure substance will only ever produce one spot on a chromatogram, regardless of the solvent. If a sample produces more than one spot, it must be a mixture.

Edexcel typically presents it as a structured practical question — a stem describing an experiment, followed by sub-parts testing different aspects of the technique.

Specific worked sittings from the current NotebookLM data:

  • 1CH0/1F — 2019 June, Q4(c)(i): candidates were asked to list the experimental steps of a paper chromatography procedure in the correct order. 2 marks. This is the sitting where examiners praised candidates' procedural recall.
  • 1CH0/1F — 2023 June, Q1(a): a 1-mark multiple-choice question asking candidates to identify which of several inks contained only a single dye (i.e. which produced only one spot on the chromatogram). 1 mark. Tests the "pure substance = one spot" idea directly.
  • 1CH0/1H — 2020 November, Q5(b): a chromatography of inks question with multiple sub-parts. Part (b)(i) involved measuring spots and calculating R_f values; a later part asked for a way to make the experiment more accurate. This was the question flagged in the November 2020 examiner report.

The R_f calculation element on Higher tier belongs to 2.2.3.1 Interpreting Chromatograms — see that note for the full worked maths.

Key Takeaways
  1. Paper chromatography separates soluble substances in a mixture; substances that do not dissolve in the chosen solvent will not move from the baseline.
  2. The mobile phase is the solvent that moves up the paper; the stationary phase is held within the chromatography paper. Each substance partitions between the two and travels at its own characteristic rate.
  3. Substances that are more soluble in the solvent and less strongly attracted to the paper travel further up the chromatogram.
  4. The baseline must be drawn in pencil — pencil graphite does not dissolve in the solvent, so it stays in place and does not contaminate the chromatogram.
  5. The sample spots must be placed above the solvent level, otherwise the sample dissolves straight into the solvent in the tank instead of being carried up the paper.
  6. The chromatography tank must be sealed to keep the atmosphere inside saturated with solvent vapour and prevent the solvent evaporating from the paper.
  7. The solvent front must be marked immediately in pencil before the paper dries, so that the distance the solvent travelled can be measured.
  8. A pure substance produces only one spot on a chromatogram in any solvent; a sample that produces multiple spots must be a mixture.
  9. Two substances with the same R_f value in the same solvent are the same substance — this is how unknown components are identified against reference samples. R_f calculations themselves are covered in 2.2.3.1 Interpreting Chromatograms.
  10. Edexcel examiners report (1CH0/1F — 2019 June) that candidates can recall the sequence of steps well — make sure the marks on procedural questions are secured before the exam.
  11. The 1CH0/1H 2020 November report flagged that candidates struggled to suggest improvements to the chromatography experiment — practise phrasing modifications such as narrower capillary tubes, longer run times, or a more suitable solvent.
  12. Common command words on this SP at Foundation include Identify and Which (1 mark); at Higher, Explain, Calculate and Suggest dominate (1–2 marks each).
2.2.3.1 Interpreting Chromatograms F+H ▼

Interpreting Chromatograms

Spec 2.10: Interpret a paper chromatogram (a) to distinguish between pure and impure substances, (b) to identify substances by comparison with known substances, (c) to identify substances by calculation and use of Rf values. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate + Combined Science, Paper 1.

Paper chromatography produces a piece of evidence — the developed chromatogram — and most of the marks in the exam come from reading that piece of evidence correctly. The technique itself (drawing the baseline in pencil, dipping into solvent, leaving the spots to rise) is tested elsewhere; here, you are handed a chromatogram and asked three kinds of question: is it pure? what is in it? and what is the Rf value? Each one has a fixed mark scheme pattern, and this note walks through all three.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
BaselineThe pencil line drawn near the bottom of the chromatography paper on which the spots of the substance(s) being tested are placed before the paper is dipped into the solvent
Solvent frontThe furthest distance the solvent has travelled up the paper at the moment the chromatogram is removed from the beaker
Rf valueThe ratio of the distance moved by a substance (its spot) to the distance moved by the solvent front, measured from the baseline. A value between 0 and 1, with no units
Pure substanceA substance made of only one compound or one element; produces a single spot on a chromatogram
Impure substance / mixtureA substance containing more than one component; produces more than one spot on a chromatogram, one spot for each component

(a) Distinguishing pure and impure substances

A chromatogram is, at its simplest, a count of spots. If a sample is placed on the baseline and only one spot is visible after the chromatogram has been developed, the sample is a pure substance. If two or more spots are visible after development, the sample is a mixture (impure), and each spot represents a different component in that mixture.

The reason this works comes from how chromatography separates: different substances have different attractions for the stationary phase (the paper) and the mobile phase (the solvent). Substances with stronger attraction to the solvent and weaker attraction to the paper travel further up the paper; substances with stronger attraction to the paper and weaker attraction to the solvent travel less. A single substance has only one set of attractions, so it can only produce one spot. A mixture has as many sets of attractions as it has components, so each component travels its own distance and produces its own spot.

Two further details often catch students out:

  • A spot that does not move at all still counts as a spot — it means a component of the mixture has zero solubility in the chosen solvent, not that nothing is there.
  • A spot directly on the solvent front line also counts as a spot — it means that component is so soluble in the solvent that it has travelled at the same rate as the solvent itself.

When you are asked which of several samples on the same chromatogram contains the most dyes, the answer is simply whichever sample shows the most spots above its position on the baseline.

(b) Identifying substances by comparison with known standards

A chromatogram can be used to identify an unknown substance by running it side-by-side with a set of known standards (also called reference substances) on the same piece of paper, in the same solvent, at the same time. The known standards are placed as separate spots on the baseline at known positions, and the unknown sample is placed alongside them.

After the chromatogram is developed, the unknown substance is identified by matching:

  1. The vertical position of its spot (i.e. its Rf value) against the position of each known standard's spot, and
  2. The colour of the spot (for visibly coloured substances such as food dyes or inks).

A component of the unknown sample is identified as a particular known substance if both its Rf value and its colour match that known substance.

It is important that all the samples — knowns and unknowns — are run on the same chromatogram, in the same solvent, at the same time. Rf values depend on the solvent (and on temperature, paper type, and how far the solvent is allowed to run), so comparing a sample run on one piece of paper with a known run on a different piece of paper is not a valid identification. This is also why the chromatogram alone tells you what is in a mixture, but only when accompanied by the right known standards.

If an unknown component does not match the Rf or colour of any known on the chromatogram, then either (i) the known standard for that component was not included on the paper, or (ii) the solvent chosen was not suitable for dissolving that component, and a different solvent would need to be tried.

(c) Calculating Rf values

The Rf value (sometimes written R_f) is a number that locates a spot on a chromatogram. It is the fraction of the distance up the paper that a spot has travelled compared with the solvent front, measured from the baseline.

Mathematical Skills — the Rf formula

The defining equation, which must be reproduced from memory, is:

`` Rf = distance moved by substance / distance moved by solvent front ``

Three things to keep in mind every time you use this formula:

  • Measure both distances from the baseline, not from the bottom of the paper. The baseline is the pencil line on which the spots were originally placed.
  • Measure the distance to the centre of the spot for the "distance moved by substance". If the spot has spread out, the centre of the spot is the agreed measurement point.
  • Rf is always between 0 and 1. A value greater than 1 means a measurement error: the spot cannot have travelled further than the solvent itself.
  • Rf has no units. The "cm" in numerator and denominator cancel, leaving a pure ratio.
  • The Edexcel mark scheme almost always wants the answer given to two significant figures.

Worked example 1 — June 2019 Q4(c)(iii) style

A chromatogram is developed. The distance from the baseline to the centre of the dye spot is measured as 2.30 cm. The distance from the baseline to the solvent front is measured as 8.00 cm. Calculate the Rf value of the dye to two significant figures.

Step 1 — write the formula:

Rf = distance moved by dye / distance moved by solvent front

Step 2 — substitute the measured values:

Rf = 2.30 cm / 8.00 cm

Step 3 — divide:

Rf = 0.2875

Step 4 — round to 2 significant figures:

*Rf = 0.29*

Notice that the answer is dimensionless (the cm cancel), is between 0 and 1, and is quoted to 2 s.f., as the Edexcel examiner reports require.

Worked example 2 — typical Foundation calculation

A green ink is run on a chromatogram and separates into two spots: a yellow spot and a blue spot. The distance from the baseline to the yellow spot is 3.6 cm, and the distance from the baseline to the blue spot is 5.4 cm. The solvent front has travelled 9.0 cm from the baseline. Calculate the Rf value of each component to 2 s.f.

For yellow:

Rf = 3.6 / 9.0 = 0.40

For blue:

Rf = 5.4 / 9.0 = 0.60

Two spots, two Rf values, both between 0 and 1, both quoted to 2 s.f. Because the values differ, the green ink is a mixture (not pure), and contains at least these two components.

Worked example 3 — using Rf to identify

Suppose the same chromatogram from worked example 2 is run alongside three known dyes whose Rf values in this solvent are quoted as 0.20 (red), 0.40 (yellow), and 0.60 (blue). The yellow component has Rf = 0.40 (matches known yellow) and the blue component has Rf = 0.60 (matches known blue). The colours match the known standards as well. The green ink is therefore identified as a mixture of the yellow and blue dyes. The red dye is not present, because no component of the green ink has Rf = 0.20.

This is the full identification chain: measure, calculate, compare with knowns, match Rf and colour.

Key Takeaways
  1. A pure substance produces one spot on a developed chromatogram; a mixture produces two or more spots, one for each component.
  2. The number of spots from a sample equals the number of components in that sample — so the sample showing the most spots contains the most components (e.g. the most dyes).
  3. An unknown substance is identified by running it on the same chromatogram, in the same solvent, at the same time as one or more known standards, and matching both Rf value and colour.
  4. The baseline is the pencil line on which spots are placed before development; the solvent front is the furthest distance the solvent has travelled when the chromatogram is removed.
  5. The Rf value is defined as: Rf = distance moved by substance / distance moved by solvent front, both measured from the baseline to the centre of the spot.
  6. Rf values are always between 0 and 1, are dimensionless (no units), and are conventionally quoted to two significant figures in Edexcel mark schemes.
  7. The 2019 June Foundation paper specifically penalised candidates who could not recall the Rf formula or who failed to give the answer to 2 s.f. — that exact value, 0.29, was the expected response.
  8. If a spot does not move from the baseline in a particular solvent, the explanation needed for a 2-mark "explain" question is that a different solvent is required because the original solvent did not dissolve that substance.
  9. Two different substances can give the same Rf value in one solvent and different Rf values in another, which is why colour must also be matched for a reliable identification.
  10. Chromatogram questions appear in the front half of both Foundation and Higher Paper 1, with the command words Calculate, Explain and Identify dominating; typical tariffs are 1–2 marks per part.
  11. For "Calculate the Rf value" questions, the two marks split predictably into (i) correct substitution into the formula and (ii) the final value quoted to the correct sig figs — so showing working is essential even when the question seems simple.
  12. A spot at the solvent front line (Rf ≈ 1) or stuck on the baseline (Rf = 0) is still a spot — make sure you count it when deciding whether a sample is pure or a mixture.

Core Practical: Investigating Inks

Spec 2.11: Investigate the composition of inks using simple distillation and paper chromatography. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate (1CH0) and Combined (1SC0), Paper 1.

This is the Edexcel core practical that ties together two separation techniques in a single investigation. An ink is a mixture of one or more coloured dyes dissolved in a solvent (commonly water or ethanol). Because the ink contains two very different kinds of component — a volatile liquid solvent and one or more non-volatile dissolved dyes — no single separation technique can handle the whole job. So the practical splits into two parts: Part A uses simple distillation to recover the solvent, and Part B uses paper chromatography to separate the dyes that are left behind. Together, they let you identify every substance in the original ink.

The investigation question

The investigation answers a single overarching question: what is this ink made of? That breaks into two sub-questions that map directly onto the two halves of the practical:

  1. What is the solvent? Recover a sample of the pure solvent by simple distillation, then identify it from its boiling point (read off the thermometer at the side-arm).
  2. What dyes does the ink contain? Separate the dyes by paper chromatography, then identify each one by comparison with known reference dyes and/or by calculating its Rf value.

Part A — Simple Distillation: recovering the solvent

Aim: to recover a sample of the pure solvent from the ink and use its boiling point to identify what the solvent is.

*Apparatus:*

  • Round-bottomed (distillation) flask
  • A few anti-bumping granules
  • Bung holding a thermometer with the bulb at the side-arm exit
  • Side-arm linking the flask to a condenser
  • Liebig condenser with cold water flowing counter-current (water in at the lower end, out at the upper end)
  • Heat source — Bunsen burner on a tripod and gauze, or an electric heating mantle
  • Clean dry collection beaker at the condenser outlet
  • A small sample of the ink under investigation

*Method:*

  1. Half-fill the distillation flask with the ink and add a few anti-bumping granules.
  2. Set the apparatus up so the thermometer bulb sits level with the side-arm opening, the condenser is connected with cold water flowing counter-current (in at the bottom, out at the top), and the receiving end is open to the atmosphere.
  3. Heat the flask gently with a Bunsen burner (or heating mantle). The solvent begins to boil and rises through the flask as vapour.
  4. The vapour reaches the side-arm and passes into the condenser. The thermometer reading at this point tells you the boiling point of whichever liquid is distilling over — this is how you identify the solvent.
  5. In the condenser, cold water in the outer jacket cools the vapour below its boiling point. The vapour condenses back to liquid and drips into the collection beaker as distillate.
  6. The dyes are left behind in the distillation flask as a coloured residue — they have boiling points far above the heating temperature and cannot enter the vapour phase.
  7. Stop heating before the flask runs dry, and do not seal the receiving end at any point.

*Observations and what is collected versus left behind:*

  • The thermometer holds steady at a temperature that matches the boiling point of the solvent. For an ink made with water, the reading is around 100 °C. For an ink made with ethanol, the reading is around 78 °C.
  • Clear, colourless liquid drips into the collection beaker — this is the pure recovered solvent.
  • A coloured, sticky residue is left behind in the distillation flask — this is the dye fraction of the ink, ready to be analysed by Part B.

So Part A delivers two products in one step: a clean sample of the solvent identified by its boiling point, and the dye mixture concentrated up by the loss of the solvent.

Part B — Paper Chromatography: separating the dyes

Aim: to separate the coloured dyes present in the ink, and to identify them either by comparison with known reference dyes or by calculating Rf values.

*Apparatus:*

  • A strip of chromatography paper
  • A pencil and a ruler (for the baseline and solvent front)
  • A capillary tube or fine glass rod for spotting
  • A beaker or chromatography tank
  • A lid or watch-glass to cover the tank
  • A suitable solvent (often water, or sometimes ethanol if water alone will not dissolve the ink)
  • The ink under investigation, plus reference samples of known dyes for comparison

*Method:*

  1. Use a pencil and ruler to draw a horizontal baseline about 1–2 cm from the bottom edge of the chromatography paper.
  2. Use a capillary tube or fine glass rod to place a small concentrated spot of the ink on the baseline. Place reference spots of known dyes on the same baseline, well separated from each other and from the ink spot. Let the spots dry, then re-spot two or three times to build up a strong concentration.
  3. Pour a shallow depth of solvent into the beaker — less than 1 cm deep, so that the solvent level is below the baseline when the paper is lowered in.
  4. Hang the paper in the beaker so the bottom edge dips into the solvent but the baseline sits above the solvent level. Cover the beaker with a lid or watch-glass.
  5. Leave the apparatus undisturbed. The solvent (the mobile phase) rises up the paper by capillary action, carrying the dyes with it at different rates because each dye has a different attraction for the paper (the stationary phase) versus the solvent.
  6. When the solvent front has nearly reached the top of the paper, remove the paper. Immediately mark the position of the solvent front with a pencil line before it evaporates.
  7. Let the paper dry. The dried paper is the chromatogram.

*What you observe:*

  • The single ink spot at the baseline has separated into a vertical line of individual coloured spots — one for each dye in the ink.
  • An ink containing a single dye appears as a single coloured spot moved up the paper.
  • An ink containing a mixture appears as several coloured spots at different heights.
  • Reference spots can be matched against the ink spots: any reference dye whose spot ends up at the same height as one of the ink's spots is present in the ink.

Key practical points (and the reasons behind them)

These are the details examiners reach for first when they want to test understanding rather than recall. Every one has a "what?" plus a "why?".

Practical pointWhy it matters
The baseline is drawn in pencil, never in penPen ink is itself a mixture of dyes that would dissolve and run up the paper, contaminating the chromatogram. Pencil graphite is insoluble in the solvent, so the line stays put.
The ink spots sit above the solvent levelIf the spots were below the solvent line, the dyes would dissolve directly into the solvent in the beaker rather than travel up the paper — the experiment would simply wash the dye off the baseline.
A lid is placed on the tankTo stop the solvent evaporating during the run. If the solvent evaporates, the solvent front does not rise as far, Rf values become unreliable, and the spots may dry out partway up the paper.

Worked Rf example (carried in from 2.2.3.1)

Rf is the ratio of how far a dye has moved up the paper to how far the solvent has moved. Algebraically:

`` Rf = distance moved by dye / distance moved by solvent front ``

Both distances are measured in millimetres from the baseline (not from the bottom edge of the paper). For dye spots, measure to the centre of the spot, not the leading edge.

Worked example. A student investigates a black ink. After running the chromatogram, three coloured spots appear. The solvent front has moved 5.0 cm above the baseline. The centre of the topmost (red) dye spot is 1.45 cm above the baseline. Calculate the Rf value of the red dye.

`` Rf = 1.45 / 5.0 = 0.29 (to 2 s.f.) ``

The answer is given to two significant figures because Rf values are conventionally quoted to 2 s.f. — and because the 1CH0/1F June 2019 Examiner Report specifically flagged candidates losing marks for not doing so.

Rf is always a number between 0 and 1 (a dye cannot move further than the solvent that is carrying it). An Rf of 0 means the dye did not move at all; an Rf of 1 means the dye moved as far as the solvent front. Each dye, in a given solvent and on a given paper, has a fixed Rf value — so by matching your calculated Rf to a database value, you can name the dye even when no reference spots were run on the same paper.

Variables (independent, dependent, control)

A core practical examination question can ask you to identify the variables in the investigation. For the chromatography half of this practical, the variables are:

Variable typeWhat it is
Independent variableThe ink (or dye sample) placed on the baseline — i.e. what you are testing
Dependent variableThe number of coloured spots that appear and the distance each spot travels up the paper (from which you calculate Rf)
Control variablesSolvent used; type of chromatography paper; distance the solvent is allowed to rise (or equivalently, the run time); temperature; size of the dye spot at the baseline; that the tank is covered

For the distillation half, the same logic applies: the independent variable is the ink, the dependent variable is the temperature at which the distillate comes over (which identifies the solvent), and the controls are the heating rate, the position of the thermometer bulb, and the volume of ink used.

Safety considerations

  • Wear safety goggles throughout. Hot glass and hot vapour are at eye height during distillation.
  • If the solvent is ethanol (likely for a permanent ink), treat it as flammable — keep the Bunsen flame well away from the receiving end, or use an electric heating mantle instead of an open flame. Some inks contain organic solvents (propanol, propanone) which are also flammable.
  • Anti-bumping granules must be added before heating; without them, the boiling liquid can erupt suddenly and force hot vapour or liquid out of the apparatus.
  • Do not seal the receiving end of the distillation apparatus. A sealed system pressurises as the liquid boils and can shatter.
  • Take care with hot glassware, especially the condenser, which stays hot for several minutes after the flame is removed.
  • For the chromatography half, keep the tank covered while it is running so that solvent vapour does not build up around the experiment. Wash hands after handling dye-stained paper.

  1. The investigation answers what is this ink made of? by combining two techniques: simple distillation to recover and identify the solvent, and paper chromatography to separate and identify the dyes.
  2. In Part A (distillation), the thermometer reading at the side-arm gives the boiling point of the solvent — this is how you identify it (around 100 °C for water, around 78 °C for ethanol). The dyes are left behind in the flask because their boiling points are far higher than the heating temperature.
  3. The thermometer bulb sits at the side-arm exit, the condenser uses counter-current cold-water flow, and the receiving end is open to the atmosphere — sealing it can shatter the glassware.
  4. In Part B (chromatography), an ink is spotted on a pencil baseline above the solvent level; the solvent rises by capillary action through the paper, carrying the dyes up at different rates.
  5. The baseline is drawn in pencil (not pen) because pen ink would itself dissolve and run up the paper, contaminating the chromatogram. The tank is covered to stop the solvent evaporating.
  6. A single coloured spot on the chromatogram means a single dye; multiple spots mean a mixture of dyes. A dye is identified by matching its position with a reference dye run on the same paper or by calculating its Rf value.
  7. Rf = distance moved by dye / distance moved by solvent front, with both measured from the baseline. The answer is always between 0 and 1 and is quoted to 2 significant figures — examiners specifically flagged this rounding rule in the 1CH0/1F June 2019 Examiner Report.
  8. If a dye stays on the baseline, the current solvent has failed to dissolve it. A different solvent is needed because it can dissolve the dye, allowing it to be carried up the paper — this is the reasoning step that the 1CH0/1H November 2020 discriminator question was looking for.
  9. Independent variable: the ink. Dependent variable: the number and position of the coloured spots (or the Rf values). Control variables: solvent, paper type, run distance, temperature, spot size, that the tank is covered.
  10. Safety: wear goggles, treat ethanol and other organic solvents as flammable (use a heating mantle if needed), always include anti-bumping granules, never seal the receiving end, and never heat the distillation flask to dryness.

2.2.5.1 Purifying Water F+H ▼

Purifying Water

Spec 2.12: Describe how (a) waste and ground water can be made potable, including the need for sedimentation, filtration and chlorination; (b) sea water can be made potable by using distillation; (c) water used in analysis must not contain any dissolved salts. Foundation + Higher tier, Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0), Paper 1.

This spec point pulls together three different starting materials — fresh waste/ground water, sea water, and water destined for the chemistry lab — and asks you to describe how each is treated to give a final product fit for its intended use. The most important thing to nail before you go any further is that the target of treatment depends on the use: drinking water (potable) is judged on safety, while analysis water is judged on chemical purity. These are two different bars and they need two different processes.

Potable water is not the same as pure water — the highest-yield exam point

This is the single most-tested idea in the spec point and the misconception most likely to lose a 2-mark question. Read it twice.

In everyday language, "pure water" is often used to mean "clean water" or "water that's safe to drink" — the language used on bottled water advertising. In chemistry, those two things are completely different.

  • Pure water is a sample of only H₂O molecules. Nothing dissolved in it. No sodium ions, no calcium ions, no chloride, no nitrate. A measured 100.0 °C boiling point and a sharp 0.0 °C melting point are the experimental fingerprints of chemistry-pure water. Distilled water and deionised water are both chemistry-pure.
  • Potable water is water that is safe to drink. To be safe, the level of dissolved salts has to be low enough that the water doesn't make you ill, and the level of microbes has to be near zero. But potable water can — and almost always does — still contain dissolved salts (calcium, magnesium, sodium, chloride, nitrate, sulfate, fluoride, etc.). Tap water is potable. It is not chemistry-pure.

In short:

  • Chemistry pure → only one substance present (only H₂O).
  • Potable → safe to drink — still contains dissolved ions, but at low enough concentration to be safe.

A common exam framing gives you a table of dissolved ions found in a sample of tap water (e.g. Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻) and asks: "Use the data in the table to explain why this sample of potable water is not the same as pure water." You score by saying that the water contains dissolved ions / dissolved salts, so it is not a single substance; pure water is only H₂O (a single compound). You should refer to the data in the table rather than answering in the abstract.

(a) Treating waste and ground water to give potable water

For fresh water from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, aquifers (ground water) and treated waste water, the standard public-supply process at GCSE has three stages: sedimentation, filtration and chlorination, in that order. (Edexcel mark schemes sometimes accept "screening" before sedimentation, but the named three are the ones in spec 2.12.)

Step 1: Screening (preliminary) and sedimentation

The first thing that happens to raw water arriving at a treatment works is the removal of large objects. The water is passed through a metal screen or mesh that holds back twigs, leaves and other large floating items. After the screen, the water is held in a large settling tank for several hours. The flow is slowed almost to a halt and any heavier particles — sand, silt, mud and other insoluble grit — settle out under gravity at the bottom of the tank. The clearer water above is then drawn off for the next step. This is sedimentation.

Sedimentation only removes particles that are heavy enough to settle in still water within the time the tank gives them. Smaller particles that are still in suspension — fine clays, microorganisms, organic matter — are not removed at this stage.

Step 2: Filtration

The partially clarified water is then passed through a filter bed, usually layers of fine sand on top of gravel, which acts as the same kind of size filter you meet in 2.2.2.3 Filtration. Smaller insoluble particles that did not settle out in sedimentation are trapped in the spaces between the sand grains, while the water passes through. The water leaving the filter is much clearer.

A critical point — and one that examiners flag explicitly — is what filtration does not do:

  • Filtration removes insoluble solid particles only.
  • Filtration does not remove dissolved salts (the dissolved ions are far too small to be trapped by the filter).
  • Filtration does not kill or remove bacteria (bacteria are smaller than typical sand-bed pores and many will pass straight through; even if some are trapped, they are not killed by being trapped).

The November 2021 Higher tier examiner report is explicit on this point: "Some candidates lost marks here by not being specific enough about what would be removed by filtration (impurities) or suggesting that filtration removed bacteria." So in an exam answer, never say filtration removes "bacteria", "germs", "microbes" or just "impurities". Say it removes insoluble solids (or, more specifically still, smaller insoluble particles after sedimentation).

Step 3: Chlorination

The filtered water now looks clean but is not yet safe to drink — it still contains bacteria and other microbes that have made it through both sedimentation and filtration. The third stage, chlorination, fixes this by adding a small, carefully controlled amount of chlorine (Cl₂) to the water. Chlorine is a powerful disinfectant: it kills bacteria and other microbes in the water, making it safe to drink. A trace of chlorine remains in the supply network so that any microbes entering the pipes downstream are also killed.

Again, examiners are very strict about what counts as a correct description here. The November 2021 Higher tier report flags: "Marks were lost here by not being specific enough. Candidates using the idea of cleaning the water or removing impurities were not awarded a mark, although many responses did get the mark." So write "chlorine kills bacteria / microbes" — not "chlorine cleans the water" and not "chlorine removes impurities".

Order of the three steps — this matters

Edexcel like to test whether you know the order. The correct sequence is sedimentation → filtration → chlorination, and there is a clear reason behind it:

  1. Sedimentation first — the easy-to-remove large/heavy particles are taken out by gravity, so they don't clog the filter beds.
  2. Filtration second — the remaining finer insoluble particles are trapped, leaving water clear enough that chlorination is effective.
  3. Chlorination last — bacteria are killed at the end, after the water is clear; if you tried to chlorinate dirty water full of organic matter, the chlorine would react with the organic matter and there would be too little left to kill the microbes.

So sedimentation and filtration are mainly there to remove insoluble physical material; chlorination is the only step that addresses biological contamination.

Why this whole process does not produce pure water

The treated water leaving the works is potable — it is clean, clear and microbe-free. But it still contains the dissolved ions that were present in the original ground or waste water source: calcium, magnesium, sodium, chloride, sulfate, fluoride, nitrate, traces of trace metals. None of the three steps (sedimentation, filtration, chlorination) removes any dissolved solid — they only deal with insoluble particles and microbes. So the final tap water is safe to drink, but it is not chemistry-pure water.

(b) Treating sea water to give potable water

Sea water is a very different starting material. By mass, sea water is roughly 3.5% dissolved salts — mostly sodium chloride, plus magnesium, sulfate, potassium and many other ions. That salt concentration is far too high to drink safely. So the problem with sea water is not insoluble dirt or microbes; it is the dissolved salts.

Why sedimentation, filtration and chlorination cannot fix sea water on their own

If you ran sea water through the three-stage process above, you would get clear, microbe-free salt water — still completely undrinkable. The reason is exactly the same as in 2.2.2.3 Filtration: filter paper (and a sand bed) cannot trap dissolved ions, because dissolved Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are far smaller than the pores in the filter. Dissolved salts pass straight through with the filtrate. To remove them, you need a separation technique that exploits a physical property other than particle size — and the obvious one is the difference in boiling point between water (100 °C) and the dissolved salts (which only boil at temperatures of several thousand degrees, well above any practical heating temperature).

How distillation works for sea water

Sea water is converted into potable water by simple distillation (see 2.2.2.1 Simple Distillation for the full technique). The process in outline:

  1. The sea water is heated until it boils. The water turns into water vapour and rises out of the flask.
  2. The dissolved salts cannot enter the gas phase at this temperature, so they stay behind in the original flask as a concentrated solution (eventually as solid residue if you boil long enough).
  3. The water vapour passes into a condenser, where it is cooled by cold water in an outer jacket. The vapour condenses back into liquid water.
  4. The condensed water drips into a clean receiving vessel as the distillate. This water has had the dissolved salts removed and is now potable.

The clean, distilled water collected is also chemistry-pure (or nearly so), because the only thing that crossed from the boiling flask to the receiving vessel was water in the vapour phase. So distillation produces water that is both potable and pure, in one step — much better than the three-stage treatment of fresh water, which only achieves potability.

The trade-off: very high energy cost

If distillation is so effective, why is it not used for the public water supply? The answer is energy. Heating water to its boiling point and then evaporating it requires a large amount of energy per litre — water has a high specific heat capacity (the energy to raise its temperature) and a very large latent heat of vaporisation (the extra energy to actually convert it from liquid to vapour). For each litre of fresh water you produce, you need to supply enough energy to heat hundreds of grams of sea water from room temperature up to 100 °C and then evaporate it.

That makes desalination by distillation very expensive and energy-hungry, both financially and in terms of the carbon footprint of generating the energy. It is used in countries where the only practical source of fresh water is sea water (e.g. parts of the Middle East), but it would be uneconomic in countries with plenty of fresh ground and surface water — which is why the UK treats ground/waste water with the much cheaper three-stage process instead.

(c) Water used in chemical analysis

For chemical analysis, "potable" is not good enough. Water used in the lab — for making up standard solutions, for rinsing apparatus, for adding to a reaction — must contain no dissolved salts at all.

The reason is that any dissolved ions already present in the water would interfere with chemical tests carried out on the sample. Consider a worked example:

  • A common test for chloride ions in solution is to add dilute nitric acid and silver nitrate solution; a white precipitate of silver chloride (AgCl) confirms chloride.
  • Tap water already contains dissolved chloride ions (a few tens of milligrams per litre). If you used tap water to make up the test solution, the silver nitrate would react with the chloride already in the tap water and form a white precipitate — even if the sample you were analysing contained no chloride at all. The test would give a false positive.

The same problem applies in reverse: if you were testing for sulfate, or for calcium, or for almost any common ion, tap water contains enough of those ions to skew the test result. To avoid this, chemists use water that has had the dissolved ions removed.

Two types of water are used for analysis:

  • Distilled water — made by simple distillation (see 2.2.2.1 Simple Distillation). Boiling and re-condensing leaves the dissolved salts behind in the distillation flask, so the distillate contains essentially only H₂O.
  • Deionised water — made by passing tap water through an ion-exchange resin that swaps the dissolved Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺, Cl⁻ and other ions out of the water, replacing them with H⁺ and OH⁻ (which then combine to give more H₂O). Like distilled water, it contains essentially no dissolved ions.

Either of these is suitable for chemical analysis. Tap water is not.
