Module 3 The Periodic Table and Energy
P1
3.1.1 Periodicity
Structure of the periodic table: periods, groups, s/p/d blocks. Trends across Period 3: atomic radius, electronegativity, melting point. Oxide character (acidic/basic/amphoteric).
 P1
3.1.2 Ionisation Energy
First ionisation energy: definition and trend across a period and down a group. Successive ionisation energies — evidence for shell structure and group number. Explaining anomalies (Be vs B, N vs O).

3.1.3 Structure & Physical Properties
Giant metallic, giant ionic, giant covalent and simple molecular structures. Relating melting/boiling points, electrical conductivity and solubility to structure and bonding.
 P1
3.2.1 Group 2 Elements
Reactions of Group 2 metals (Mg–Ba) with water and oxygen. Trend in reactivity down the group. Products and equations for each reaction.
 P1
3.2.2 Group 2 Compounds
Solubility trends of Group 2 hydroxides and sulfates. Uses of Group 2 compounds: CaO (quicklime), Ca(OH)₂ (limewater, agriculture), CaCO₃, MgO (antacids). Flame colours.
 P1
3.3.1 The Halogens
Properties and trends down Group 7: electronegativity, boiling point, colour, reactivity. Reactions of halogens with halide ions (displacement). Disproportionation of chlorine in water and alkali.
 P1
3.3.2 Uses of Chlorine
Chlorine in water treatment: killing bacteria, forming HClO. Risks and benefits. Fluoride in toothpaste/water. Environmental concerns of halogenated compounds (CFCs, ozone depletion).
 P1
3.3.3 Qualitative Analysis
Tests for cations: flame tests (Li⁺ crimson, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ brick-red, Ba²⁺ pale green, Cu²⁺ blue-green). Hydroxide precipitate tests. Tests for anions: CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, halides (AgNO₃). Gas tests: H₂, O₂, CO₂, Cl₂, NH₃, HCl.
 P1
3.4.1 Enthalpy Changes
Exothermic and endothermic reactions. Standard enthalpy of combustion, formation, neutralisation and atomisation. Standard conditions (298 K, 100 kPa). Enthalpy profile diagrams.
 P1
3.4.2 Calorimetry
Measuring enthalpy changes using q = mcΔT. Calculating ΔH per mole. Limitations of calorimetry (heat loss, incomplete combustion). Experiment design and error analysis.
 P1
3.4.3 Bond Enthalpies
Average bond enthalpies. Calculating ΔH from bonds broken and formed. Limitations of using average bond enthalpies. Endothermic = more energy to break bonds; exothermic = more energy released forming bonds.
 P1
3.4.4 Hess's Law
Hess's Law: enthalpy change is independent of route. Constructing Hess cycles. Calculating unknown enthalpy changes from standard enthalpies of combustion or formation.
 P1
3.5.1 Simple Collision Theory
Collision theory: particles must collide with sufficient energy (≥ Ea) and correct orientation. Effect of concentration, pressure, temperature and surface area on rate. Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.
 P1
3.5.2 Catalysis
Homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts. How catalysts lower activation energy. Industrial examples (Fe in Haber process, V₂O₅ in Contact process). Autocatalysis.
 P1
3.5.3 The Boltzmann Distribution
Shape of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. Effect of temperature on the distribution. Why a small temperature rise causes a large increase in rate. Activation energy on the distribution curve.
 P1
3.6.1 Dynamic Equilibrium
Reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium. Conditions for equilibrium (closed system, constant macroscopic properties). Forward and reverse reaction rates equal at equilibrium.
 P1
3.6.2 Le Chatelier's Principle
Le Chatelier's principle: effect of changing concentration, pressure and temperature on equilibrium position. Industrial applications: Haber process, Contact process — compromise conditions and reasons.
 P1
3.6.3 The Equilibrium Constant, Kc
Writing Kc expressions from equations. Calculating Kc from equilibrium concentrations. Units of Kc. Effect of temperature on Kc. Kc unaffected by pressure, concentration or catalyst.

Combustion (complete and incomplete). Thermal cracking and catalytic cracking. Products and conditions. Uses of fractions from fractional distillation of crude oil.

E/Z isomerism arising from restricted rotation about C=C. Conditions for E/Z isomers (two different groups on each carbon). Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priority rules for E/Z assignment.

Addition of H₂ (hydrogenation), HX (hydrohalogenation), X₂ (halogenation), H₂O (hydration). Conditions for each. Markovnikov's rule for unsymmetrical addition of HX.

Combustion of alcohols. Oxidation: primary → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; secondary → ketone; tertiary — not oxidised. Dehydration to alkenes (elimination). Esterification.

5.1.2 Concentration–Time Graphs
Shape of [A] vs time for zero, first and second order. Half-life: constant for first order. Calculating k from half-life (k = ln2 / t½). Tangent method for rate at a point.
 P1
5.1.3 Rate–Concentration Graphs
Rate vs [A] graphs for zero (horizontal), first (straight line through origin), second order (curve). Determining order and k from gradient of rate–concentration graph.
 P1
5.1.4 Investigating Reaction Rates
Clock reactions, colorimetry, gas collection, conductance. Continuous monitoring vs initial rate methods. Sources of error and how to minimise them. Processing data to determine order.

5.1.5 Rate-determining Step
Multi-step reactions and rate-determining step (slowest step). Deducing the rate equation from the mechanism. Molecularity. Proposing mechanisms consistent with a given rate equation.

Effect of temperature on k. Calculating k at different temperatures. The relationship between k, Ea and T qualitatively and using the Arrhenius equation.

k = Ae^(−Ea/RT). Using ln k = ln A − Ea/RT. Graphical method: ln k vs 1/T — gradient = −Ea/R. Calculating Ea from two temperatures.
 P1
5.2.1 Equilibrium Terms
Mole fractions. Partial pressures. Total pressure = sum of partial pressures. Calculating mole fractions and partial pressures from equilibrium amounts.
 P1
5.2.2 Kc & Kp
Writing Kp expressions using partial pressures. Relationship between Kc and Kp: Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn. Units of Kp. Calculating Kp from equilibrium amounts and total pressure.
 P1
5.2.3 Equilibrium Constants & Changing Conditions
Effect of temperature on K (endothermic: K increases with T; exothermic: K decreases). Pressure and concentration changes do not affect K. Predicting direction of shift using Q vs K.
 P1
5.3.1 Acids & Bases
Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis definitions. Strong vs weak acids — degree of dissociation. Ka as equilibrium constant for weak acid dissociation. pKa = −log Ka.
 P1
5.3.2 Ka, pH & Kw
pH = −log[H⁺]. [H⁺] from pH. Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 298 K. pKw = 14. pH of water and neutral solutions at different temperatures.
 P1
5.3.3 pH Calculations
pH of strong acids and strong bases. pH of weak acids using Ka approximation: [H⁺] = √(Ka × c). pH changes during acid–base titrations. Shape of titration curves for all four combinations.
 P1
5.3.4 Buffers
Buffer action: mixture of weak acid and its conjugate base (or excess weak acid + salt). How a buffer resists pH change. Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. Calculating pH of a buffer.
 P1
5.3.5 Neutralisation
Enthalpy of neutralisation. Titration curves — identifying equivalence point and buffer region. Choosing indicators (pKin within steep region). Half-equivalence point: pH = pKa.
 P1
5.4.1 Lattice Enthalpy
Definition of lattice enthalpy (formation). Factors affecting lattice enthalpy: ionic charge and ionic radius. Comparison of theoretical and experimental values — polarisation and covalent character.
 P1
5.4.2 Building Born–Haber Cycles
Stages of a Born–Haber cycle: atomisation, ionisation, electron affinity, lattice enthalpy. Drawing the cycle for ionic compounds (NaCl, MgO etc).
 P1
5.4.3 Calculations Using Born–Haber Cycles
Using Hess's Law to calculate unknown enthalpy terms (commonly lattice enthalpy or electron affinity) from a Born–Haber cycle. Successive ionisation energies in cycles for 2+ cations.
 P1
5.4.4 Enthalpy of Solution & Hydration
Enthalpy of solution = lattice enthalpy (dissociation) + enthalpy of hydration. Why some ionic compounds dissolve endothermically. Hydration enthalpy depends on charge density of ion.
 P1
5.4.5 Effect of Ionic Charge & Radius on Enthalpy
Higher charge → more exothermic lattice enthalpy and hydration enthalpy. Smaller radius → greater charge density → more exothermic hydration. Explaining solubility trends.
 P1
5.4.6 Entropy
Entropy (S) as a measure of disorder. Units: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Predicting sign of ΔS for reactions (gases increase entropy; dissolving usually increases entropy). Standard entropy values.
 P1
5.4.7 The Feasibility of a Reaction
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Feasibility when ΔG < 0. Effect of temperature on feasibility for different sign combinations of ΔH and ΔS. Temperature at which reaction becomes feasible: T = ΔH / ΔS.
 P1
5.4.8 Gibbs Free Energy, ΔG
Calculating ΔG from ΔH, T and ΔS. Relationship between ΔG and equilibrium constant: ΔG° = −RT ln K. Interpreting the sign of ΔG in terms of spontaneity and equilibrium position.
 P1
5.5.1 Redox
Oxidation numbers in transition metal compounds. Balancing redox equations using half-equations (acidic and alkaline conditions). Disproportionation reactions.
 P1
5.5.2 Redox Titrations
Carrying out redox titrations (MnO₄⁻ and Cr₂O₇²⁻ as oxidising agents). Calculations to find concentration or percentage purity. Iodine–thiosulfate titrations.
 P1
5.5.3 Electrode Potentials
Standard electrode potential E°: definition, standard hydrogen electrode. Measuring E° using a standard cell. Electrochemical series. Predicting feasibility of redox reactions using E° values.
 P1
5.5.4 Electrode Potential Calculations
Calculating EMF of a cell: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. Predicting direction of electron flow. Limitations of E° predictions (kinetics, concentration, temperature effects).
 P1
5.5.5 Electrochemical Cells
Cells and batteries: non-rechargeable (Zn/C), rechargeable (Li-ion), fuel cells (H₂/O₂). Half-equations at anode and cathode. Advantages and disadvantages of fuel cells.
 P1
5.6.1 Transition Elements
Definition: d-block elements that form at least one ion with an incomplete d sub-shell. Electron configurations of Sc–Zn (including exceptions: Cr, Cu). General properties: variable oxidation states, coloured ions, catalytic activity.

5.6.2 Ligands & Complex Ions
Ligands as Lewis bases donating a lone pair to a central metal ion. Monodentate, bidentate and polydentate ligands. Coordination number (commonly 4 or 6). Naming complexes (IUPAC). Shape: octahedral, tetrahedral, square planar.

Ligand substitution reactions — competing ligand strengths. Haem and CO poisoning. EDTA as a chelating agent. Stability constants (Kstab) — definition and use in comparing complex stability.

Colours and oxidation states of vanadium (V, IV, III, II), chromium (Cr²⁺/³⁺/VI), manganese (II/IV/VII), iron (II/III), copper (I/II). Qualitative analysis using oxidation state changes and colour changes.
