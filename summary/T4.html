Extracting Metals & Equilibria — Summary Sheet

4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The reactivity series in order is: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, (hydrogen), copper, silver, gold — memorise this order, including the positions of carbon and hydrogen.
  2. The more reactive a metal, the more readily its atoms lose their outer electrons to form positive ions (cations); the reactivity series is effectively an oxidation series.
  3. Metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. K, Na and Ca react vigorously with cold water; Mg reacts very slowly with cold water but rapidly with steam; Al, Zn, Fe react only with steam; Cu, Ag, Au do not react.
  4. Metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen, but only for metals above hydrogen. K, Na and Ca are far too reactive to test with acid safely in a school lab; Mg, Al, Zn and Fe react at decreasing rates; Cu, Ag and Au do not react.
  5. In a displacement reaction, a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt, e.g. Zn (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → ZnSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s).
  6. A reactivity series can be deduced by mixing each metal with each salt solution, recording observations (colour change, metal coating, temperature change, bubbles), and ranking each metal by the number of salts it can react with.
  7. When describing this practical in an exam, name the metals, name the salt solutions, give the observations, and explain how the observations let you rank the metals — vague answers without observations are penalised heavily (1CH0/1H Nov 2021).
  8. The further up the series a metal is, the less resistant it is to oxidation — it tarnishes, corrodes or burns in air more readily.
  9. Aluminium appears unreactive because of a thin protective Al₂O₃ layer on its surface; underneath, the metal is genuinely reactive.
  10. Gold, silver and copper resist oxidation well, which is why they are used in jewellery, ornaments, coins, electrical wiring and water pipes.
  11. The correct exam reason for copper not reacting with dilute acid is that copper is less reactive than hydrogen, not that copper is a transition metal or has the wrong electron configuration (1CH0/1F June 2019).
  12. Very reactive metals (K, Na) are only ever found as compounds in the Earth's crust; unreactive metals (Au, often Ag and Cu) can be found as the uncombined element.
4.1.2.1 Displacement Reactions & Redox H only
Key Takeaways
  1. A displacement reaction occurs when a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in one of its compounds (linked to the reactivity series in 4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals).
  2. At Higher tier, displacement reactions must be explained as redox reactions in terms of electron transfer, not oxygen transfer.
  3. OILRIG — Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
  4. For Zn (s) + CuSO₄ (aq) → ZnSO₄ (aq) + Cu (s): the ionic equation is Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, with SO₄²⁻ as the spectator ion.
  5. Half-equations: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation) and Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (reduction).
  6. Zn is oxidised and is the reducing agent; Cu²⁺ is reduced and is the oxidising agent — the agent is named for what it does to the other species.
  7. Observations of Zn + CuSO₄: blue solution fades to colourless, grey zinc becomes coated with brown/pink copper, slight temperature rise — and no bubbles or gas.
  8. Spectator ions (e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻) do not gain or lose electrons — never include them in a half-equation.
  9. To explain a redox reaction fully, you must state both the oxidation step and the reduction step and link each one explicitly to electron loss or gain.
  10. June 2019 Q8(b) on 1CH0/1H was a 4-mark "explain in terms of electrons" question; June 2024 Q5(b)(i) on 1CH0/1H asked the same skill for 3 marks.
  11. Common examiner-flagged errors: writing about oxygen gain/loss at Higher tier, claiming the spectator ion transfers electrons, and describing "bubbles" or "fizzing" for a metal-metal salt displacement.
  12. The same electron-transfer definition of oxidation and reduction underpins electrolysis (3.2.4.1 Electrolysis & Redox) and ionic equation writing (1.6.9.2 Ionic Equations) — learn it once and reuse it across the spec.
4.1.3.1 Sources of Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The Earth's crust is the source of all metals; metals are a finite resource.
  2. An ore is a rock containing enough of a metal or metal compound to make extraction economically worthwhile — the economic test is part of the definition.
  3. Most metals are found in the crust as compounds inside ores, because they are reactive enough to have combined with non-metals (especially oxygen, sulfur and carbon) over geological time.
  4. Examples of ores: bauxite (Al₂O₃) for aluminium, haematite (Fe₂O₃) for iron, cinnabar (HgS) for mercury, zinc oxide / zinc blende for zinc.
  5. Unreactive metals — gold, silver, platinum, and sometimes copper — are found in the crust as the uncombined element (native metals).
  6. The reason is low reactivity: these metals do not react with oxygen, water, or other elements in the environment, even over billions of years.
  7. The lower a metal sits in the reactivity series, the more likely it is to be found native — link directly to 4.1.1.1 Reactivity of Metals.
  8. In a "Which metal is found uncombined?" MCQ, the correct option is always the least reactive metal on the list.
  9. Copper is a borderline case: most copper is extracted from sulfide or carbonate ores, but some native copper does occur.
  10. This SP covers where metals come from; the how of extracting them is in 4.1.3.2 Extracting Metals.
  11. The choice of extraction method depends on reactivity, so identifying the source of a metal is the first step before picking a reduction route.
  12. Recycling (see 4.1.5.1 Recycling Metals) and life cycle assessment (see 4.1.6.1 Life Cycle Assessment) both reduce demand on ores, extending the lifetime of finite metal reserves.
4.1.3.2 Extracting Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Oxidation is the gain of oxygen and reduction is the loss of oxygen (the GCSE definition used in spec point 4.5).
  2. The extraction of a metal from its ore is always a reduction — the metal compound loses oxygen (or gains electrons) to become the metal element.
  3. The method used to extract a metal depends on its position in the reactivity series and the cost of the extraction process.
  4. Metals less reactive than carbon (Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, Cu) are extracted by heating the metal oxide with carbon; carbon is the reducing agent, and it is oxidised to carbon dioxide.
  5. A standard equation to know: 2 ZnO + C → 2 Zn + CO₂ — zinc oxide is reduced, carbon is oxidised. Iron uses the same template: Fe₂O₃ + 3 C → 2 Fe + 3 CO₂. Knowledge of the blast furnace is not required.
  6. Metals more reactive than carbon (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) cannot be reduced by carbon and must be extracted by electrolysis of the molten compound.
  7. Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite; the overall change is 2 Al₂O₃ → 4 Al + 3 O₂. At the cathode Al³⁺ + 3 e⁻ → Al (reduction); at the anode 2 O²⁻ → O₂ + 4 e⁻ (oxidation).
  8. Cryolite is added to lower the melting point of aluminium oxide and so reduce the energy (and cost) of the process.
  9. Heating with carbon is cheaper than electrolysis because carbon (coke) is inexpensive and a furnace uses less energy than running a large electrolytic cell.
  10. To explain why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis rather than carbon, always link two ideas: aluminium is more reactive than carbon, and electrolysis (though expensive) is the only viable method.
  11. The percentage of metal in an ore is found using % metal = (mass of metal / mass of ore) × 100; for example, 42.5 kg of copper in 5000 kg of ore gives 0.85 % copper (Math skill 1c).
  12. If a question asks you to describe an experiment (e.g. to deduce a reactivity series), you must include practical activity — what you mix, what you observe, what the results show — not just abstract reasoning, as flagged in the 1CH0/1H November 2021 Examiner Report.
4.1.4.1 Bioleaching & Phytomining H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Bioleaching uses bacteria (e.g. Thiobacillus) on low-grade sulfide ores to produce a leachate solution containing dissolved metal compounds.
  2. Phytoextraction uses hyperaccumulator plants to absorb metals from low-grade soil; the plants are harvested and burnt, leaving an ash rich in metal compounds.
  3. In both methods, the final recovery step is displacement with a more reactive metal (often scrap iron) or electrolysis.
  4. Both methods are used when an ore is too low-grade to be worth mining and smelting traditionally.
  5. Advantages of biological methods: works on low-grade ores; lower energy use; lower CO₂ emissions; less landscape damage than open-pit mining; phytoextraction can clean up contaminated land (phytoremediation).
  6. Disadvantages of biological methods: very slow (months to years); lower yield than traditional mining; bioleaching produces toxic leachates; phytoextraction is climate-dependent.
  7. Phytoextraction does not have higher yield or lower cost than traditional mining — this is the most common wrong answer in exams (flagged in the 1CH0/1H 2019 examiner report).
  8. Vague answers like "better for the environment" or "causes less pollution" do not score — you must give specific advantages such as "less landscape damage" or "lower energy demand because no smelting is required".
  9. To calculate percentage by mass of metal in ash: (mass of metal ÷ total mass of ash) × 100. Example: 142.0 g of nickel in 1.00 kg of ash → 14.2% nickel.
  10. The metal in the ash is much more concentrated than in the original soil because the carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in the plant material burn off as CO₂ and water, leaving the non-volatile metal compounds behind.
  11. Phytoremediation is the use of plants to clean up contaminated land — the metal may or may not be recovered afterwards.
  12. This SP is Higher tier only and appears on Paper 1 (1CH0/1H) for both Separate Chemistry and Combined Science.
4.1.5.1 Recycling Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Recycling metals means collecting used metal items and melting them down to make new products, instead of disposing of them.
  2. Metal ores are a finite resource, so recycling preserves the supply of raw materials for future generations.
  3. Recycling reduces the amount of metal waste sent to landfill — metals do not biodegrade, so they would otherwise stay there indefinitely.
  4. Recycling uses much less energy than primary extraction; for aluminium, recycling uses roughly 5% of the energy needed to extract it from bauxite.
  5. Less energy use means fewer fossil fuels are burned at power stations, which reduces CO₂ emissions (climate change) and SO₂ emissions (acid rain).
  6. Less mining and quarrying is needed, which reduces habitat destruction, landscape damage, dust and noise pollution.
  7. Recycling has economic benefits: it is often cheaper than primary extraction, creates jobs in collection and reprocessing, and reduces a country's dependence on imported ore.
  8. The main drawbacks are the cost of collection and sorting, contamination lowering recycled-metal quality, and dependence on the public actually separating their waste.
  9. Examiners reject vague answers like "better for the environment", "less pollution", "quicker", "easier" and "less waste" — every advantage must be qualified with a specific consequence (e.g. "less waste metal goes to landfill").
  10. A 2-mark "Give two advantages…" question needs two distinctly different, fully-qualified points — pair an environmental point with an energy or resource point rather than restating the same idea twice.
  11. For an evaluate question, include at least one drawback (cost of sorting, contamination, or energy still used in melting) alongside the advantages.
  12. The same chemistry applies across metals, but aluminium is the headline worked example because the energy saving compared to electrolysis is so dramatic.
4.1.6.1 Life Cycle Assessment F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) considers the total environmental impact of a product across its whole life — from raw materials to disposal.
  2. There are four stages in every LCA, always in this order: obtaining the raw materials, manufacturing the product, using the product, and disposing of the product when it is no longer useful.
  3. Stage 1 (raw materials) considers energy used, habitat destruction, landscape damage, pollution, and resource depletion.
  4. Stage 2 (manufacturing) considers energy use (mostly fossil fuels), water use, emissions, and waste byproducts.
  5. Stage 3 (use) considers energy consumed during use (e.g. petrol burned by a car), emissions during use, maintenance, and how long the product lasts.
  6. Stage 4 (disposal) considers landfill, incineration, and recycling; recycling generally has the lowest impact and feeds raw materials back into the cycle.
  7. You must consider all four stages together — a product that is "green" in one stage may be far worse in another (e.g. an electric car has higher manufacturing impacts but a much lower use-stage impact than a petrol car).
  8. LCA is broader than carbon footprint: it covers water use, habitat loss, pollution, and waste as well as CO₂ emissions.
  9. A common exam mistake (1CH0/1F June 2019) is to give only two stages, and adjacent ones at that — make sure you list all four stages in the correct order.
  10. The typical command word for this SP at Foundation tier is "List", usually for 2 marks; the answer is the four stages in order with no need for further explanation.
  11. Worked comparisons (plastic vs paper vs cotton bags; petrol vs electric cars; single-use vs reusable bottles) show that the "best" product is rarely obvious from one stage alone — the full LCA is what decides.
  12. This SP is the descriptive half of LCAs; the data-evaluation half (weighing and comparing actual LCA numbers) sits in 4.1.6.2 Evaluating LCA.
4.1.6.2 Evaluating LCA F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The spec point is about interpreting LCA data, not describing the four stages — for the stages themselves see 4.1.6.1 Life Cycle Assessment.
  2. An LCA evaluation question almost always gives you a data table with products in one direction and stages (raw materials, manufacture, use, disposal) in the other.
  3. The unit is usually kg CO₂ equivalent, but can also be MJ of energy, litres of water, or kg of waste — read the column heading carefully.
  4. Always add up the totals for each product as the first step of an evaluation — a per-stage comparison alone is incomplete.
  5. Identify the dominant stage for each product — the single biggest number in that product's column tells you where most of its impact comes from.
  6. Look for trade-offs: one product is usually better at some stages and worse at others. Naming the trade-off explicitly with figures earns marks.
  7. Every evaluation point must be backed by a specific number from the table, not a generalisation like "X is more eco-friendly".
  8. The conclusion must answer the question asked — "lower carbon footprint", "less waste", and "less water use" are different criteria and can give different winners.
  9. Useful comparators include percentage difference ("48% lower"), multiples ("three times as much"), and differences in totals ("a saving of 0.11 kg CO₂e per bag").
  10. Sometimes there is no trade-off — one product (e.g. tap water vs bottled water) dominates at every stage. Say so and back it with figures.
  11. LCA data is not perfect: different studies use different assumptions, and the "use" stage depends on consumer behaviour. Mention this only if the question asks about reliability.
  12. Maths skills 2c and 4a — extracting and interpreting data from a table — are the underlying skill examined; check units carefully and process the data (totals, percentages) rather than just quoting raw numbers.
4.2.1.1 Reversible Reactions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A reversible reaction is one in which the products can react together to re-form the original reactants.
  2. The ⇌ symbol is used in place of a single arrow to indicate that the reaction is reversible.
  3. When asked to name the type of reaction shown by the ⇌ symbol, the accepted answers are reversible (best) or equilibrium. "Reversed" is not accepted.
  4. The forward reaction runs left to right (reactants to products); the reverse reaction runs right to left (products back to reactants).
  5. Hydrated copper(II) sulfate is the classic Edexcel example: CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue) ⇌ CuSO₄ (white) + 5H₂O. Heat drives the forward (dehydration) reaction; adding water drives the reverse (rehydration) reaction and the blue colour returns.
  6. Ammonium chloride is another standard demonstration: NH₄Cl (s) ⇌ NH₃ (g) + HCl (g). Heating decomposes the white solid into two colourless gases, which recombine on the cooler part of the tube to deposit white solid again.
  7. The forward and reverse reactions have opposite energy profiles — if the forward is exothermic by X kJ, the reverse is endothermic by X kJ, and vice versa.
  8. The direction of some reversible reactions can be changed by altering conditions — most commonly temperature (heating vs cooling) and adding or removing a reactant or product.
  9. If asked to devise an experiment to show a reaction is reversible (e.g. hydrated copper(II) sulfate), describe the practical steps — heat the blue solid until it turns white, then add water and observe the blue colour return. Do not answer in terms of equilibrium theory.
  10. The deeper idea of dynamic equilibrium — where forward and reverse rates are equal in a closed system — belongs to 4.2.1.2 Dynamic Equilibrium and is not required for this spec point.
4.2.1.2 Dynamic Equilibrium F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Dynamic equilibrium is the state reached in a reversible reaction (in a closed system) when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.
  2. At equilibrium, the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant — but they are usually not equal to each other.
  3. "Dynamic" means the forward and reverse reactions are still happening at the molecular level — the reaction has not stopped.
  4. Equilibrium can only be reached in a closed system where nothing can enter or leave; if a product escapes, the reverse reaction cannot occur and no equilibrium is set up.
  5. The two essential marking points on "explain what is meant by dynamic equilibrium" are: (i) equal forward and reverse rates, and (ii) closed system / constant concentrations of reactants and products.
  6. The swimming-pool analogy: water flowing in at the same rate as water flowing out keeps the level constant even though water is still moving — that is exactly what is happening in a chemical system at equilibrium.
  7. In the Haber process, N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, at equilibrium the concentrations of N₂, H₂ and NH₃ are constant, but ammonia molecules are still being formed and decomposed continuously at equal rates.
  8. Two very common errors to avoid: writing that "the reaction stops" (it does not — it is dynamic), and writing that "the concentrations are equal" (they are constant, not equal).
  9. The June 2019 1CH0/1H examiner report warned candidates not to apply equilibrium principles when a question asks them to describe a laboratory experiment showing reversibility — that needs practical detail, not equilibrium theory.
  10. Foundation-tier questions on this spec point are often "use words from the box" gap-fill style (e.g. November 2021 Q5(b), 2 marks); Higher-tier questions are more likely to ask for a short prose explanation.
  11. Whether the equilibrium mixture is mostly products, mostly reactants, or a balanced mixture is called the position of equilibrium, covered in 4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium.
  12. Reversible reactions are introduced in 4.2.1.1 Reversible Reactions; dynamic equilibrium is the natural follow-up because it describes what eventually happens inside any closed reversible system left long enough.
4.2.2.1 The Haber Process F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The Haber process makes ammonia (NH₃) from nitrogen (from the air) and hydrogen (from natural gas) — the reaction is reversible and reaches a dynamic equilibrium inside the reactor.
  2. The balanced equation is N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g) — learn the formula of ammonia exactly. Examiners flag NH₂ and NH₄ as the most common wrong answers (1CH0/1F June 2019).
  3. The conditions used are: temperature 450 °C, pressure 200 atm, iron catalyst — these specific values must be recalled.
  4. 450 °C is a compromise between yield (favoured by lower temperature, because the forward reaction is exothermic) and rate (favoured by higher temperature).
  5. 200 atm is a compromise between yield (favoured by higher pressure, because 4 mol of gas converts to 2 mol of gas) and the cost and safety problems of running at very high pressures.
  6. The iron catalyst speeds up the rate at which equilibrium is reached — it does not shift the position of equilibrium and is not used up.
  7. Inside the plant the gas mixture is heated, compressed, and passed over the iron catalyst; the product mixture is then cooled so that ammonia liquefies and can be removed, while unreacted N₂ and H₂ are recycled back into the reactor.
  8. The yield per pass is low (around 10–15%), but recycling means very little raw material is wasted overall.
  9. The two raw materials are cheap: N₂ from fractional distillation of liquid air and H₂ from reacting methane with steam.
  10. Over 80% of global ammonia output is used to make fertilisers; ammonia is also used to make nitric acid, explosives, and household cleaners.
  11. The reversible-reaction symbol ⇌ must appear in the equation — a single arrow (→) is wrong for the Haber process.
  12. The full Le Chatelier reasoning for why each condition shifts the position of equilibrium is examined at Higher tier and is covered in 4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium.
4.2.3.1 The Position of Equilibrium H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Le Chatelier's principle: if a closed system at dynamic equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose that change.
  2. Temperature increase favours the endothermic direction; temperature decrease favours the exothermic direction.
  3. Pressure increase favours the side with fewer moles of gas; pressure decrease favours the side with more moles of gas. Pressure has no effect if the two sides have the same number of gas moles.
  4. Increasing the concentration of a reactant shifts the position toward products; increasing the concentration of a product shifts the position toward reactants.
  5. A catalyst does not shift the position of equilibrium — it only increases the rate at which equilibrium is attained. The final equilibrium mixture is unchanged.
  6. For the Haber Process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, exothermic forward, 4 mol → 2 mol gas): high pressure and low temperature maximise yield. In industry, 200 atm and 450 °C are used as cost/rate compromises.
  7. For methane-steam reforming (CH₄ + H₂O ⇌ CO + 3H₂, endothermic forward, 2 mol → 4 mol gas): high temperature and low pressure maximise H₂ yield — the opposite of the Haber Process.
  8. To predict a pressure shift, count moles of gas on each side of the balanced equation, then apply the "fewer-moles-when-compressed" rule.
  9. At higher tier, full-mark explanations of pressure effects must address both the rate of attainment of equilibrium and the shift in position (decrease in number of gas molecules) — 1CH0/1H November 2020.
  10. Never apply Haber-Process reasoning to a non-Haber equilibrium — different equations have different exothermic directions and different gas-mole counts; you must work the prediction from the equation given (1CH0/1H November 2021).
  11. The command word is almost always "explain" and the mark tariff is typically 4–6 marks at the end of the paper — plan your answer around four or more distinct linked points.
  12. Always check the equation for state symbols before applying the pressure rule — only (g) species count for the mole comparison.
