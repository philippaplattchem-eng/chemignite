Calculations — Summary Sheet

1.6.1.1 Relative Formula Mass F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the average mass of an atom of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom — it has no units.
  2. Relative formula mass (Mr or RFM) is the sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms shown in a chemical formula — it also has no units.
  3. For every element in the formula, multiply Ar × subscript first, then add all the products together.
  4. The classic exam error is forgetting the subscript: Mr(CaCO₃) = 100, not 68. June 2019 examiner reports across 1CH0/1F and 1CH0/1H specifically flagged candidates "failing to multiply the relative atomic mass of oxygen by three".
  5. A bracket subscript multiplies every atom inside the brackets — Mg(OH)₂ contains 1 Mg, 2 O and 2 H, giving Mr = 58 (not 41 or 57).
  6. For complex bracketed formulae like Al₂(SO₄)₃, multiply each inside subscript by the outside subscript before summing: 2 Al, 3 S, 12 O → Mr = 342.
  7. The centred dot (·) in a hydrated salt formula means "together with" — count the water atoms in the same single sum (e.g. CuSO₄·5H₂O has 1 Cu, 1 S, 9 O, 10 H → Mr = 249.5).
  8. Decimal Ar values (Cl = 35.5, Cu = 63.5) give decimal RFMs — do not round to a whole number.
  9. Show each Ar × subscript multiplication explicitly in your working — this earns the working mark (typically 1 of 2 marks) even if the final addition slips.
  10. RFM has no units — never write "g", "kg", or "g/mol" next to your answer.
  11. Rewriting bracketed formulae without brackets (Mg(OH)₂ → MgO₂H₂; Al₂(SO₄)₃ → Al₂S₃O₁₂) before calculating eliminates most error opportunities.
  12. RFM is the foundation calculation for every later mass-based skill: moles, reacting masses, percentage yield, percentage composition, and empirical formulae all start by calculating Mr.
1.6.2.1 Empirical Formulae F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound; the molecular formula is the actual number of atoms in one molecule.
  2. The two formulae can be the same (H₂O, CO₂) or different (CH₂O vs C₆H₁₂O₆) — they are the same whenever the molecular subscripts cannot be simplified further.
  3. To find an empirical formula from masses: mass → divide by Ar → divide by smallest → multiply if needed → write the formula with subscripts.
  4. Always divide mass by Ar — never multiply. Multiplying mass × Ar is the most common Foundation error and gives a meaningless number.
  5. Mass goes on top of the fraction, Ar goes on the bottom. Inverting this is the most common Higher-tier slip and gives the wrong final formula.
  6. If the ratio you get is not whole numbers, multiply through — 1.5 means × 2, 1.33 means × 3, 1.25 means × 4.
  7. To find an empirical formula from a molecular formula: divide every subscript by their highest common factor (e.g. C₆H₁₂O₆ ÷ 6 → CH₂O).
  8. To find a molecular formula from an empirical formula and Mr: divide the compound's Mr by the empirical-unit Mr to get n, then multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by n.
  9. For CH₂ with Mr = 84: empirical Mr = 14, n = 84 ÷ 14 = 6, so molecular formula = C₆H₁₂.
  10. Always finish by writing a proper chemical formula with element symbols and subscripts — a ratio like "1 : 2 : 1" or just a number is not a final answer and will lose the final mark.
  11. Ionic compounds (like NaCl) do not have molecular formulae — their chemical formula is always an empirical formula because they exist as lattices, not molecules.
  12. Empirical formula questions are typically 3 marks on both tiers: 1 mark for the divide-by-Ar step, 1 mark for the simplified ratio, and 1 mark for the final formula in proper notation.
1.6.3.1 Determine an Empirical Formula F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The empirical formula of magnesium oxide is determined by heating a known mass of magnesium in a crucible until it reacts completely with oxygen, and weighing the magnesium oxide produced.
  2. The chemical reaction is 2Mg (s) + O₂ (g) → 2MgO (s), and the expected empirical formula is MgO (1 : 1 ratio).
  3. The key apparatus is a crucible with lid, pipeclay triangle, tripod, Bunsen burner, balance and tongs.
  4. The magnesium ribbon must be cleaned with emery paper first to remove the oxide layer, otherwise the mass recorded as "magnesium" already includes some oxygen.
  5. The lid stays on for most of the heating but is lifted briefly to allow oxygen in while keeping the white MgO smoke trapped inside.
  6. The crucible is heated, cooled, weighed and reheated until two consecutive weighings give the same mass — this is "heating to constant mass" and is the evidence that the reaction is complete.
  7. The mass of oxygen that reacted is calculated by difference: mass of MgO − mass of Mg = mass of O.
  8. To calculate the empirical formula: divide each mass by the relative atomic mass to get moles, then divide both values by the smallest to get the simplest whole-number ratio.
  9. Major sources of error are escape of MgO smoke (lid open too long), incomplete reaction (not heated to constant mass), and uncleaned magnesium (oxide already present).
  10. Safety: do not look directly at the burning magnesium — the bright light can damage your eyes. Use tongs and a heat-resistant mat for the hot crucible.
  11. The maths skills used are decimals, ratios and significant figures; the calculation method is the same as for any empirical-formula problem.
  12. In the exam this spec point most often appears as a 2–3 mark "describe" or "explain why" question on the procedure, sometimes followed by a numerical sub-part using sample mass data.
1.6.4.1 Conservation of Mass F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The law of conservation of mass: the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products in any chemical reaction.
  2. The law works because atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed — the same atoms appear on both sides of a balanced equation.
  3. In a closed system the balance reading does not change during the reaction because no substances can enter or leave the flask.
  4. A precipitation reaction in a sealed flask (e.g. Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2 KI → PbI₂ + 2 KNO₃) shows no change in total mass even though a solid forms.
  5. In an open system the mass of the contents of the flask can change — but the law of conservation of mass is still obeyed when the gas entering or leaving is counted.
  6. Mass decreases when a gas is produced and escapes the open container (e.g. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂).
  7. Mass increases when a gas from the air is used up by the reaction (e.g. 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO).
  8. To find the mass of gas released: mass of gas = starting mass of solid − final mass of solid.
  9. Saying "the mass decreases" is description and earns only 1 mark — you must explain why by referring to the gas leaving the open container.
  10. The word evaporation is wrong for these reactions — the gas is produced by a chemical reaction, not evaporated from a liquid. Use "released", "given off" or "escapes".
  11. A correct 2-mark "explain" answer always names the gas (CO₂ or O₂) and says it leaves the flask or is taken from the air.
  12. A correctly balanced symbol equation is the algebraic statement of conservation of mass — the same number of atoms of each element appears on both sides.
1.6.5.1 Reacting Masses F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The balanced equation is the source of the ratio — coefficients give the ratio of moles (or "formula units") in which substances react and are produced.
  2. The ratio method scales the equation's RFM-weighted mass ratio to the actual mass given in the question.
  3. The moles method converts mass to moles, applies the equation ratio, and converts back to mass — and it works for any ratio.
  4. At Higher tier, examiners explicitly recommend the moles method: full-mark scripts in June 2019 used it and laid out working clearly.
  5. RFM of the product on its own is not the answer — this is the most common Foundation mistake. The full method needs both RFMs, the ratio, and the scaling step.
  6. Always check the equation is balanced before you start — an unbalanced equation gives a wrong ratio and a wrong answer.
  7. Use the coefficients, not just the RFMs — 2 Mg + O₂ → 2 MgO has a 2 : 2 (= 1 : 1) Mg : MgO ratio, not whatever the bare RFMs suggest.
  8. Set out your working clearly — examiners explicitly criticised poor layout in the 1CH0/2F — June 2019 Examiner Report; method marks cannot be awarded if working is unreadable.
  9. State units (g, kg, tonnes) on every mass in your answer — getting the units wrong on the final mass loses an easy mark.
  10. Give the final answer to an appropriate number of significant figures — match the data in the question, usually 2 or 3 sig fig.
  11. The method is the same whether the unknown is a product or a reactant — you can scale either direction along the equation.
  12. Reacting-mass calculations are worth 3–4 marks on Paper 2 with the command word "Calculate" — practise the full layout so you can pick up every available mark.
1.6.5.2 Percentage Composition F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Percentage by mass of an element in a compound = (total mass of element in compound ÷ RFM of compound) × 100.
  2. The total mass of an element is Ar of the element × the subscript — not just Ar alone. For CaCO₃, the mass of oxygen is 3 × 16 = 48, not 16.
  3. For formulae with brackets like (NH₄)₂SO₄, the number outside the bracket multiplies every atom inside it — count carefully before adding up the RFM.
  4. For hydrated salts like CuSO₄·5H₂O, the "·5H₂O" adds five water molecules (mass 90) to the formula; the percentage of water uses 90 ÷ RFM_total × 100.
  5. % of carbon in CH₄ is 75%; % of oxygen in CaCO₃ is 48%; mass of carbon in 100 g of butane is 82.8 g to 3 s.f.; % of nitrogen in (NH₄)₂SO₄ is 21.2% to 3 s.f.; % of water in CuSO₄·5H₂O is 36.1% to 3 s.f.
  6. Always read the question for the significant-figures instruction before you start the calculation — both 1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H — June 2019 Examiner Reports flagged sig figs as the most common reason for losing the final mark.
  7. Do not round midway through your working — keep the long decimal and only round at the very end.
  8. If the sample size happens to be 100 g, the percentage by mass is also the mass in grams — a convenient shortcut.
  9. The maths skills explicitly required are 1a (decimals), 1c (ratios, fractions and percentages) and 2a (significant figures).
  10. Typical exam tariff is 2–3 marks; the command word is almost always "Calculate" with a sig-fig precision instruction attached.
  11. Common real-world contexts include fertilisers (comparing nitrogen content), iron ores (comparing iron content) and hydrated salts (water of crystallisation driven off by heating).
  12. The method is procedural and learnable — Foundation candidates who feel lost should drill the four steps: (i) write the RFM, (ii) write the mass of the named element, (iii) divide and multiply by 100, (iv) round to the requested significant figures.
1.6.6.1 Calculating Concentration F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Concentration is the mass of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, measured in g dm⁻³ at this spec point.
  2. The core formula is c = m / V, where m is in grams and V is in dm³.
  3. The two rearrangements are m = c × V (to find mass) and V = m / c (to find volume).
  4. 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 1 L, and 1 cm³ = 1 mL.
  5. To convert cm³ to dm³, divide by 1000; to convert dm³ to cm³, multiply by 1000.
  6. The single most common exam error is failing to convert the volume from cm³ to dm³ before substituting — this loses both the substitution and final-answer marks (1CH0/2F — June 2019 Examiner Report).
  7. Always show the unit conversion as a separate line of working — it bags an isolated mark even if the rest of the calculation slips.
  8. The "volume of solution" in the formula is the total final volume of the mixture, not just the volume of solvent added.
  9. Always include the unit (g dm⁻³, g, dm³ or cm³) in your final answer — a number without a unit can lose the final-answer mark.
  10. A typical concentration question is worth 2–3 marks: 1 for the unit conversion, 1 for the substitution, 1 for the final answer with units.
  11. Concentration in g dm⁻³ is the mass version only — the moles version (mol dm⁻³) is a separate Higher-tier Separate-Chemistry spec point in Section 5.
  12. Sense-check the magnitude: GCSE g dm⁻³ concentrations are normally between 1 and 100 — answers like 0.016 or 16,000 almost always signal a missed conversion.
1.6.7.1 The Mole H only
Key Takeaways
  1. One mole of any substance contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles — this is the Avogadro constant.
  2. The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is numerically equal to its Ar (for atoms) or Mr (for compounds).
  3. Use moles = mass ÷ Mr to convert grams into moles, and the rearrangement mass = moles × Mr to go the other way.
  4. Use particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³ to convert moles into number of particles, and divide by the Avogadro constant to go the other way.
  5. Mass → moles → particles (or the reverse) is the two-step chain that links what you can weigh to what is actually reacting.
  6. Always identify what kind of particle the question is asking about — atoms, molecules, formula units or ions — before applying the Avogadro constant.
  7. For a compound like CuI, the number of atoms in one mole = 6.02 × 10²³ × the number of atoms in one formula unit; just quoting the Avogadro constant alone is not enough (1CH0/1H — June 2019 Examiner Report).
  8. Show full working line by line — examiners specifically rewarded clear setting-out on the 1CH0/1H June 2019 paper, while common errors included arithmetic slips and missed unit conversions.
  9. Convert units before substituting — mg → g (divide by 1000) and cm³ → dm³ (divide by 1000) are the two conversions that catch most candidates out.
  10. Write any answer that involves the Avogadro constant in standard form — particle counts almost always lie between 10¹⁸ and 10²⁵.
  11. Round to an appropriate number of significant figures — usually 2 or 3 sig fig, matching the precision of the data given.
  12. This is a Higher tier only spec point — it appears on 1CH0/1H and 1CH0/2H as a 2–4 mark "Calculate" question, typically in the middle or later parts of the paper.
1.6.8.1 Limiting Reagents H only
Key Takeaways
  1. The limiting reagent is the reactant that runs out first; it controls the maximum mass of product that can be formed.
  2. A reactant in excess is left over when the limiting reagent is fully used up — it cannot make any more product on its own.
  3. Reactants combine in the fixed ratio given by the coefficients in the balanced equation — never by their masses.
  4. To compare two reactants fairly, first convert each mass to moles using moles = mass ÷ Mr.
  5. Then divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient in the balanced equation; the smaller number is the limiting reagent.
  6. The mass of product is calculated using the limiting reagent's moles, the equation ratio, and mass = moles × Mr.
  7. Adding more of the excess reactant does not increase the mass of product — the limiting reagent is still the ceiling.
  8. Reactants are deliberately put in excess in practice to make sure the valuable or slower-reacting reactant is fully used up, or to speed the reaction up.
  9. Explain-style answers must mention the balanced equation ratio, the reactant that runs out first, and the fact that excess reactant does nothing extra.
  10. Always show your working clearly: equation, Mr values, moles of each reactant, divide-by-coefficient comparison, limiting reagent named, then product mass with units.
  11. Limiting reagents is Higher tier only (Y) and can appear on Paper 1 or Paper 2 because it sits in Section 1.
  12. Typical tariff is 2–4 marks with command word Calculate (numerical version) or Explain (prose version).
1.6.9.1 Writing & Balancing Equations F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A word equation uses names, separated by + signs and a single arrow →; reactants go on the left of the arrow and products on the right.
  2. When the question gives you the names of reactants and products in the stem, use those names in your word equation — the 1CH0/1F June 2019 Examiner Report makes this explicit.
  3. A symbol equation uses chemical formulae instead of names, and must include state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous.
  4. Leave a small space between the formula and the state symbol — HCl (aq), not HCl(aq).
  5. Balancing means adjusting the big coefficients in front of each formula so that the atom count of every element is the same on both sides of the arrow.
  6. You only ever change the coefficients (big numbers in front), never the subscripts (small numbers inside) — changing a subscript changes the substance, not the balance.
  7. The seven diatomic elements in uncombined form are H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂ — writing them as a single atom is one of the most common balancing errors.
  8. After every coefficient change, re-count every element — a single change can throw multiple atom counts out of balance.
  9. Balanced equations should use the smallest whole-number ratio of coefficients; multiply through if you end up with a fractional coefficient.
  10. The 1CH0/1H June 2019 Examiner Report noted that "the large majority of equations with correct formulae were correctly balanced" — get the formulae right first and balancing follows.
  11. Word equations are usually worth 1–2 marks (command word Write or Complete); balanced symbol equations are usually worth 2–3 marks, with 1 mark often strictly for the balancing.
  12. A correctly balanced equation is the algebraic statement of conservation of mass and is the source of the ratio used in every reacting-mass calculation — see 1.6.4.1 Conservation of Mass and 1.6.5.1 Reacting Masses.
1.6.9.2 Ionic Equations H only
Key Takeaways
  1. An ionic equation shows only the particles that change during a reaction; spectator ions are cancelled out.
  2. Only aqueous ionic compounds — those with state symbol (aq) — are split into their separate ions in an ionic equation.
  3. Solids (s), liquids (l), gases (g), elements, and covalent molecules such as H₂O are not split, regardless of where they appear.
  4. The standard process is: full balanced equation → split (aq) ionic compounds → cancel ions identical on both sides → check atoms and charges balance.
  5. Charge must balance just like atoms: the total charge on the left must equal the total charge on the right.
  6. Silver nitrate + sodium chloride: ionic equation is Ag⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq) → AgCl (s).
  7. Strong acid + strong alkali neutralisation: the ionic equation is always H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l), no matter which acid and alkali.
  8. Displacement of copper by zinc: Zn (s) + Cu²⁺ (aq) → Zn²⁺ (aq) + Cu (s); this reveals that zinc is oxidised and copper(II) is reduced.
  9. Simply re-copying the full balanced equation in answer to an "ionic equation" question scores zero marks (1CH0/2H June 2019 Examiner Report).
  10. Common errors flagged by the November 2021 Examiner Report include strange chemical formulae such as "AgN", incorrect ion charges (e.g. Cl₂⁻ instead of Cl⁻), and pairing two negative ions together — none of which can ever be correct.
  11. Always derive each ion's correct charge from its position in the periodic table or the polyatomic-ion list before writing the equation.
  12. Ionic equations are Higher tier only, typically worth 2–3 marks, and can appear on either Paper 1 or Paper 2.
1.6.9.3 Deducing Stoichiometry H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Stoichiometry is the whole-number ratio in which substances react and are produced — the big numbers in front of each formula in a balanced equation.
  2. Deducing stoichiometry is the reverse of a reacting-mass calculation: you start with experimental masses and work out the coefficients.
  3. The mole ratio of substances taking part in a reaction is identical to the ratio of coefficients in the balanced equation — because a mole is just a fixed count of particles.
  4. Procedure: masses → Mr of each → moles of each (mass ÷ Mr) → divide every moles value by the smallest → if any value ends in .5 or .33, multiply the whole ratio through to clear fractions.
  5. If a mass is missing from the data — typically the mass of a gas that has been taken from or released to the air — use conservation of mass to find it before you start the moles step.
  6. Keep at least 3 significant figures during the moles step; do not round too early or the final ratio will go wrong.
  7. A 1 : 1 : 1 result is a perfectly valid stoichiometry — small whole-number ratios include 1 : 1 : 1, 2 : 1 : 2, 1 : 2 : 2, 4 : 3 : 2 and so on.
  8. Always atom-check at the end: substitute your deduced coefficients into the equation and verify the number of atoms of each element matches on both sides.
  9. Show every line of working with units (g, mol, etc.) — examiners need to follow your method to award the method marks even if the final number is slightly wrong.
  10. The procedure is the same as for an empirical formula, but applied to substances in a reaction rather than elements in a single compound — divide by Mr (not Ar) and the output is a set of coefficients (not a formula).
  11. The spec point is Higher tier only (1CH0/1H and 1CH0/2H), with typical tariff 2–4 marks and command word Calculate or Deduce.
  12. Mathematical Skills assessed: 1a (decimal expressions) and 1c (ratios, fractions and percentages).
