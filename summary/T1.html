Atomic Structure & the Periodic Table — Summary Sheet

1.1.1.1 Changing Models of the Atom F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Dalton's model (early 1800s) said atoms are tiny solid spheres that cannot be divided, are identical for a given element, and combine in fixed whole-number ratios.
  2. Dalton's model was replaced because new experiments showed atoms contain even smaller charged particles (subatomic particles).
  3. Thomson's discovery of the electron in 1897 led to the plum pudding model: a sphere of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.
  4. Rutherford's alpha-scattering experiment (Geiger and Marsden, 1909) showed that most alpha particles passed through gold foil, some were deflected, and a very small number bounced back — proving the positive charge and mass are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus and that most of the atom is empty space.
  5. Bohr (1913) proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed energy levels (shells), which explained why the nuclear atom is stable.
  6. Chadwick (1932) discovered the neutron, which explained the extra mass of the nucleus and the existence of isotopes.
  7. The modern nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus made of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons arranged in shells, with most of the atom being empty space.
  8. To score on "give two differences between Dalton's model and today's model", state new positive content of the modern model — subatomic particles, the nucleus, electrons in shells, mostly empty space, or isotopes — not just the negative reverse of Dalton.
  9. Saying "Dalton thought atoms were the smallest particle, today we know they are not" is not a scoring answer — it does not name what the modern atom actually contains.
  10. Scientific models change when new experimental evidence cannot be explained by the existing model — this is the underlying "how science works" idea behind this spec point.
  11. The most common command word on this SP is give, usually for 1–2 marks; explain questions ask you to link new experimental evidence to a change in the model.
  12. Both the November 2021 Foundation and Higher papers used the same overlap question on this SP, showing it is a high-confidence area to revise for both tiers.
1.1.2.1 The Structure of an Atom F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An atom has a small, dense nucleus at the centre, surrounded by electrons in shells.
  2. The nucleus contains two sub-atomic particles: protons and neutrons.
  3. Protons are positively charged (+1), neutrons have no charge (0), and electrons are negatively charged (−1).
  4. Electrons sit in shells around the nucleus — not inside it.
  5. An atom is electrically neutral because the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the positive and negative charges cancel out.
  6. Almost all of the mass of an atom is in the nucleus, because protons and neutrons have a relative mass of 1 each, while electrons have negligible mass.
  7. Almost all of the volume of an atom is empty space — the nucleus is tiny compared with the atom as a whole.
  8. Electrons fill shells in a fixed order: the first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the second and third hold up to 8 each (at GCSE).
  9. When asked to describe atomic structure, name all of the components — nucleus, protons, neutrons, electrons — and say where each is found.
  10. When asked to compare today's model with an older model (e.g. Dalton's), build the answer around the Spec 1.2 components rather than just repeating the stem.
  11. Watch your charge wording: the nucleus is positively charged because of the protons, not neutral because of the neutrons.
  12. This spec point is the foundation for everything that follows in atomic structure — mass number, atomic number, isotopes, ions, electronic configuration and bonding all assume the model described here.
1.1.2.2 The Sub-atomic Particles F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. There are three sub-atomic particles: protons, neutrons and electrons.
  2. Protons and neutrons are found inside the nucleus; electrons move in shells around the nucleus.
  3. A proton has a relative mass of 1 and a relative charge of +1.
  4. A neutron has a relative mass of 1 and a relative charge of 0.
  5. An electron has a very small (negligible, ~1/1836) relative mass and a relative charge of −1.
  6. The negative sign on the electron's charge must always be written explicitly — "1" is not the same as "−1".
  7. Almost all the mass of an atom comes from the protons and neutrons in the nucleus; the electrons contribute negligibly.
  8. A neutral atom has no overall charge because the number of protons equals the number of electrons.
  9. The +1 charge of each proton is exactly cancelled by the −1 charge of each electron — neutrons play no part in balancing charge because they are uncharged.
  10. If an atom gains or loses electrons it is no longer neutral and becomes an ion (this is not an atom anymore).
  11. For a 2-mark "explain why an atom has no overall charge" answer, give both (a) equal numbers of protons and electrons and (b) the charges therefore cancel or balance.
  12. In 1CH0/1H November 2021 Q2(a), examiners noted that successful answers named the particles specifically; vague references to "smaller particles" did not score.
1.1.3.1 The Nucleus F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The nucleus is the small, dense, positively charged centre of an atom containing protons and neutrons.
  2. The nucleus is very small compared to the whole atom — about 10,000 times smaller in diameter (atom ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m, nucleus ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ m).
  3. Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in the nucleus because protons and neutrons are about 1,836 times heavier than electrons.
  4. Electrons orbit the nucleus in shells and contribute almost no mass to the atom.
  5. Atoms are mostly empty space — by volume the nucleus is roughly one trillionth (10⁻¹²) of the atom.
  6. The density of nuclear matter is enormous — this is what allows the nucleus to deflect alpha particles back during the alpha-scattering experiment.
  7. The fact that most alpha particles passed straight through gold foil shows the nucleus is tiny; the fact that a few bounced back shows the nucleus is heavy and dense.
  8. To describe the nucleus accurately on an exam, use phrases like "very small compared to the overall size of the atom" and "most of the mass is in the nucleus" — these are the spec wordings.
  9. To estimate the relative size of the nucleus and atom, use standard form: divide atom diameter by nucleus diameter and read off the order of magnitude (Maths skill 1d).
  10. The size and mass of the nucleus underpin downstream content on isotopes, mass number, atomic number, and the modern model of the atom.
  11. Do not confuse "where most of the mass is" with "where the most particles are" — there are still electrons present, they just weigh almost nothing.
  12. Spec 1.5 and 1.6 are foundation building blocks: they rarely appear alone but are constantly assumed in questions on atomic structure, isotopes, and the development of the atomic model.
1.1.3.2 Mass Number & Atomic Number F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus and is unique to each element.
  2. The mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
  3. In a nuclear symbol, the mass number sits at the top left and the atomic number sits at the bottom left of the element symbol.
  4. For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons (= the atomic number).
  5. The number of neutrons is calculated as mass number − atomic number (A − Z).
  6. The mass number is always a whole number because it counts particles, but the relative atomic mass on the periodic table is usually not a whole number because it is a weighted average across isotopes.
  7. Two atoms of the same element always have the same atomic number, but they can have different mass numbers if they are different isotopes.
  8. Changing the number of protons changes the element — this never happens in ordinary chemical reactions.
  9. For an ion, the proton and neutron counts are unchanged from the neutral atom; only the electron count is different. Negative ions have gained electrons; positive ions have lost them.
  10. The most common Higher-tier mistake (flagged in the 1CH0/1H November 2021 examiner report) is treating an ion as an atom when calculating subatomic particles — always check the charge before working out electrons.
  11. For a negative ion, electrons = atomic number + size of charge; for a positive ion, electrons = atomic number − size of charge.
  12. Show your subtraction when calculating neutrons (or electrons in an ion) — this can earn a method mark even if your final number is wrong.
1.1.4.1 Isotopes F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Isotopes are different atoms of the same element containing the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons in their nuclei. Both halves are needed to score full marks on a definition.
  2. The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons; it defines the element and never changes between isotopes of the same element.
  3. The mass number (A) is the total number of protons + neutrons in the nucleus.
  4. Number of neutrons = A − Z. This rearrangement is Maths skill 3b and is the most-tested 1-mark calculation in Topic 1.
  5. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons. In an ion, electrons = protons − charge (so a 2− ion has 2 more electrons than protons; a 2+ ion has 2 fewer).
  6. Isotopes of the same element have identical chemistry because chemistry depends on electrons, and the electron count is the same — only mass differs.
  7. Relative atomic mass is the weighted average mass of all naturally occurring isotopes of an element, compared to 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  8. Aᵣ values like 35.5 (chlorine), 63.5 (copper) or 24.3 (magnesium) are not whole numbers because the element exists as a mixture of isotopes with different masses, and the average sits between the integer mass numbers — closer to whichever isotope is more abundant.
  9. The Foundation explanation only needs the qualitative reasoning. The full quantitative weighted-average calculation is (Higher tier) only and lives in 1.1.5.1.
  10. When asked "in terms of subatomic particles" — name protons and neutrons explicitly. Naming "atomic number" and "mass number" only scores 1 of 2 marks.
  11. Avoid the three classic mistakes flagged by examiner reports: (i) saying "same neutrons" (wrong — that breaks the definition); (ii) saying "different elements" (wrong — isotopes are the same element); (iii) confusing isotopes with ions (different things — ions involve electron transfer, isotopes do not).
  12. The standard exam wording — "Explain, in terms of subatomic particles, what is meant by the term isotopes" (2 marks) — is reused across sittings on both tiers. Learn it.
1.1.5.1 Calculating Relative Atomic Mass H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Relative atomic mass (A_r) is the weighted mean mass of the atoms of an element compared to 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom — it is not a simple arithmetic mean.
  2. The standard formula is A_r = (mass₁ × % abundance₁ + mass₂ × % abundance₂ + …) / 100.
  3. If abundances are given as decimal fractions rather than percentages, divide by 1 instead of 100 — check the form of the data before substituting.
  4. Always show every step of your working — multiplication, sum, division by 100 — so you can pick up error-carried-forward marks if a single arithmetic slip occurs.
  5. Always present the final answer to the requested number of decimal places (commonly 2 d.p. on Edexcel) — this is usually a stand-alone mark you can earn even if your earlier calculation is wrong.
  6. Sense-check: A_r must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotopic masses, and must be closer to the more abundant isotope — if your answer is outside that range or skewed the wrong way, recheck your working.
  7. Do not round a calculated A_r down to a whole number — the whole purpose of the calculation is to produce a non-integer weighted mean (the November 2021 silicon error pattern).
  8. Isotopes have the same atomic number (same chemistry) but different mass numbers — the difference comes from the number of neutrons in the nucleus.
  9. Lithium worked example (June 2019): 7.59% lithium-6, 92.41% lithium-7 → A_r = (6 × 7.59 + 7 × 92.41) / 100 = 6.92 (2 d.p.).
  10. Silicon worked example (Nov 2021): three isotopes (28, 29, 30) → A_r ≈ 28.11 (2 d.p.) — must not be rounded to 28.
  11. This spec point is Higher tier only on both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science) — Foundation candidates are not expected to perform A_r calculations.
  12. Do not confuse A_r calculations with empirical formula calculations — both use percentages, but empirical formulae use moles and ratios, not weighted means (another error pattern flagged in the Nov 2021 examiner report).
1.2.1.1 Mendeleev's Arrangement F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Dmitri Mendeleev published the first widely accepted periodic table in 1869, working from only about 60 known elements.
  2. He arranged the elements in order of increasing relative atomic mass, because atomic number had not yet been discovered.
  3. He grouped elements with similar chemical and physical properties into vertical columns (groups), using their reactions and the formulae of their compounds (especially oxides and chlorides) as evidence.
  4. He left gaps in the table for elements he believed existed but had not yet been discovered.
  5. He swapped some pairs of elements (e.g. tellurium and iodine) so they sat in the column that matched their chemistry, even when this broke the strict atomic-mass order.
  6. Mendeleev's table did not include the noble gases, because none of them had been discovered in 1869.
  7. The modern periodic table is ordered by atomic (proton) number rather than atomic mass, which removes the order anomalies Mendeleev had to fix manually.
  8. The modern table contains all 118 known elements, includes the noble gases as Group 0, and separates the transition metals into their own block between Groups 2 and 3.
  9. The principle of periodicity — that properties repeat at regular intervals — is the key idea Mendeleev recognised and is the reason the modern table is so powerful.
  10. In the exam, a "state one difference" question is worth 1 mark and a "describe two differences" question is worth 2 marks; choose differences from genuinely different categories (ordering property, element coverage, noble gases, transition-metal position).
  11. The most common student error is saying Mendeleev used "atomic number" — he used atomic mass; atomic number is the modern ordering property.
  12. Mendeleev's willingness to trust the chemistry over the numerical mass-order is what made his table predictive and is why his arrangement is still recognised as the foundation of the modern periodic table.
1.2.1.2 Mendeleev's Predictions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Mendeleev published his periodic table in 1869, when only about 60 elements were known.
  2. He arranged the known elements largely in order of atomic mass and grouped them by similar chemical properties.
  3. Where the next known element didn't fit the pattern, he left a gap rather than forcing a poor match.
  4. Each gap was treated as a placeholder for an undiscovered element — he gave these elements provisional names with the prefix "eka-" (e.g. eka-aluminium, eka-silicon).
  5. He predicted the properties of these missing elements by looking at the trends in the properties of the elements directly above, below, left and right of the gap.
  6. His three most famous predictions were eka-aluminium (gallium, discovered 1875), eka-boron (scandium, 1879) and eka-silicon (germanium, 1886).
  7. The actual measured properties of these elements — relative atomic mass, density, melting point — closely matched Mendeleev's predictions.
  8. The success of these predictions is the main reason Mendeleev's table was accepted as the standard arrangement of the elements.
  9. To answer a 2-mark "Describe" question on this spec point, you must mention both ideas: that he left gaps AND that he used trends in neighbouring elements to predict the properties.
  10. Mendeleev's method is a textbook example of a scientific theory making testable predictions, which were then confirmed by later experiments.
1.2.1.3 Problems with Mendeleev's Table F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Mendeleev arranged the elements in order of increasing relative atomic mass, then grouped together elements with similar chemistry.
  2. For most elements the mass order matches the chemical order, but for a few adjacent pairs the rule breaks down.
  3. The most commonly cited swap-pairs are argon/potassium (Ar 39.9 / 39.1), tellurium/iodine (Ar 127.6 / 126.9), and cobalt/nickel (Ar 58.9 / 58.7) — in each case the lighter-Ar element actually has the higher atomic number.
  4. The modern periodic table fixes this by ordering elements by atomic number (number of protons), not by relative atomic mass.
  5. Atomic number is a whole number that is fixed for every atom of an element, so it always gives a consistent ordering.
  6. Relative atomic mass is a weighted mean of isotope masses — that is why values like 35.5 (Cl) or 63.5 (Cu) appear on the periodic table.
  7. The cause of the swap-pairs is the relative abundance of isotopes: if a lighter element's isotopes happen to be unusually heavy, or a heavier element's isotopes are unusually light, the average masses end up in the "wrong" order.
  8. Mendeleev could not explain the swaps from first principles because isotopes, protons and neutrons were not yet known in 1869.
  9. Mendeleev sometimes swapped pairs by hand (e.g. tellurium before iodine) because the chemistry demanded it, even though mass alone did not justify the swap.
  10. In an Explain question on this spec point, your answer should mention isotope abundance, weighted-average relative atomic mass, and ordering by atomic number in the modern table.
  11. This spec point is examined on both Foundation and Higher tier, on both 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry) and 1SC0 (Combined Science), on Paper 1.
  12. Use the words isotope and atomic number explicitly — paraphrases like "different mass" or "different size" do not score the marking points reliably.
1.2.2.1 The Modern Periodic Table F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The modern periodic table arranges every element in order of increasing atomic number — that is, in order of the number of protons in each element's nucleus.
  2. Atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus. Every element has a unique atomic number, so it has a unique place in the table.
  3. The horizontal rows are called periods. The period number tells you how many electron shells are occupied.
  4. The vertical columns are called groups. The group number (for the main groups) tells you how many electrons are in the outer shell.
  5. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons, and outer-shell electrons control how an element reacts.
  6. For example, all Group 1 elements (Li, Na, K…) have one outer electron, lose it to form 1+ ions, and react vigorously with water.
  7. Atomic number is a better ordering principle than atomic mass because the proton count defines the element and produces the chemical-family pattern with no need for swaps (unlike Mendeleev's mass-based table, where Te and I had to be swapped to fit).
  8. Mendeleev left gaps in his table for undiscovered elements; the modern table has no gaps because all naturally occurring elements have been found and placed.
  9. The noble gases (Group 0) were not in Mendeleev's original table — they had not yet been discovered — but are included in the modern table on the far right.
  10. To find an element's position, locate it on the data-sheet periodic table, read across to find the period number and up the column to find the group number — then state both clearly.
  11. Confusing "group" with "period" is a known examiner-flagged error (1CH0/1F June 2019). Always state both explicitly: "in group X and in period Y".
  12. For a "describe two differences between Mendeleev's table and the modern table" question (e.g. 1CH0/1F June 2024 Q5(d)), good answers compare ordering principle (mass vs atomic number), the presence of gaps, the inclusion of the noble gases, and the absence of swap-anomalies.
1.2.2.2 Metals and Non-Metals in the Periodic Table F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The periodic table is split into metals and non-metals by a stepped or staircase line running diagonally from between boron and aluminium down to between polonium and astatine.
  2. Everything to the left of the staircase line is a metal; everything to the right is a non-metal. About 80% of all elements are metals.
  3. Metals lose electrons from their outer shell to form positive ions (cations) such as Na⁺, Mg²⁺, and Al³⁺.
  4. Non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions (anions) such as Cl⁻, O²⁻, and N³⁻ — except the noble gases, which already have full outer shells and form no ions.
  5. Metals lose electrons easily because they have few outer-shell electrons (1, 2, or 3) and these electrons are far from the nucleus and shielded, so weakly held.
  6. Non-metals gain electrons easily because they have almost-full outer shells (5, 6, or 7 electrons) and only need to gain 1, 2, or 3 to reach the noble-gas configuration.
  7. Physical properties of metals: shiny, malleable, ductile, sonorous, good conductors of heat and electricity, generally high melting points and high density.
  8. Physical properties of non-metals: mostly dull, brittle when solid, poor conductors of heat and electricity, generally low melting points and low density (graphite is the main exception for conductivity; diamond and silicon for melting point).
  9. Metal oxides are basic (react with acids to form salts); non-metal oxides are acidic (react with alkalis to form salts). The behaviour of an unknown oxide is a reliable diagnostic for the parent element.
  10. Hydrogen is an exception: it sits in Group 1 but behaves as a non-metal — colourless gas, does not conduct, can gain or share electrons.
  11. When asked to Explain the metal/non-metal classification of an element, always link the answer to the number of outer-shell electrons and whether they are lost or gained to reach a full shell.
  12. Knowing whether an element is a metal or a non-metal is the gateway to predicting bonding type: metal + non-metal → ionic bond; non-metal + non-metal → covalent bond; metal + metal → metallic bond.
1.2.3.1 Electronic Configurations F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The first 20 elements have electrons arranged in shells with the capacity sequence 2, 8, 8, 2 — first shell holds 2, second holds 8, third holds 8, fourth begins after the third reaches 8.
  2. Electrons always fill shells from the innermost outwards, because the inner shells are at lower energy and are more strongly attracted to the nucleus.
  3. The electronic configuration is written as numbers separated by full stops (e.g. sodium = 2.8.1), starting from the innermost shell.
  4. To predict an electronic configuration: take the atomic number (= number of electrons in a neutral atom) and fill shells in order, respecting the 2-8-8-2 capacities.
  5. A dot-and-cross shell diagram showing the correct totals in each shell is an accepted alternative to the 2.8.1 numerical form, but the numerical form is the spec's preferred shorthand.
  6. The period number of an element equals the number of shells its atom uses (e.g. calcium has four shells, so it is in period 4).
  7. The group number of an element equals the number of electrons in its outer shell (e.g. calcium has 2 outer-shell electrons, so it is in group 2; chlorine has 7, so it is in group 7).
  8. Helium is in group 0 despite having a full outer shell of 2 electrons — it is grouped with the other noble gases because it behaves chemically like them.
  9. Common exam wording trap (Nov 2020 1H): saying an element has "four outer shells" is not credit-worthy — an atom has only one outer shell. Correct phrasing is "four shells" (justifies period) and "two electrons in its outer shell" (justifies group).
  10. Common exam wording trap (Nov 2020 1H): always check whether the question asks for the period or the group — a significant share of candidates lose marks by giving the wrong one.
  11. Spec 1.19 is typically tested as a 1-mark "predict / give the electronic configuration" item; spec 1.20 is typically tested as a 2-mark "explain" item linking configuration to position.
  12. Elements in the same group all have the same number of outer-shell electrons, which is why they react in similar ways — the foundation for the periodic patterns you study in Group 1, Group 7 and Group 0.
