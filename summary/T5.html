5.1.1.1 Transition Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Most metals are transition metals, found in the central d-block of the Periodic Table between Group 2 and Group 3 (Groups 3–12).
  2. The four typical properties of transition metals (spec 5.1C) are: (a) high melting point, (b) high density, (c) formation of coloured compounds, (d) catalytic activity of the metals and their compounds.
  3. Iron is the exemplar transition-metal catalyst, used in the Haber Process to make ammonia.
  4. Other important transition-metal catalysts: platinum/rhodium (catalytic converters), manganese(IV) oxide (decomposition of H₂O₂), nickel (hydrogenation of vegetable oils).
  5. Typical aqueous solution colours to recognise: Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ pale green, Fe³⁺ yellow/orange-brown, Cr³⁺ green, Mn²⁺ pale pink.
  6. Transition metals contrast sharply with Group 1 (alkali) metals: transition metals are hard, dense, high-melting, low-reactivity, and form coloured compounds; Group 1 metals are soft, low-density, low-melting, highly reactive, and form white/colourless compounds.
  7. On a "state" question about a transition metal's position, write one short statement — "it is a transition metal" or "it is in the d-block". Do not write "in the middle", name only the period, or just say "it is a metal" — these scored zero in 1CH0/1H 2019 June.
  8. Catalytic activity is linked to transition metals' ability to switch between different oxidation states, providing alternative reaction pathways with lower activation energy.
  9. Real-world uses link directly to the four properties: hardness/melting point for construction, density for shielding, coloured compounds for pigments, catalytic activity for industrial processes.
  10. This SP is Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only — it does not appear on the Combined Science paper.
  11. The SP is examined on Paper 1 at both Foundation and Higher tier, usually as 1–3 mark recall, MCQ or tick-box style questions.
  12. When asked to identify whether an unknown metal is a transition metal, look for the combination of high melting point, high density, coloured compounds, and catalytic activity.
5.1.2.1 Oxidation of Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Corrosion is the gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with substances in its environment, primarily oxygen and water.
  2. The chemistry of corrosion is oxidation: at Foundation tier this means the metal gains oxygen; at Higher tier it also means the metal loses electrons.
  3. The general reaction is metal + oxygen → metal oxide, with water often involved as well (e.g. 4 Fe + 3 O₂ + 6 H₂O → 4 Fe(OH)₃).
  4. Rusting is the specific corrosion of iron in the presence of both oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide — flaky orange-brown rust.
  5. More reactive metals (Na, K, Mg) corrode faster than less reactive metals (Cu, Ag); gold and platinum essentially do not corrode, which is why gold is used in jewellery.
  6. Aluminium forms a thin, hard, impermeable layer of Al₂O₃ on its surface that blocks further oxidation — this is why aluminium objects appear not to corrode despite aluminium's high reactivity.
  7. Other everyday examples: copper develops a green patina of basic copper carbonate over years; silver tarnishes black as silver sulfide (Ag₂S) reacts with H₂S in the air.
  8. In the June 2019 Higher paper, examiners insisted on specific answers — "iron is reactive" did not score; you must refer to reaction with oxygen and/or water or to corrosion/rusting.
  9. The same report warned candidates not to invent wrong reasons — iron does conduct electricity, and claiming it does not is a common scoring trap.
  10. When oxygen is removed from a system (e.g. by hydrazine), corrosion slows because oxygen is required for it — exam answers must explicitly link "less oxygen" to "less rusting" to get the second mark.
  11. Common command words: Describe (state what happens, 2–3 marks), Explain (give a reason, 2–3 marks). Always include a chemical reason, not just a restatement of the stem.
  12. Prevention methods (barriers, galvanising, sacrificial protection) are not part of this spec point — they are covered in 5.1.2.2 Rusting of Iron.
5.1.2.2 Rusting of Iron F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Rusting is the corrosion of iron in the presence of both oxygen and water, producing hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).
  2. Word equation: iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide. Symbol equation: 4 Fe + 3 O₂ + 6 H₂O → 4 Fe(OH)₃.
  3. If either oxygen or water is absent, rusting cannot occur — this is the key teaching point and the basis of every prevention method.
  4. Salt water (sea water, gritted roads) speeds up rusting because the dissolved ions act as an electrolyte; pure dry air does not cause rusting.
  5. The classic 3-tube experiment: A (water + air, rusts), B (boiled water + oil layer, no oxygen, no rust), C (anhydrous calcium chloride, no water, no rust).
  6. Method (a) — exclusion of oxygen: difficult in everyday use; achieved by storing iron under oil or by boiling water and sealing it. Removes the O₂ reactant.
  7. Method (b) — exclusion of water: physical barriers such as painting, oiling/greasing, and plating (tin, chromium, zinc). Cheap and widely used, but fails if the barrier is damaged.
  8. Method (c) — sacrificial protection: a more reactive metal (Zn or Mg) is attached to the iron and corrodes preferentially, sparing the iron. Used on ship hulls and pipelines.
  9. Galvanising combines methods (b) and (c): zinc is both a barrier and a sacrificial metal, so galvanised iron stays protected even when the zinc layer is scratched.
  10. Tin-plating is barrier-only — if scratched, iron rusts faster than bare iron because tin is less reactive than iron. Similarly, connecting iron to copper accelerates corrosion of the iron.
  11. Sacrificial protection only works with a metal above iron in the reactivity series. Zn ✓, Mg ✓, Cu ✗.
  12. In "explain" questions, always link the prevention method back to the rusting requirement — e.g. "the oil layer stops oxygen reaching the iron, and oxygen is needed for rusting." Examiners deduct marks when this linkage is left implicit.
5.1.2.3 Electroplating F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Electroplating is a process of coating a metal object with a thin layer of another metal using electrolysis.
  2. The object being plated is the cathode (negative electrode); the plating metal is the anode (positive electrode).
  3. The electrolyte is an aqueous solution of a salt of the plating metal (e.g. silver nitrate for silver-plating; copper(II) sulfate for copper-plating).
  4. At the cathode, metal ions gain electrons and are deposited as solid metal: Mⁿ⁺ (aq) + n e⁻ → M (s).
  5. At the anode, atoms of the plating metal lose electrons and dissolve into the electrolyte: M (s) → Mⁿ⁺ (aq) + n e⁻.
  6. Because the anode replaces what the cathode removes, the concentration of metal ions in the electrolyte stays essentially constant.
  7. Electroplating is used to improve appearance (e.g. silver-plate cheap cutlery, gold-plate jewellery, chromium-plate taps).
  8. Electroplating is used to prevent corrosion (e.g. chromium-plate steel car parts, tin-plate steel food cans).
  9. Other valid reasons include saving cost (less of an expensive metal needed) and improving hardness or wear resistance (e.g. chromium-plating tools).
  10. In exam answers, be specific — "protects from corrosion" earns the mark; just "protects" does not; "makes it stronger" is alloying, not electroplating; "cheaper" alone is too vague.
  11. Electroplating differs from alloying (which mixes metals throughout the bulk) and from galvanising (zinc coating that also gives sacrificial protection).
  12. The same set-up, run with a thin pure-copper cathode in copper(II) sulfate, is used industrially to purify copper (3.2.5.2) — same chemistry, different purpose.
5.1.3.1 Alloys F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually metals, sometimes carbon), designed to improve a specific property — most often strength or corrosion resistance.
  2. Pure metals are made of identical-size atoms arranged in regular layers; these layers can slide easily over each other, which is why pure metals are soft and malleable but often not strong.
  3. In an alloy, the added element's atoms are a different size to the host metal's atoms, disrupting the regular layers.
  4. Because the layers can no longer slide easily past each other, alloys are harder and stronger than the pure metal — but typically less malleable.
  5. The standard "model" for spec point 5.5C is the 2D particle diagram: pure metal as identical circles in straight lines, alloy as the same lattice disrupted by larger/smaller circles. Math skill 5b (translating diagrammatic and numerical information).
  6. Steel = iron + carbon. Mild steel (low C) is malleable; high-carbon steel (more C) is harder but more brittle.
  7. Stainless steel = iron + chromium (often with nickel). Strong and doesn't rust — ideal for cutlery and surgical tools.
  8. Brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn) and magnalium (Al + Mg) are the main non-ferrous alloys to know. Solder is Sn + Pb (older) or Sn + Cu (modern lead-free).
  9. Gold carats: 24-carat = pure gold (too soft for everyday jewellery); 18-carat = 75% gold + 25% other metals (harder and more durable).
  10. When asked why an alloy is used for a job, name a specific property (doesn't rust, stronger, harder, low density) and link it to the use. Do not write "cheaper" — alloys are often more expensive than the pure metal.
  11. Do not cite malleability as a reason an alloy is preferred over a pure metal — alloys are usually less malleable than the pure metal.
  12. Typical 5.5C exam wording is "Explain, in terms of the arrangement of metal particles, why [alloy] is stronger than pure [metal]" — worth 2–3 marks, command word explain, expects the particle model in both words and (often) a diagram.
5.1.3.2 Steel Alloys F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Pure iron is relatively soft, malleable, and rusts in moist air, which is why almost all iron used in everyday objects is converted into steel.
  2. Steel is an alloy of iron with carbon and sometimes other metals; it is the most economically important alloy in the world.
  3. Alloying works because the added atoms are a different size from iron atoms, disrupting the regular lattice and preventing layers of iron atoms from sliding — making the alloy harder and stronger.
  4. Mild steel (0.05–0.25% C) is malleable and easily shaped — used for car bodies, food cans and large structures.
  5. High-carbon steel (0.6–1.5% C) is hard but more brittle — used for cutting tools, chisels and knife blades.
  6. As carbon content rises, hardness rises but malleability and ductility fall.
  7. Stainless steel is iron + chromium (~10–25%) + nickel; it does not rust because chromium reacts with oxygen to form a thin, impermeable, self-healing layer of Cr₂O₃ that seals the iron below.
  8. Stainless steel is also stronger than pure iron — both properties are needed for full marks when comparing stainless steel to iron in an exam.
  9. Tool steel (iron + tungsten / vanadium / molybdenum) is very hard and keeps its hardness at high temperatures — used for drill bits and lathe tools.
  10. The classic Edexcel question (1CH0/1F, June 2019, Q2(a)) asks for two reasons cutlery is made of stainless steel rather than iron: most students give "doesn't rust" but forget "stronger/harder than iron".
  11. Avoid the examiner-flagged wrong answers: "cheaper" (stainless steel is usually more expensive), "not staining" (too vague — say "doesn't rust"), and any answer based on malleability (not the discriminator).
  12. For an explain question, name the property and link it to the use; for a give question, a short factual reason is enough.
5.1.4.1 Using Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The uses of metals are chosen because their properties match the job — every exam answer must link a specific property to a specific use and explain why the link works.
  2. Aluminium has a low density (aircraft bodies), is a good electrical conductor (overhead power cables), resists corrosion through a tough Al₂O₃ layer (window frames, drinks cans), and is shiny (cooking foil).
  3. Copper is an excellent electrical conductor (wiring), a good thermal conductor (saucepans, hot water pipes), unreactive with air and water at room temperature (plumbing), and malleable (drawn into wires).
  4. Gold does not corrode or tarnish (jewellery, electrical contacts), is very malleable (gold leaf), has an attractive yellow colour (decoration), and is a reliable electrical conductor in high-end electronics.
  5. Magnalium is an aluminium–magnesium alloy: stronger than pure aluminium while keeping a low density, used for aircraft frames and racing bikes.
  6. Brass is a copper–zinc alloy: harder than copper with a gold-like colour, used for musical instruments, door handles, screws and decorative fittings.
  7. Alloys are harder and stronger than pure metals because the different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers of the lattice, making it harder for layers to slide over one another.
  8. Aluminium's corrosion resistance comes from a thin, tough Al₂O₃ layer that seals the surface — the metal underneath is actually reactive but is protected by its own oxide.
  9. Gold's corrosion resistance is different: gold is unreactive in the first place and sits low in the reactivity series, so it does not need a protective layer.
  10. The signature exam task for this SP is a 6-mark open-response on Foundation Paper 1 — give one property, one use, and one reason for each named metal, covering all metals listed in the stem.
  11. Vague reasoning loses marks: "conducts electricity" is weaker than "is an excellent conductor of electricity, so very little energy is lost as heat".
  12. Cited exam appearances: 1CH0/1F November 2020 Q7(c) (all five metals, 6 marks) and 1CH0/1F June 2024 Q7(b) (copper specifically, 6 marks).
5.2.1.1 Using Concentrations in mol/dm³ H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Concentration measures how much solute is dissolved in a fixed volume of solution; the two GCSE units are g dm⁻³ (F+H) and mol dm⁻³ (Higher, Separate only).
  2. The two key equations are C (g dm⁻³) = m / V and C (mol dm⁻³) = n / V, where V is always in dm³.
  3. To convert g dm⁻³ → mol dm⁻³, divide by Mr. To convert mol dm⁻³ → g dm⁻³, multiply by Mr.
  4. 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³, so any volume given in cm³ must be divided by 1000 before substituting into a concentration equation.
  5. Mr is the bridge between mass and moles: 1 mole of any substance has a mass equal to its Mr in grams.
  6. Rearrangements you'll use most often: n = C × V (moles in a given volume) and m = C × V (mass in a given volume), again with V in dm³.
  7. Use the 5-step recipe every time: identify what's given → convert volume → calculate Mr → substitute → check precision.
  8. The biggest mark-loss source flagged by examiners (1CH0/1H June 2019) is failing to divide volume by 1000, followed by dividing the wrong way round when switching between g dm⁻³ and mol dm⁻³.
  9. Always read the question for specified precision (decimal places or sig figs) — losing the final mark for wrong rounding is avoidable.
  10. Show every line of working, including the Mr calculation and the volume conversion, so that method marks are banked even if the final number is wrong.
  11. This SP is Higher tier only and Separate Chemistry only, appearing exclusively on 1CH0/1H (Paper 1).
  12. Mastering this SP unlocks the titration calculations in 5.2.3.1 — every titration problem reduces to "use C = n / V to find an unknown".
5.2.2.1 Core Practical: Acid-Alkali Titration F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The titration is the standard practical for measuring the exact volume of one solution that reacts with a known volume of another, using an indicator to detect the end-point.
  2. Carry out a rough titration first to find the approximate end-point, then at least two accurate titrations dropwise near that volume.
  3. Only concordant results — within 0.10 cm³ of each other — count towards the mean titre. The rough run is discarded.
  4. Key apparatus you must be able to name: burette (acid), pipette with pipette filler (alkali), conical flask, white tile, funnel (for filling burette only), retort stand.
  5. Read the burette from the bottom of the meniscus at eye level to the nearest 0.05 cm³.
  6. Phenolphthalein (pink → colourless) is the standard indicator for HCl + NaOH; methyl orange (yellow → red) is used when titrating a strong acid with a weak base such as Na₂CO₃; universal indicator is not suitable because the colour change is gradual.
  7. Remove the funnel from the burette before titrating so drips don't add extra acid uncounted.
  8. Rinse the pipette with the alkali (not water) before drawing the measured volume — residual water dilutes the solution.
  9. Run a little acid through the tap before starting to remove any air bubble — otherwise the titre will read too high.
  10. Swirl the conical flask gently and continuously so the contents mix without splashing out.
  11. If a question covers both titration and crystallisation (e.g. preparing a soluble salt from an alkali), you must describe both halves to score top-band marks — and the second titration to prepare the salt is done without indicator.
  12. The actual calculation of concentration or mass from titration data is assessed under 5.2.3.1 Titration Calculations — not under this core-practical SP.
5.2.3.1 Titration Calculations H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Titration calculations only use concordant titres — those within 0.10 cm³ of each other — and the rough titre is discarded before averaging.
  2. Volumes must be converted from cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000 before substituting into n = c × V.
  3. The full procedure has seven steps: mean titre → convert volumes → moles of the known → mole ratio from the balanced equation → moles of the unknown → final c or V → correct precision.
  4. The mole ratio comes from the balancing numbers in the equation — not from assuming 1 : 1. Diprotic acids (H₂SO₄) and dihydroxide bases (Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂) give 1 : 2 or 2 : 1 ratios.
  5. To find an unknown concentration, rearrange to c = n / V; to find an unknown volume, rearrange to V = n / c.
  6. When a concentration is given in g dm⁻³, divide by Mr to convert to mol dm⁻³ — mass ÷ Mr, never the inverse.
  7. If a question asks for the answer in cm³, remember to convert dm³ back to cm³ (× 1000) at the end.
  8. Always quote the final answer to the sig figs of the supplied data (usually 3 sf) or to the decimal places specified in the question — examiner reports flag both as common mark-losers.
  9. Common slips flagged by the 1CH0/1H June 2019 examiner report are inverting mass / Mr (e.g. 159.5 / 15.95), forgetting ÷ 1000 on the volume, dividing 15.95 by 50, and failing to give the final answer to one decimal place.
  10. The command word is almost always "Calculate", the typical tariff is 2–4 marks, and the question lives in the middle-to-end region of 1CH0/1H.
  11. This spec point is Higher tier only and Separate Chemistry only — it does not appear on Foundation or on Combined Science papers.
  12. Show every step of your working: examiners explicitly note that candidates who write out full working pick up part marks even when their final number is wrong.
5.2.4.1 Yield F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product predicted from the balanced equation; actual yield is the mass actually obtained from the experiment.
  2. % yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100 — both yields must be in the same units, and the answer is a percentage with no unit.
  3. Actual yield is almost always less than theoretical yield.
  4. Three spec reasons that actual < theoretical (5.12C): (a) incomplete reactions — including reversible reactions reaching equilibrium and reactants not mixing fully; (b) practical losses — spillage, residue on filter paper, transfer losses, evaporation; (c) competing, unwanted side reactions that use up reactant to form a different product.
  5. If actual yield appears greater than theoretical yield, the product is not fully dried or contains impurities — never a reason from 5.12C.
  6. For multi-step calculations: find moles of reactant, use the balanced equation ratio, calculate theoretical yield in grams using Mr, then apply the % yield formula.
  7. A worked example: theoretical yield = 50.0 g KCl, actual yield = 38.5 g → % yield = 38.5 / 50.0 × 100 = 77.0%.
  8. A high % yield means an efficient reaction — less wasted reactant, lower cost per kg of product, less waste to dispose of in industry.
  9. Always sense-check your answer: a real reaction should give a result between 0% and 100% unless the impurity/water trap is being tested.
  10. On Paper 1, yield questions are typically 2–4 marks, with command words "Calculate" and "Suggest". The "Suggest a reason" part is usually where marks are lost.
  11. Be careful to read the question carefully — the 2020 examiner report flagged that candidates lost marks by giving a "less than 100%" reason when asked about a yield greater than 100%.
  12. % yield measures efficiency at making the product; it is one of two key efficiency measures studied alongside 5.2.5.1 Atom Economy — a reaction can have high yield but poor atom economy if a lot of unwanted by-product is formed.
5.2.5.1 Atom Economy F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Atom economy measures the proportion of reactant mass that ends up in the desired product, expressed as a percentage.
  2. The formula is: % atom economy = (Mr of desired product / total Mr of all products) × 100. You can use total Mr of reactants instead — by conservation of mass it gives the same answer.
  3. A reaction has 100% atom economy if and only if there is one product. Addition reactions (e.g. ethene + water → ethanol) fit this case.
  4. Reactions with two or more products always have atom economy less than 100%, because at least one product is not the one you want.
  5. High atom economy = less waste, lower raw-material cost, more sustainable — this is a central principle of green chemistry.
  6. Common exam trap (1CH0/1F June 2019): writing 100% because "both products are useful". The atom-economy formula uses the desired product only — not the total mass of all products.
  7. Common exam trap (1CH0/1H Nov 2020): writing 80 when the question asks for 1 decimal place. One decimal place means one digit after the decimal point (80.5). Always re-read the rounding instruction at the very end.
  8. Worked example — hydration of ethene: C₂H₄ + H₂O → C₂H₅OH has atom economy 100% (one product).
  9. Worked example — fermentation of glucose: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂ has atom economy 51.1% (92/180 × 100), because half the mass becomes CO₂.
  10. Worked example — iron extraction: Fe₂O₃ + 3 C → 2 Fe + 3 CO has atom economy 57.1% for Fe (112/196 × 100), because CO is a wasted byproduct.
  11. Always show your working. A bare incorrect final answer scores zero, but full working with a small arithmetic slip can still pick up 2–3 of the 4 marks.
  12. Atom-economy questions in 1CH0 appear on Paper 1 only, are worth 2–4 marks, and the command word is almost always Calculate.
5.2.6.1 Choosing a Reaction Pathway H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Choosing a reaction pathway means evaluating data across multiple factors — there is no single "best" pathway, only the best trade-off for a given product.
  2. The six factors to weigh are: atom economy, yield, rate, equilibrium position, cost/availability of raw materials, and usefulness of by-products.
  3. Higher atom economy means less waste and more sustainable use of reactants; a single-product reaction has 100% atom economy.
  4. Higher yield means more product per amount of reactant; a reaction with perfect atom economy but low yield still wastes the unconverted reactants.
  5. Faster rate means more product per unit time, so lower cost per tonne — slow processes (like fermentation) are commercially less attractive.
  6. Equilibrium position sets the ceiling on yield for reversible reactions; conditions are chosen to shift equilibrium towards products (Haber process is the classic example).
  7. Raw material cost and availability decide whether a pathway is economically viable — cheap, abundant, reliably-supplied feedstocks are preferred.
  8. Useful by-products can rescue a pathway with poor atom economy — selling the CO₂ from fermentation, or recycling unreacted gases, effectively raises the atom economy.
  9. Ethanol is the standard worked example: fermentation (51% atom economy, slow, batch, renewable sugar feedstock, CO₂ by-product) vs hydration of ethene (100% atom economy, fast, continuous, non-renewable ethene feedstock, no by-product).
  10. Hydration of ethene is industrially preferred wherever ethene is available, because its 100% atom economy, fast rate and continuous operation give a much lower cost per tonne.
  11. To improve the atom economy of fermentation in practice, collect and sell the CO₂ by-product so neither product is wasted (1CH0/1F June 2024 Q10(b)(ii) answer style).
  12. In exam answers, always cite the data, link to a factor, acknowledge the trade-off, and conclude with a justified choice — examiners reward weighed reasoning over a bare verdict.
5.2.7.1 Molar Volume H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of molecules of any gas at room temperature and pressure: 24 dm³ or 24 000 cm³ (provided in the question — you do not need to memorise the conditions).
  2. The rule is the same for every gas — H₂, O₂, CO₂, NH₃ — because Avogadro's law says equal moles of any gas occupy equal volumes at the same temperature and pressure.
  3. Multiply by 24 when going from moles to volume; divide by 24 when going from volume to moles. The single most common exam error is doing this the wrong way round.
  4. Convert any volume given in cm³ to dm³ (÷ 1000) before applying the molar volume formula.
  5. The core formulae: V (dm³) = moles × 24 · moles = V (dm³) ÷ 24 · mass (g) = moles × Mr.
  6. For a multi-step problem: balance the equation → moles of given species → mole ratio → moles of target species → convert to required units.
  7. When all species are gases at the same conditions, volume ratios equal mole ratios — you can apply the balanced equation directly to volumes (as in 1CH0/1H 2024 June Q10(b)).
  8. For reactions involving air, remember that air is approximately 20% O₂ by volume, so multiply the volume of O₂ by 100/20 (= 5) to obtain the volume of air needed.
  9. Molar volume questions live at the end of Higher tier Paper 1 (Q9 or Q10), worth 2–4 marks, and are almost always introduced with the command word "Calculate".
  10. Show every step with units — examiners give error-carried-forward marks for later steps even when an early step is wrong (per the 1CH0/1H June 2019 examiner report).
  11. Sanity-check your answer: one mole of gas is 24 dm³, so a few moles should produce tens of dm³, not fractions of a dm³.
  12. This spec point is Higher tier only and Separate Chemistry only — it does not appear on Foundation tier or on Combined Science (1SC0).
5.2.8.1 Avogadro's Law H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Avogadro's law: equal volumes of any gas at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules (and equal numbers of moles).
  2. For a gas-only reaction at constant T and p, the volume ratio is identical to the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
  3. You do not need the molar volume (24 dm³ mol⁻¹) for an Avogadro's law calculation — that conversion cancels out when both sides are gases.
  4. Procedure: balance the equation, identify gas species, read the coefficients, apply V_unknown = V_known × (coefficient_unknown / coefficient_known).
  5. A larger coefficient on the unknown side means multiply; a smaller coefficient means divide. Reflexively dividing was flagged as a common error in the 1CH0/1H June 2019 examiner report.
  6. The rule applies only to gases. Liquids and solids in the equation (e.g. H₂O (l)) are excluded from the volume ratio.
  7. For methane combustion CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O, 1 volume of CH₄ produces 1 volume of CO₂ and 2 volumes of water vapour — total products = 3 volumes per 1 volume of CH₄.
  8. For the Haber process N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃, 1 volume of N₂ + 3 volumes of H₂ produce 2 volumes of NH₃ — gas volume halves overall.
  9. Edexcel places these questions at the very end of Higher tier papers (Q9 or Q10), typically awarding 2–4 marks, and the command word is almost always "calculate."
  10. June 2024 Q10(b) asked candidates to calculate the maximum volume of products from 650 dm³ of methane — answer 1950 dm³ (650 dm³ CO₂ + 1300 dm³ H₂O).
  11. Always show your ratio and your working — a single line of working ("CH₄ : H₂O = 1 : 2, so 650 × 2 = 1300 dm³") secures both marks even if you slip at the final addition.
  12. If a question gives you all the information you need and you still feel unsure, ask: are all the species I'm using in the gas state, and is the equation balanced? If yes to both, the ratio is the answer — no further conversion needed.
5.3.1.1 Predicting the Conditions H only
Key Takeaways
  1. The Haber process is a reversible reaction: N₂ (g) + 3H₂ (g) ⇌ 2NH₃ (g); the forward direction is exothermic.
  2. Rate of attainment of equilibrium is how quickly the forward and reverse rates equalise — it is not the same as the position of equilibrium.
  3. Higher temperature speeds attainment (more kinetic energy → more successful collisions) but reduces the yield of an exothermic-forward reaction such as Haber (equilibrium shifts to the endothermic side).
  4. Higher pressure (for gas equilibria) speeds attainment (more molecules per unit volume → more collisions) and shifts position to the side with fewer gas moles — for Haber, more ammonia.
  5. Higher concentration of a reactant speeds attainment and shifts position to remove the added species (i.e. towards products).
  6. A catalyst speeds attainment in both directions equally and has no effect on the position of equilibrium or the yield.
  7. Energy of particles depends on temperature only. Pressure and concentration increase collision frequency, not particle energy — writing the opposite is the single most-flagged error on this spec point (1CH0/1H, June 2019).
  8. A 6-mark "Explain" question always wants both the rate-of-attainment effect and the yield effect, each with a reason — answers that cover only one half cap at around half marks (1CH0/1H, June 2019 + November 2020).
  9. Industrial Haber conditions (≈ 450 °C, ≈ 200 atm, iron catalyst) are a compromise between rate and yield — see 5.3.1.2 Choosing the Conditions for the cost-vs-yield trade-off.
  10. The mark-scheme template for full-marks 6-mark answers: state rate effect → reason → state yield effect → reason, for each condition the question names.
5.3.1.2 Choosing the Conditions H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Industrial conditions are chosen to give an acceptable yield in an acceptable time at an acceptable cost — never to maximise yield alone.
  2. The Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃) runs at 450 °C, 200 atm, with an iron catalyst — and every one of these is a compromise.
  3. The forward Haber reaction is exothermic, so a low temperature would give a higher yield but too slow a rate; 450 °C is the balance.
  4. The forward Haber reaction has 4 moles of gas → 2 moles of gas, so higher pressure favours yield; 200 atm is the balance between yield and the cost/safety of high-pressure equipment.
  5. The iron catalyst speeds up the rate of attainment of equilibrium but does not shift the position of equilibrium — it lets the process run cooler without sacrificing rate.
  6. Raw materials matter: nitrogen comes free from air; hydrogen is made from natural gas, so the cost of natural gas drives the cost of ammonia.
  7. To score full marks on a 6-mark "Explain" question you must cover both rate AND yield effects — examiners repeatedly note that candidates under-develop the yield side.
  8. Always check the equation in the question — exo- vs endothermic and the mole change in gas tell you which way Le Chatelier's principle points; never just transfer Haber reasoning.
  9. Pressure only matters for yield when the number of moles of gas changes across the equation; if both sides have the same moles of gas, pressure has no effect on yield.
  10. Other industrial equilibria use very different conditions for the same reason: Contact process uses low pressure (yield already favourable), steam reforming uses high temperature (endothermic forward).
  11. Use a 5-step answer template: state the chemistry → effect on rate → effect on yield → bring them together → state the industrial compromise.
  12. Edexcel routinely tests this SP using unfamiliar reactions — practise the reasoning, don't just memorise the Haber numbers.
5.3.2.1 Fertilisers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Fertilisers may contain nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) compounds to promote plant growth (spec 5.22C).
  2. Nitrogen is needed for protein synthesis and leaf/stem growth; plants absorb it from soil as NO₃⁻ or NH₄⁺ ions.
  3. Phosphorus is needed for root growth, energy transfer and DNA; absorbed as soluble phosphate compounds.
  4. Potassium is needed for enzyme activation, water regulation and flower/fruit growth; absorbed as K⁺ ions.
  5. Fertiliser salts must be soluble in water so plant roots can absorb the dissolved ions.
  6. The formula of ammonia is NH₃ — not NH₂ and not NH₄.
  7. The ammonium ion is NH₄⁺ — a positively charged ion formed when NH₃ gains a H⁺.
  8. Ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate: NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃ (spec 5.23C). This is the main fertiliser salt for this spec point.
  9. Ammonium nitrate is widely used because it has a very high percentage of nitrogen by mass and is very soluble in water.
  10. When asked to suggest why ammonium salts are used as fertilisers, the marking point is that they are soluble in water so the NH₄⁺ ions can be absorbed through plant roots and provide nitrogen for growth — do not just write "it is a fertiliser".
  11. Different acids react with ammonia in different ratios: 1 NH₃ : 1 HNO₃ (ammonium nitrate); 2 NH₃ : 1 H₂SO₄ (ammonium sulfate); 3 NH₃ : 1 H₃PO₄ (ammonium phosphate).
  12. This spec point is 1CH0 only (Separate Chemistry) and appears on Paper 1 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
5.3.2.2 Making Fertilisers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Ammonium sulfate is a soluble, nitrogen-containing fertiliser made by neutralising sulfuric acid with ammonia: 2 NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄.
  2. The same chemical reaction is used in both the laboratory and the industrial method — the differences are in scale, process, raw materials, and end-point detection.
  3. Lab method: pipette 25 cm³ dilute H₂SO₄ into a flask, add indicator (methyl orange or phenolphthalein), titrate dilute ammonia from a burette until the colour changes, record the volume, then repeat without indicator using that volume — the second run gives a pure salt solution.
  4. The lab method finishes by evaporating to the point of crystallisation, cooling to crystallise, filtering, and drying — gentle heating prevents the crystals decomposing.
  5. The "repeat without indicator" step is essential — otherwise the crystals would be contaminated with indicator dye.
  6. Industrial method: ammonia is made on site by the Haber process (N₂ from air, H₂ from natural gas) and sulfuric acid is made on site by the Contact process (details not required); the two are mixed in continuous-flow reactors and the product is crystallised on a huge scale.
  7. Industrial plants use pH electrodes and automated control to detect neutralisation — far more reliable than indicators at scale, and no dye contamination.
  8. Similarities to remember: same reactants, same product, same neutralisation reaction.
  9. Differences to remember: lab is small / batch; industrial is massive / continuous; lab buys in raw materials, industrial makes them on site; lab uses an indicator, industrial uses pH electrodes; per-gram cost is high in the lab and low industrially.
  10. If a 2-mark question asks for "one similarity AND one difference", give one of each — examiners credit only one of each type. Many candidates lost this mark in 1CH0/1H November 2020.
  11. Ammonium sulfate is useful as a fertiliser because it is soluble (so plants can absorb it through their roots) and contains nitrogen in a form (NH₄⁺) that plants can use — do not just say "because it's a fertiliser".
  12. Practise writing 2 NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ from memory — examiners noted that many candidates struggled with this balanced equation in 1CH0/1H November 2020.
5.4.1.1 Electrochemical Cells F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A chemical cell is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy using a redox reaction between two different metals (or metal compounds) in an electrolyte.
  2. The more reactive metal is the negative electrode — it is oxidised and loses electrons into the external circuit.
  3. The less reactive metal is the positive electrode — it is the site of reduction, where positive ions gain electrons.
  4. The electrolyte completes the circuit by allowing ions to move between the electrodes.
  5. The voltage produced depends on the difference in reactivity between the two metals — a larger gap in the reactivity series gives a larger voltage.
  6. A magnesium/copper cell gives a large voltage; a zinc/copper cell a moderate one; a copper/silver cell a small one.
  7. A chemical cell produces a voltage only until one of the reactants is used up — once a reactant is depleted, current stops flowing and the cell is flat.
  8. At Higher tier, you may be asked to write half-equations for the reactions at each electrode — the more reactive metal is oxidised (e.g. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻); the less reactive metal ion is reduced (e.g. Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu).
  9. Half-equations for cells follow the same rules as for electrolysis — the electrons must balance and a half-equation must be a balanced equation, not words or state symbols alone.
  10. Fuel cells differ from chemical cells because their reactants are supplied continuously from outside, so they do not go flat in the same way — they keep producing electricity as long as fuel flows.
  11. Common commercial chemical cells include alkaline batteries (Zn/MnO₂), silver-oxide button cells, lead-acid car batteries and lithium-ion batteries.
  12. The most common exam command word for higher-tariff questions on this spec point is evaluate, usually comparing chemical cells with hydrogen-oxygen fuel cells (e.g. 1CH0/1H, November 2020, Q7(b), 6 marks).
5.4.2.1 Fuel Cells F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell produces electricity from the reaction of hydrogen and oxygen; the overall reaction is 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O.
  2. Water is the only product. This is the single most heavily examined fact for 5.26C and the most commonly mis-recalled — examiners flagged "hydroxide", "oxygen" and "hydrogen" as frequent wrong answers in 1CH0/1F June 2019.
  3. The fuel cell produces a voltage that can drive current through an external circuit; it converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy.
  4. Hydrogen and oxygen are supplied continuously from outside the cell, so the cell does not "go flat" — unlike a chemical cell whose internal reactants run out.
  5. At the negative electrode, hydrogen is oxidised: it loses electrons and forms H⁺ ions. (Higher tier: 2 H₂ → 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻.)
  6. At the positive electrode, oxygen is reduced: it gains electrons and combines with H⁺ ions to make water. (Higher tier: O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 2 H₂O.)
  7. Electrons flow from the hydrogen electrode through the external circuit to the oxygen electrode — that flow of electrons is the electrical current that powers the load.
  8. H⁺ ions move internally through the electrolyte from the negative electrode to the positive electrode.
  9. The electrodes are typically porous and platinum-coated, with platinum acting as a catalyst for the electrode reactions.
  10. At the point of use, the only emission is water; this is the key environmental advantage of fuel-cell vehicles such as the Toyota Mirai and of historic uses in spacecraft and submarines (full evaluation in 5.4.2.2 Evaluating Fuel Cells).
  11. On 1CH0/1F, expect a 1-mark recall question (e.g. "Give the name of the product formed in this fuel cell" — 1CH0/1F 2019 Q7(c)).
  12. On 1CH0/1H, expect questions worth 2–6 marks including writing balanced equations and half-equations; the November 2021 examiner report flagged that many Higher candidates left these blank or wrote prose instead of equations — practise writing them as actual equations.
5.4.2.2 Evaluating Fuel Cells F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. 5.27C is an "evaluate" spec point — you must write strengths, weaknesses, and a justified conclusion, not a one-sided list.
  2. The only chemical product of a hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell is water — clean at the point of use, no CO₂, NOₓ, SO₂, or particulates.
  3. Fuel cells are around 50–60% efficient, compared with around 25–30% for a petrol or diesel internal combustion engine.
  4. A fuel cell gives continuous output as long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied; a chemical cell goes flat when its stored chemicals run out.
  5. Fuel cells have no moving parts, so they are quiet and low-maintenance.
  6. For long missions (spacecraft, submarines), a fuel cell can be lighter than a chemical-cell battery of equivalent total energy because tanks can be refilled.
  7. Most commercial hydrogen is made by steam reforming of natural gas, which produces CO₂ — so the carbon footprint depends on where the hydrogen came from; only "green" hydrogen from electrolysis with renewable electricity is genuinely low-carbon.
  8. Hydrogen is hard to store (very high pressure or below −253 °C), is flammable, and has low energy density by volume compared with petrol.
  9. Fuel cells currently use expensive platinum catalysts, and hydrogen refuelling infrastructure is very limited.
  10. The 1CH0/1H November 2020 Q7(b) spacecraft question is the canonical 6-mark evaluation: two or three advantages, two or three disadvantages, and a conclusion.
  11. The 1CH0/1H November 2021 examiner report flags a common error — candidates answer "why hydrogen is a better fuel than hydrocarbons" instead of evaluating the fuel-cell device versus chemical cells. Read the question carefully.
  12. Match your emphasis to the application named in the question — spacecraft, submarine, and passenger car each pull out different strengths and weaknesses from the same underlying list.
