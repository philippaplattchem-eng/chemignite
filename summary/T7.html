Rates of Reaction & Energy Changes Summary Sheet

7.1.1.1 Determining the Rate of a Reaction F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Rate of reaction is the change in amount (or concentration) of a reactant or product per unit time, calculated as rate = change in quantity ÷ time.
  2. Choose the measurement method to match the observable change: gas produced, precipitate formed, colour change, or mass loss.
  3. Gas can be collected over water (using an inverted measuring cylinder) for insoluble gases like H₂ and O₂, but not for CO₂ because CO₂ dissolves in water.
  4. A gas syringe is the most accurate routine method for collecting gas: it works for soluble and insoluble gases, has low friction, and has finer graduations than a measuring cylinder.
  5. The mass-loss method works for reactions that produce a heavy gas (typically CO₂). A loose cotton wool plug stops splashing while still letting gas escape; you cannot use this method for H₂ because the mass change is too small to detect on a school balance.
  6. The disappearing-cross method works for precipitate reactions (e.g. Na₂S₂O₃ + HCl); rate is inversely proportional to the time taken for the cross to disappear, so rate ∝ 1/time.
  7. Apparatus diagrams must show a sealed pathway from reaction flask to collection vessel — missing the bung is a very common mark-losing error.
  8. Always convert time to seconds before substituting into the rate formula, and always quote the unit (cm³/s, g/s, or mol/dm³/s).
  9. Mean rate is calculated over a whole interval; initial rate is the steepest gradient at the start of the reaction.
  10. Colorimetry can be used to follow reactions where a coloured species appears or disappears, removing the subjectivity of "by eye" judgements.
  11. Common Paper 2 tasks are: complete the apparatus diagram (2 marks), name a better piece of apparatus (1–2 marks), devise a plan (4 marks).
  12. When devising a plan, include the apparatus, what you measure, how often you measure it, the variable you change, the variables you control, and how you process the data into a rate.
7.1.1.2 Core Practical: Investigating Rate of Reaction F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Edexcel's core practical for rates uses two reactions: (a) HCl + marble chips, measuring CO₂ gas produced over time; and (b) sodium thiosulfate + HCl, timing the disappearance of a cross under a flask.
  2. Method (a) needs a conical flask with a bung and delivery tube leading to a gas syringe (preferred) or an inverted measuring cylinder over water — without the bung, the gas escapes and no marks are awarded.
  3. Method (a) is used to investigate concentration and surface area (and can also investigate temperature); method (b) is the standard set-up for investigating temperature.
  4. The rate at any moment is the gradient of the tangent to the volume–time curve; the initial rate (gradient at t = 0) is the fairest measure to compare different conditions.
  5. For method (b), plot 1/time against temperature so that the quantity on the y-axis is proportional to rate; rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise.
  6. The independent variable is what you change, the dependent variable is what you measure, and control variables (volume of acid, mass of marble chips, observer) must be kept constant for a fair test.
  7. Sulfur dioxide produced in method (b) is toxic — keep the room well ventilated and do not heat above 60 °C.
  8. The biggest source of error in method (a) is gas escape before the bung is replaced; in method (b) it is the subjective endpoint, fixed by using a light sensor / data logger.
  9. Examiner reports stress that diagram-drawing marks depend on scientific accuracy, not artistic skill — include every component (flask, bung, delivery tube, gas-collection vessel) clearly labelled.
  10. This spec point appears on Paper 2 only, on both tiers, and most often asks you to complete a diagram, name a better piece of apparatus, or devise a plan — tariffs range from 1–2 marks (apparatus) up to 4 marks (full plan).
7.1.2.1 Collision Theory F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A chemical reaction only happens when reactant particles collide with each other.
  2. Most collisions are unsuccessful — particles bounce off without reacting.
  3. A successful collision requires two things: enough energy (≥ activation energy) and the correct orientation.
  4. Activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum energy colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur; it is fixed for a given reaction.
  5. The rate of reaction increases when the frequency of collisions increases, the energy of collisions increases, or both.
  6. Frequency of collisions = how often particles meet. Increased by raising concentration, raising pressure (gases), increasing surface area, or raising temperature.
  7. Energy of collisions = how hard particles hit. Increased mainly by raising temperature; a catalyst lowers Eₐ so more collisions count as successful.
  8. Concentration, pressure and surface area change only the frequency — not the energy — of collisions. Mixing these up is the classic mark-losing mistake.
  9. Temperature is special: it raises both the frequency and the energy of collisions.
  10. A reaction slows down over time because reactant particles are used up, so the frequency of reactant-reactant collisions falls.
  11. In "explain" answers, always use the words particles, collide / collisions, frequency and, where relevant, energy — examiners flag the absence of this vocabulary.
  12. Collision theory underpins every factor in 7.1.2.2 Explaining Rates — master it here and the four factors fall into place.
7.1.2.2 Explaining Rates F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Reactions speed up when reactant particles collide more often (higher frequency) and/or when a greater proportion of collisions have energy at least equal to the activation energy.
  2. Three of the four named factors — concentration, pressure, surface area to volume ratio — act only on frequency of collisions. They do not change the energy of the particles.
  3. Temperature is the only factor that changes the energy of collisions. At GCSE level the energy effect is the dominant one — a small temperature rise can roughly double the rate of many reactions.
  4. Increasing concentration of a solution means more solute particles per unit volume, so collisions with the other reactant happen more frequently.
  5. Increasing pressure of a gas squeezes more particles into the same volume — same logic as concentration. Pressure changes have no effect on solids or liquids.
  6. Increasing surface area to volume ratio (smaller lumps of a solid) exposes more solid particles to the other reactant, so collisions happen more frequently. Volume of solid stays the same; only the exposed surface changes.
  7. For a cube of side a, surface area = 6a² and volume = a³, so the SA : V ratio = 6/a — smaller cubes have a larger SA : V ratio.
  8. Always include the words "particles" and "frequency of collisions" in an explain-in-terms-of-particles answer — the June 2019 1CH0/2F examiner report flagged that very few candidates did this.
  9. The canonical examiner trap is muddling frequency with energy of collisions. For concentration, pressure or surface area, do not write that "the particles have more energy".
  10. Read the question stem carefully — if it asks about mass loss, volume of gas, or another quantity, do not default to writing about rate of reaction.
  11. The 6-mark extended-response question on Paper 2H frequently asks you to combine two factors (e.g. lump size and concentration) using collision theory.
  12. Best-mark answers are concise — examiners praised "succinct explanations using the correct scientific terminology" on 1CH0/2H June 2019 Q8(c).
7.1.3.1 Rate Graphs F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A rate graph plots reactant or product quantity (mass, volume, or concentration) on the y-axis against time on the x-axis.
  2. The curve is steepest at the start because reactant concentrations are highest, giving the most frequent collisions.
  3. The curve levels off (plateaus) when the limiting reactant has been completely used up; the height of the plateau depends on the starting amount of that reactant.
  4. Mean rate between two times = (change in quantity) ÷ (change in time), with units of (y-axis units)/(x-axis units).
  5. Instantaneous rate at a single moment = gradient of a tangent drawn at that point on the curve.
  6. A tangent must touch the curve at one point only — never cross it, and never be too short to read accurately.
  7. When sketching a faster reaction with the same amount of reactant, draw a steeper curve that levels off at the same plateau height as the original.
  8. When sketching a reaction with less reactant, draw a curve with a similar starting gradient that levels off earlier and lower.
  9. The 2019 June Foundation report flagged that many candidates lose marks by failing to level off, or levelling off at the wrong height, when sketching comparison curves.
  10. Units of rate always come from the axes: cm³/s, cm³/min, g/s, or mol/dm³/s. Check the axis labels every time.
  11. Typical exam tariff is 2–3 marks: one for reading the graph, one for the calculation, one for the final answer with units.
  12. Rate-graph questions appear on Paper 2 of both 1CH0 (Separate) and 1SC0 (Combined), and very often follow on from core-practical data.
7.1.4.1 Catalysts F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A catalyst speeds up the rate of a reaction without altering the products, and is itself unchanged in mass and chemically at the end of the reaction.
  2. A catalyst is not a reactant — it does not appear in the overall balanced equation and is often written above the arrow.
  3. Catalysts do not change the amount of product made or the type of products; they only change how fast you get there.
  4. A catalyst works by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy.
  5. Because the activation energy is lower, a greater proportion of colliding particles have enough energy to react, so the frequency of successful collisions per second increases.
  6. The overall energy change (ΔH) of the reaction is unchanged by a catalyst — only the height of the activation-energy "hump" is reduced.
  7. On a reaction profile, the catalysed curve has the same start and end points as the uncatalysed curve but a lower peak.
  8. Enzymes are biological catalysts (usually proteins) that catalyse reactions in living organisms; yeast fermentation is a common GCSE example.
  9. Industrial catalysts you should recognise include iron (Haber process), vanadium(V) oxide (Contact process), and nickel (hydrogenation of oils).
  10. For a 1-mark recall question on the effect of a catalyst, "speeds up / increases the rate of reaction" is enough; for explanation marks, mention activation energy explicitly.
  11. For a "Devise" experiment showing a catalyst's mass is unchanged: weigh before, filter, wash and dry the catalyst at the end, then reweigh and compare.
  12. Never describe a catalyst as being "used up" or as "reacting" — that is the most common factual error on this spec point.
7.1.5.1 Enzymes F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An enzyme is a biological catalyst — a protein made by living cells that speeds up a specific reaction without being used up.
  2. Enzymes obey the full catalyst definition: they lower the activation energy, are not consumed, and do not change the products.
  3. The named example on the Edexcel spec is the use of enzymes in the production of alcoholic drinks, i.e. fermentation.
  4. Fermentation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide, catalysed by enzymes in yeast, in solution, without oxygen, at about 30–40 °C.
  5. The yeast is the source of the enzymes — saying "yeast catalyses the reaction" is loose; the chemistry mark is for identifying the enzymes within the yeast.
  6. Temperature must be high enough for a reasonable rate (collisions, energy above E_a) but low enough that the enzyme is not denatured.
  7. Denaturation is a permanent change in the enzyme's shape, especially the active site, caused by high temperatures (above roughly 45 °C). Cooling does not reverse it.
  8. The high-tariff exam question is "explain why 30 °C rather than 80 °C" — score by giving one rate point and one denaturing point, and by linking the enzymes back to the yeast.
  9. Common mistakes flagged in the June 2019 examiner report on 1CH0/2F: vague answers like "too hot," failing to mention yeast as the source of enzymes, and confusing carbohydrates (the sugar) with the yeast.
  10. Compared with industrial catalysts (e.g. iron in the Haber process), enzymes work in a much narrower temperature range and are destroyed by high temperatures rather than helped by them.
  11. The reaction's enthalpy change (ΔH) is not affected by the presence of an enzyme — the enzyme only changes the rate, not the energy of the products vs reactants.
  12. This spec point only appears on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H) — typically in the mid-to-late part of the paper, tied into a fermentation question.
7.2.1.1 Heat Energy & Temperature changes F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Heat energy changes accompany salts dissolving in water, neutralisation, displacement and precipitation reactions, and these can be measured in solution by the temperature change of the solution.
  2. ΔT = T(final) − T(initial); a positive ΔT means the temperature rose, a negative ΔT means it fell.
  3. A temperature rise of the solution means the reaction released heat to the surroundings — the reaction is exothermic.
  4. A temperature fall of the solution means the reaction absorbed heat from the surroundings — the reaction is endothermic.
  5. Dissolving salts can be either exothermic (e.g. calcium chloride) or endothermic (e.g. ammonium nitrate), because the lattice-breaking step and the ion-hydration step compete.
  6. Neutralisation, displacement and precipitation reactions are almost always exothermic in GCSE contexts — the solution gets warmer.
  7. When comparing several reactions from a table, the largest positive ΔT identifies the most exothermic; the largest negative ΔT identifies the most endothermic / largest amount of heat absorbed from the water.
  8. The most common examiner-flagged mistake is mixing up the terms exothermic and endothermic, especially saying that "energy is taken in" when the temperature has risen — avoid that phrasing entirely (1CH0/2F June 2019 Examiner Report).
  9. State your answer in terms of heat flow to or from the surroundings (the water of the solution), not vaguely "the reaction got hot".
  10. The water in the solution is the thermometer's reference — its high specific heat capacity is what makes the temperature change a reliable proxy for the reaction's heat change.
  11. Command words tied to this spec point are Calculate, Complete and Explain, and the SP appears on Paper 2 only.
  12. This spec point is the foundation for 7.2.1.2 Exothermic & Endothermic Reactions and 7.2.1.3 Calorimetry — get the sign convention right here and the rest of Topic 7.2 follows naturally.
7.2.1.2 Exothermic Reactions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An exothermic reaction is one in which heat energy is given out, transferring energy from the reacting chemicals to the surroundings.
  2. The clearest sign of an exothermic reaction is a rise in temperature of the surroundings (the solution in the beaker).
  3. ΔT = T_final − T_starting. A positive ΔT means exothermic; a negative ΔT means endothermic.
  4. The most common GCSE examples are combustion, neutralisation, reactive metal + acid, reactive metal + water, displacement and respiration.
  5. Everyday uses include hand warmers (iron oxidation), self-heating cans (CaO + H₂O), and burning fuels for heating.
  6. Energy is conserved: in an exothermic reaction, chemical energy stored in bonds is converted into heat energy in the surroundings.
  7. A common student misconception, flagged repeatedly by examiners, is to think that energy being "taken in" causes a temperature rise — the opposite is true: a temperature rise means energy is being given out by the reaction.
  8. To score the full 2 marks on an "explain" question, link three things: the observation (e.g. temperature rose by X °C), the direction of heat transfer (from reaction to surroundings), and the classification (exothermic).
  9. In a results-table question, the most exothermic reaction has the largest positive ΔT; an endothermic reaction will show a negative ΔT.
  10. Polystyrene cups are used in temperature-change practicals because they are good thermal insulators — they reduce heat loss to the surrounding air and give a more accurate measurement of ΔT.
  11. Exothermic-reaction questions appear mid-paper on Paper 2 (Foundation and Higher), with tariffs of 1 mark for naming and 2 marks for justifying.
  12. Always read the question carefully: "most exothermic" means largest temperature rise; "absorbed most heat energy" means largest temperature fall (endothermic).
7.2.1.3 Endothermic Reactions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An endothermic reaction is one in which heat energy is taken in from the surroundings.
  2. The temperature of the surroundings decreases during an endothermic reaction — the thermometer reading falls.
  3. You classify a reaction by what happens to the surroundings, not by what you imagine is happening inside the reaction mixture.
  4. Energy is needed to break bonds in the reactants; energy is released when product bonds form. In an endothermic reaction, more energy is taken in to break bonds than is released making bonds.
  5. Common endothermic examples to recognise: thermal decomposition of metal carbonates, dissolving ammonium nitrate (cold packs), citric acid + sodium hydrogen carbonate, and photosynthesis.
  6. If a reaction needs continuous heating to keep going, it is endothermic. If it carries on by itself once started, it is exothermic.
  7. On a reaction profile, the products sit higher than the reactants for an endothermic reaction — the gap represents the heat energy taken in.
  8. Temperature change = final temperature − starting temperature. A negative value means endothermic.
  9. For a 2-mark "explain" question, you need both a temperature reference ("the temperature decreased…") and a classification with reason ("…so the reaction is endothermic because heat is taken in").
  10. The dominant exam error flagged in the June 2019 Foundation report (1CH0/2F) is confusing endothermic with exothermic — students recognise the temperature change but then attach the wrong label. Memorise the pair as one unit: endothermic = in = colder.
  11. This spec point appears on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier, and across both Combined Science (1SC0) and Separate Chemistry (1CH0).
  12. Typical tariffs are 1 mark to name the reaction type and 2 marks to fully justify the classification using the data and energy flow.
7.2.2.1 Bonds & Energy Changes F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Bond breaking is endothermic — energy is absorbed from the surroundings because energy is needed to overcome the attraction between bonded atoms.
  2. Bond making is exothermic — energy is released to the surroundings because the new bonded arrangement is at a lower, more stable energy than the separated atoms.
  3. Every reaction involves both breaking reactant bonds and making product bonds — the overall heat change is the difference between the two energy totals.
  4. A reaction is exothermic if more energy is released forming product bonds than is absorbed breaking reactant bonds.
  5. A reaction is endothermic if less energy is released forming product bonds than is absorbed breaking reactant bonds.
  6. The discriminator in "explain" questions is comparing the two energy totals, not just describing both processes — examiners explicitly note this is where most candidates lose the third mark.
  7. Counting the number of bonds broken vs made is not the same as comparing the amounts of energy — what matters is the energy, not the count.
  8. Use precise terminology: energy absorbed, energy released, bond breaking, bond forming, exothermic, endothermic. Vague phrases like "uses up energy" lose marks.
  9. Combustion, neutralisation, and most oxidation reactions are exothermic because the bonds in the products (such as C=O in CO₂ and O–H in H₂O) release a lot of energy when they form.
  10. Thermal decomposition reactions (such as CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂) are endothermic because the strong bonds in the reactant absorb more energy to break than is released forming the product bonds — that is why these reactions need continuous heating.
  11. A typical Higher-tier "explain" question on this spec point is worth 3 marks and rewards one mark for bond breaking being endothermic, one for bond making being exothermic, and one for the comparison.
  12. This idea is the qualitative foundation for the Higher-tier numerical bond-energy calculations in spec point 7.2.3.1 — getting the directions right here is essential before tackling the maths.
7.2.3.1 Bond Energy Calculations H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Bond breaking is endothermic (energy in); bond making is exothermic (energy out).
  2. ΔH = (sum of bond energies of bonds broken) − (sum of bond energies of bonds made).
  3. A negative ΔH means the reaction is exothermic; a positive ΔH means endothermic.
  4. Always draw out the displayed (structural) formulae before counting bonds — molecular formulae hide individual bonds.
  5. Count every bond, multiplying by the number of moles of that molecule in the balanced equation (e.g. 2H₂O contains four O–H bonds, not two).
  6. The four marks on a typical Higher tier question correspond to: bonds-broken total, bonds-made total, subtraction, and correctly signed final answer.
  7. Bond energies in data tables are average values, so a calculated ΔH will be close to — but not identical to — the experimental value.
  8. The shortcut of cancelling unchanged bonds is mathematically valid but riskier; the full count is safer under exam pressure.
  9. The November 2021 examiner report flagged the missing minus sign as the single most common cause of lost marks — write the sign clearly.
  10. Lay out your working in clearly labelled lines (energy in = …, energy out = …, ΔH = …) so that error-carried-forward marks can still be awarded if you slip on arithmetic.
  11. This spec point is Higher tier only and appears on Paper 2 (1CH0/2H) — it is not assessed at Foundation tier.
  12. The command word is always "Calculate" — that means show your working, give units (kJ mol⁻¹), and give the correctly signed final answer.
7.2.4.1 Reaction Profiles F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A reaction profile plots energy (y-axis) against progress of reaction (x-axis), showing how the chemicals' energy changes from reactants to products.
  2. Every reaction profile has a "hump" in the middle — the peak represents the energy required to break bonds in the reactants before new bonds can form.
  3. Exothermic profiles have products lower than reactants; ΔH is negative and energy is released to the surroundings.
  4. Endothermic profiles have products higher than reactants; ΔH is positive and energy is taken in from the surroundings.
  5. The activation energy (Eₐ) is always measured from the reactants line up to the top of the curve, never from the products line — this is the most common error flagged by examiners.
  6. Even endothermic reactions have an activation-energy peak that sits above the products line — without the peak, the activation-energy arrow cannot be drawn.
  7. A reaction profile must be drawn as a curve with a peak, not a straight line — a straight line makes it impossible to mark in the activation energy.
  8. ΔH = energy of products − energy of reactants; if numerical values are given on the y-axis, you must be able to read them off and subtract.
  9. Required labels on every profile: axes, reactants, products, activation energy (Eₐ) arrow, and overall energy change (ΔH) arrow.
  10. Reaction profiles are a Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H) topic, typically worth 2–3 marks per question, with command words "draw and label" or "use words from the box".
  11. Common exothermic examples: combustion, neutralisation, displacement, respiration. Common endothermic examples: thermal decomposition of carbonates, dissolving ammonium chloride, photosynthesis.
  12. Read the question carefully for the direction of the reaction — if it tells you the temperature rose, the reaction is exothermic; if the temperature fell, it is endothermic.
7.2.4.2 Activation Energy F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Activation energy (Eₐ) is the minimum amount of energy that colliding reactant particles must have for a reaction to occur.
  2. Particles need this minimum energy because bonds in the reactants have to start breaking before new product bonds can form, and bond breaking takes in energy.
  3. A successful collision needs both enough energy (≥ Eₐ) and the correct orientation.
  4. On a reaction profile, Eₐ is measured from the reactant energy level up to the peak of the curve — not from the x-axis, and not from the products line.
  5. The vertical gap between reactants and products on the profile is the overall energy change (ΔH), not the activation energy — these are different quantities.
  6. For both exothermic and endothermic reactions, Eₐ is always positive and is always drawn from the reactant level up to the peak.
  7. A catalyst lowers the activation energy by providing an alternative pathway with a lower peak; the reactant and product energy levels (and so ΔH) are unchanged.
  8. At a higher temperature the value of Eₐ doesn't change, but more reactant particles have at least Eₐ, so more collisions are successful and the rate increases.
  9. The most common examiner-flagged error (1CH0/2H, November 2021) is measuring Eₐ from the wrong starting line — always start the Eₐ arrow on the reactant energy level.
  10. Drawing a straight line from reactants to products instead of a curve with a peak means there is no transition state shown, and the activation-energy mark cannot be awarded.
  11. Typical exam appearance on Paper 2 is either a 1–2 mark "explain" definition, or a 2-mark "draw and label" reaction-profile task (e.g. 1CH0/2H, June 2024, Q7(b)(ii)).
  12. When asked to "draw and label" Eₐ, use a double-headed vertical arrow from the reactant line up to the peak, labelled "Eₐ" or "activation energy".
