Fuels & Earth Science — Summary Sheet

8.1.1.1 Crude Oil F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only the elements carbon and hydrogen — the word "only" is what earns the mark.
  2. Crude oil is a complex mixture of many different hydrocarbons; it is not a single compound.
  3. Because crude oil is a mixture, the hydrocarbons in it keep their own properties and can be separated by physical means (boiling point) — this is what makes fractional distillation possible.
  4. The carbon atoms in crude-oil hydrocarbons are arranged in chains or rings; the names, formulae and structures of the ring molecules are not required at GCSE.
  5. Crude oil is an important source of fuels (petrol, diesel, kerosene, heavy fuel oil, LPG) and of feedstock for the petrochemical industry (plastics, solvents, detergents, lubricants, medicines).
  6. Crude oil is a finite (non-renewable) resource because it is being used up far faster than it is forming — it took hundreds of millions of years to form from the remains of ancient marine organisms.
  7. If asked for two uses of crude oil, give one fuel and one petrochemical product — two fuels will usually score only one mark.
  8. Common exam errors flagged by examiners include calling diesel "petrol for cars" and being imprecise about which fraction goes with which use (kerosene → aircraft is the safest pairing).
  9. This spec point sits on Paper 2 at both tiers and underpins the rest of Section 8 — fractional distillation, combustion, alkanes and cracking all build on the hydrocarbon definition.
  10. Typical command words are State, Define and Draw one straight line, with 1-mark recall tariffs the norm.
8.1.2.1 Fractional Distillation of Crude Oil F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Crude oil is a mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons with very different chain lengths, and it must be separated to be useful.
  2. Fractional distillation is a physical process that separates crude oil into fractions based on differences in boiling point — no chemical reactions take place.
  3. The crude oil is heated to around 350 °C so that almost all of it evaporates.
  4. The vapours enter a tall fractionating column that is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top — this temperature gradient is essential.
  5. Each hydrocarbon rises up the column until it reaches a level cooler than its boiling point, where it condenses and is collected as a liquid on a tray.
  6. Long-chain hydrocarbons have high boiling points because they have stronger intermolecular forces, so they condense low down in the column where it is still hot.
  7. Short-chain hydrocarbons have low boiling points and rise much higher before condensing; the very shortest leave the top as refinery gases.
  8. Bitumen, the longest-chain fraction, never evaporates and is tapped off as a thick liquid from the bottom.
  9. As chain length increases, viscosity increases, volatility decreases, and flammability decreases — these property trends explain why each fraction is used for a different job.
  10. Common exam tasks include labelling a column diagram, sequencing fractions by boiling point or chain length, and a 2–4 mark "describe and explain" of the process.
  11. The most common candidate error in this area is mixing up which fraction does which job — be specific: kerosene for aircraft, diesel for cars/lorries, petrol for cars. Do not write "petrol for cars" as a use of diesel.
  12. To score full marks on an "explain" question, you must link temperature gradient up the column to boiling point to chain length.
8.1.2.2 The Main Fractions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation into six main fractions you must be able to name and pair with their uses.
  2. Gases (refinery gases / LPG) are used for domestic heating and cooking — they are short-chain, very volatile, and burn cleanly.
  3. Petrol is used as fuel for cars — short-chain, highly volatile, ignites readily with a spark.
  4. Kerosene is used as fuel for aircraft — medium chain length, burns steadily, energy-dense.
  5. Diesel oil is used as fuel for some cars and trains — longer chains, less volatile than petrol, ignites under compression.
  6. Fuel oil is used as fuel for large ships and in some power stations — long chains, viscous, cheap, energy-dense.
  7. Bitumen is used to surface roads and roofs — very long chains, very viscous, not used as a fuel.
  8. The fractions sit on the fractionating column in the order (top → bottom): gases, petrol, kerosene, diesel oil, fuel oil, bitumen.
  9. As chain length increases down the column: boiling point rises, volatility falls, viscosity rises, flammability falls.
  10. The classic exam slip is describing diesel oil as "petrol for cars" — the 2019 Foundation examiner report flagged this as the most common error.
  11. Recall of fractions and uses is examined on almost every Paper 2, usually as a 1–2 mark "state the use" question or a matching MCQ.
  12. When answering a "state" question, name the fraction and use clearly; when answering a "draw one straight line" question, draw exactly one line per fraction with no crossings and no extras.
8.1.2.3 Trends in Properties F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Crude oil fractions are mostly alkanes with general formula C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+2</sub>.
  2. As you go down the fractionating column the hydrocarbons collected have more carbon atoms (and proportionally more hydrogen atoms) per molecule.
  3. Longer carbon chains have more electrons and a larger surface area, so intermolecular forces between molecules are stronger.
  4. Boiling point increases as chain length increases — more energy is needed to overcome stronger intermolecular forces.
  5. Ease of ignition (flammability) decreases as chain length increases — long-chain hydrocarbons vaporise less readily, so are harder to set alight.
  6. Viscosity increases as chain length increases — longer molecules attract each other and tangle, so the liquid flows less easily.
  7. The four trends (chain length, boiling point, ease of ignition, viscosity) all share one underlying explanation: stronger intermolecular forces in longer molecules.
  8. In an exam, an "explain" question on a trend needs both the direction of the trend and the reason (intermolecular forces, chain length).
  9. In a "compare two fractions" question, use both answer lines — one for each fraction — and do not repeat the same idea on both lines.
  10. The fraction at the top of the column has the lowest boiling point, shortest chains, lowest viscosity and is the easiest to ignite. The fraction at the bottom is the opposite.
  11. When working from a graph, check axis labels and units before reading values; be careful when extrapolating past the plotted range.
  12. Tariffs are usually 2 marks per trend explained; expect this SP on Paper 2 of either Combined Science or Separate Chemistry.
8.1.3.1 Homologous Series F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A homologous series is a family of organic compounds that share the same general formula, differ by CH₂ between neighbours, show a gradual change in physical properties, and have similar chemical properties.
  2. The four defining features (same general formula, differ by CH₂, gradual change in physical properties, similar chemical properties) come straight from spec point 8.6 — learn all four.
  3. The general formula of the alkanes is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. You can use it to deduce the molecular formula of any alkane.
  4. Neighbouring members of a homologous series differ by exactly one carbon and two hydrogen atoms — one CH₂ unit.
  5. The first six alkanes are methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈), butane (C₄H₁₀), pentane (C₅H₁₂), and hexane (C₆H₁₄). Methane to butane are gases; pentane and above are liquids at room temperature.
  6. Boiling point rises gradually as you go up the alkane series because larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces (more electrons → stronger induced dipole attractions).
  7. Boiling involves overcoming intermolecular forces between molecules, not breaking covalent C–C or C–H bonds inside the molecule.
  8. Members of a homologous series have similar chemical reactions because they share the same functional group or saturated hydrocarbon framework — e.g. all alkanes combust similarly.
  9. In a 2-mark "Explain…" question on Paper 2, the marks are usually 1 for same general formula and 1 for differ by CH₂ (or equivalent paired reasoning).
  10. The June 2019 1CH0/2F examiner report flagged that candidates lose marks by repeating the molecular formulae from the stem instead of comparing them — make the comparison explicit.
  11. Use the general formula to deduce unfamiliar molecular formulae rather than memorising them: for the nth alkane, hydrogens = 2n + 2.
  12. The "differ by CH₂" rule is a direct consequence of all members sharing the same general formula — it is one chemical idea, written two ways in the spec.
8.1.4.1 Complete Combustion F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Complete combustion is the reaction of a hydrocarbon with a plentiful supply of oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water as the only carbon- and hydrogen-containing products.
  2. Complete combustion is exothermic — energy is given out to the surroundings, which is why hydrocarbons are useful as fuels.
  3. The general word equation is: hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
  4. Methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
  5. Butane: 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O — double everything to clear the "6.5 O₂" problem.
  6. Ethene: C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O — even though it is an alkene, the products of complete combustion are still CO₂ and H₂O.
  7. Always write oxygen as O₂, never as a single O atom (this loses marks in symbol-equation questions).
  8. In a word equation, the reactant is oxygen, never "air".
  9. Do not mix words and formulae in the same equation — pick one or the other.
  10. A clean blue flame signals complete combustion; a yellow sooty flame signals incomplete combustion (covered in 8.1.4.2 Incomplete Combustion).
  11. The "describe complete combustion" answer must include all three points: CO₂ formed, H₂O formed, energy released.
  12. Energy is released because the bonds made in CO₂ and H₂O are stronger than the bonds broken in the hydrocarbon and oxygen.
8.1.4.2 Incomplete Combustion F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Incomplete combustion happens when a hydrocarbon burns in a limited supply of oxygen.
  2. The carbon in the fuel may be only partly oxidised (to carbon monoxide, CO) or not oxidised at all (to solid carbon / soot, C); the hydrogen is still oxidised to water in both cases.
  3. Balanced equations for incomplete combustion follow the same rules as any other equation — balance C first, then H, then O₂ last. Oxygen is always O₂, never O.
  4. Two parallel pathways exist: the CO pathway (e.g. 2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O) and the C (soot) pathway (e.g. CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O).
  5. Carbon monoxide is toxic because it binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells in place of oxygen, so the blood can no longer carry enough oxygen to the body's cells.
  6. CO is colourless and odourless, so it gives no warning — hence CO alarms in homes with gas boilers and wood burners.
  7. Soot deposited by incomplete combustion blocks gas flow, insulates heat exchangers (reducing efficiency), and builds up in chimneys where it can ignite.
  8. Incomplete combustion also releases less energy per mole of fuel than complete combustion — making the appliance wasteful as well as dangerous.
  9. A clean blue flame indicates complete combustion; a yellow, smoky flame indicates incomplete combustion (the yellow colour is glowing soot particles).
  10. The standard 2-mark "CO toxicity" answer needs two points: (i) CO binds to haemoglobin (in place of oxygen), and (ii) less oxygen is carried by the blood to the body's tissues.
  11. Examiner reports stress that "explain" questions about combustion observations need a reason, not a description — link what you see (yellow flame, soot) to insufficient oxygen / partial oxidation.
  12. A common error highlighted by examiners is writing oxygen as "O" instead of "O₂" — always use the diatomic molecule in combustion equations.
8.1.5.1 Formation of Sulfur Dioxide F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Hydrocarbon fuels (petrol, diesel, fuel oil, coal, natural gas) are mainly compounds of carbon and hydrogen, but some contain sulfur as an impurity trapped in the original fossil material.
  2. When the fuel is burned, the sulfur impurities react with oxygen at the same time as the hydrocarbons burn.
  3. The equation for the combustion of sulfur is: S + O₂ → SO₂.
  4. Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) is an acidic oxide — it dissolves in atmospheric water to form sulfurous acid (and after further oxidation, sulfuric acid).
  5. SO₂ is one of the two main acidic gases responsible for acid rain (the other being oxides of nitrogen).
  6. SO₂ comes from impurities in the fuel, whereas NOₓ comes from the nitrogen and oxygen in the air reacting at the high temperatures inside an engine — do not mix these up in an extended answer.
  7. Different fuels contain different amounts of sulfur; coal and heavy fuel oils typically contain more sulfur than petrol, diesel or natural gas.
  8. Sulfur is removed from petrol and diesel at the refinery (desulfurisation) so that less SO₂ is released when the fuel is burned.
  9. For coal-fired power stations, the SO₂ is removed from flue gases using calcium-based scrubbers (flue gas desulfurisation).
  10. A short exam answer should mention the source (sulfur impurities in the fuel) and the product (sulfur dioxide); for 2 marks, add the equation or the words "reacts with oxygen on combustion".
  11. In extended 6-mark "discuss" questions on the environmental impact of fuels, link SO₂ explicitly to acid rain and pair each pollutant with its consequence (CO₂ → global warming, CO → toxic, particulates → respiratory damage, NOₓ → acid rain).
  12. The spec point is examined on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H) and on Combined Science (1SC0) Paper 2 — Foundation and Higher candidates need to know it.
8.1.5.2 Acid Rain F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Acid rain is rain with a pH significantly below the natural value of around 5.6, caused by acidic pollutant gases dissolving in atmospheric water droplets.
  2. The two main acid-rain pollutants on the Edexcel GCSE spec are sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ).
  3. SO₂ is produced when sulfur impurities in fossil fuels (coal, oil, diesel) are burned — S + O₂ → SO₂.
  4. NOₓ is produced when nitrogen and oxygen from the air react at the high temperatures inside engines — N₂ + O₂ → 2NO.
  5. SO₂ dissolves in rain water to form sulfurous acid (SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃) and is also oxidised to give sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). NO₂ reacts with water and oxygen to form nitric acid (HNO₃).
  6. Acid rain damages trees by attacking leaves directly and by leaching essential nutrient ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺) from the soil while releasing toxic Al³⁺.
  7. Acid rain lowers the pH of lakes and rivers, killing fish (eggs fail to hatch below ~pH 5) and entire aquatic ecosystems.
  8. Acid rain reacts with limestone and marble buildings and statues (CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂), eroding their surfaces irreversibly.
  9. Acid rain corrodes metal structures such as iron bridges, railings and ships, by reacting with the metal to form soluble metal salts (e.g. Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂).
  10. Acid rain lowers soil pH, reduces crop yields, and can be partly counteracted by liming with calcium carbonate or calcium hydroxide.
  11. In Edexcel extended-answer questions on acid rain, full marks require formation chemistry + at least three distinct harms with chemistry-based explanations — vague statements about "damaging the environment" will not score.
  12. Acid rain is examined on Paper 2 (Sections 8/9), typically as an extended 6-mark "Explain" or "Discuss" question, and is frequently set alongside other combustion pollutants (CO₂, CO).
8.1.5.3 Formation of Nitrogen Oxides F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) are pollutant gases — mainly nitrogen monoxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) — formed inside hot engines when fuels are burned.
  2. The nitrogen comes from the air drawn into the engine, not from the fuel. Hydrocarbon fuels contain only carbon and hydrogen.
  3. At normal atmospheric temperatures, N₂ and O₂ do not react because the N≡N triple bond is very strong, giving the reaction a high activation energy.
  4. Inside an engine combustion chamber, the temperature is high enough to give N₂ and O₂ molecules enough energy to react: N₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2NO (g).
  5. Once the exhaust gases mix with cooler air outside, NO reacts further with oxygen: 2NO (g) + O₂ (g) → 2NO₂ (g).
  6. Nitrogen oxides dissolve in atmospheric water to form acids, which fall as acid rain.
  7. Acid rain damages aquatic ecosystems, kills trees, erodes limestone and marble buildings, and lowers soil pH.
  8. NO₂ also irritates the lungs and contributes to photochemical smog in cities.
  9. A full-credit "Explain" answer must link the high temperature in the engine to overcoming the activation energy for N₂ and O₂ to react.
  10. In extended 6-mark "Discuss" questions on vehicle emissions, you must name NOₓ as a specific pollutant and link it to acid rain — not to global warming (that is CO₂) or to toxicity (that is CO).
  11. Diesel engines tend to produce more NOₓ than petrol engines because they run at higher temperatures and pressures.
  12. This SP is examined on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H), typically as a 2-mark "Explain" question or as one strand of a 6-mark "Discuss" question on vehicle emissions.
8.1.6.1 Hydrogen as a Fuel F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Hydrogen burns in oxygen to produce only water: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O — no CO₂, no CO, no soot, no SO₂.
  2. Petrol is a mixture of hydrocarbons; even complete combustion produces CO₂, and real engines also produce CO, soot, unburnt hydrocarbons and NOₓ.
  3. Hydrogen does not occur naturally as H₂ on Earth and must be manufactured — usually by electrolysis of water or by steam reforming of methane.
  4. Electrolysis with renewable electricity gives genuinely low-carbon hydrogen; electrolysis with fossil-fuel electricity, or steam reforming of methane, does not — the CO₂ is just moved upstream.
  5. Hydrogen has a much higher energy density per kilogram than petrol, but a much lower energy density per litre — so storage tanks are bulky, heavy and expensive.
  6. Hydrogen must be stored compressed to very high pressure or cooled to −253 °C as a liquid; petrol is a liquid at room temperature and stores easily.
  7. The UK has tens of thousands of petrol stations but only a handful of hydrogen filling stations — the refuelling infrastructure for hydrogen barely exists yet.
  8. Petrol comes from crude oil, which is non-renewable; hydrogen can be renewable if made by electrolysis with renewable electricity.
  9. Hydrogen cars are currently more expensive to buy and to run per mile than equivalent petrol cars, partly because they are produced in small numbers.
  10. Spec 8.14 uses the command word evaluate — you must give advantages, disadvantages and a comparative judgement, not a one-sided list.
  11. On 1CH0 and 1SC0, this spec point is examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
  12. Most hydrogen exam questions in 1CH0 sit under Topic 5 (Fuel Cells); combustion-of-hydrogen questions specifically belong to this spec point.
8.1.6.2 Fossil Fuels F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Petrol, kerosene and diesel oil are non-renewable fossil fuels obtained from crude oil; methane is a non-renewable fossil fuel found in natural gas.
  2. A fossil fuel is a fuel formed over millions of years from the remains of dead organisms buried under sediment in the absence of oxygen.
  3. Non-renewable means the resource is being used up much faster than it is being replaced and cannot be replenished on any useful human timescale.
  4. Crude oil is a finite mixture of hydrocarbons; petrol, kerosene and diesel oil are three of its fractions, separated by fractional distillation.
  5. Natural gas is a fossil fuel mostly made up of methane (CH₄), often found in underground reservoirs above or alongside crude oil.
  6. Petrol, kerosene and diesel are liquid mixtures of hydrocarbons of increasing chain length; methane is a single gaseous compound.
  7. The most common Paper 2 questions on this SP are 1-mark "State" or "Identify" parts asking you to match a fuel to its source, or to give an example of a fossil fuel.
  8. When asked why a fuel is non-renewable, link the answer to rate of use vs rate of formation — not to pollution.
  9. All four fuels release energy by burning in oxygen; complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water, while incomplete combustion (limited oxygen) can also produce carbon monoxide and soot.
  10. Use the exact spec-point names — petrol, kerosene, diesel oil, methane — to avoid losing easy marks.
8.1.7.1 Cracking F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Cracking breaks down larger, saturated alkane molecules into smaller, more useful hydrocarbons — at least one of which is an unsaturated alkene.
  2. Cracking is necessary because fractional distillation produces too much of the long-chain fractions and not enough short-chain fuels and alkenes to match consumer demand.
  3. The reactant in cracking is always a saturated alkane (general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂); the products are a shorter alkane plus one or more alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ), and sometimes hydrogen.
  4. Conditions for cracking are high temperature (around 600–700 °C) and a catalyst such as aluminium oxide or silica — this is catalytic cracking and is a type of thermal decomposition.
  5. The total number of carbon atoms and hydrogen atoms is conserved on each side of a cracking equation — use this to balance equations or deduce unknown products.
  6. A typical example: C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + 2 C₂H₄ (dodecane cracks into octane plus two molecules of ethene).
  7. Alkenes are valuable because they have a reactive C=C double bond and are used as monomers to make polymers (e.g. ethene → poly(ethene)).
  8. Alkanes and alkenes can be distinguished using bromine water — alkenes decolourise the orange solution, alkanes do not.
  9. Mass is conserved in cracking: the total mass of products equals the mass of the reactant. Use moles = mass ÷ Mr and the molar ratio from the balanced equation to calculate product masses.
  10. To deduce an unknown cracking product, subtract the carbons and hydrogens in the known products from those in the reactant — the remainder gives the formula of the unknown.
  11. Keep cracking, fractional distillation and combustion clearly separated — examiners noted in 1CH0/2H November 2021 that candidates confused cracking with the products of combustion.
  12. Typical exam command words are Calculate, Determine, Deduce, Explain and Complete, usually appearing in 1–4 mark questions in the middle of Paper 2.
8.2.1.1 Earth's Early Atmosphere F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The Earth's early atmosphere is thought to have formed about 4.5 billion years ago from gases released by intense volcanic activity.
  2. The early atmosphere contained a large amount of carbon dioxide, plenty of water vapour, little or no oxygen, and small amounts of other gases (including nitrogen, methane, and ammonia).
  3. There was little or no oxygen in the early atmosphere because photosynthesis had not yet evolved — early Earth had no plants, algae, or cyanobacteria.
  4. As Earth cooled, the surface temperature dropped below 100 °C, and atmospheric water vapour condensed into liquid water: H₂O (g) → H₂O (l).
  5. This condensed water fell as rain over millions of years and collected in surface basins to form the first oceans.
  6. Once oceans existed, large amounts of atmospheric CO₂ dissolved in the seawater, beginning the long-term reduction of atmospheric CO₂.
  7. Scientists use the word "thought to" when describing the early atmosphere because evidence is indirect — from modern volcanic gases, rocks, and fossils — not direct measurement.
  8. When asked to suggest features of the early atmosphere from data, always link your answer to the evidence provided in the question.
  9. Do not confuse the early atmosphere (CO₂-rich, no oxygen) with today's atmosphere (78% N₂, 21% O₂, 0.04% CO₂).
  10. Comparison symbols like >> and << are useful shorthand for comparing CO₂ and O₂ levels between the early and modern atmospheres, but full sentences should still be used in long-answer responses.
8.2.1.2 How Carbon Dioxide Decreased F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Earth's early atmosphere — formed by volcanic activity — was dominated by carbon dioxide and water vapour, with little or no oxygen.
  2. As the Earth cooled below about 100 °C, water vapour condensed and rain filled low-lying regions to form the oceans.
  3. Once the oceans existed, carbon dioxide dissolved in the ocean water because it is moderately soluble.
  4. Dissolved CO₂ reacted with water to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) and produced carbonate ions in solution.
  5. Carbonate ions reacted with metal ions such as Ca²⁺ to form insoluble metal carbonates (e.g. CaCO₃, calcium carbonate).
  6. These precipitates settled on the ocean floor and, over millions of years, were compacted into sedimentary rocks such as limestone and chalk — locking the carbon away from the atmosphere.
  7. Marine organisms also built shells of CaCO₃, which fell to the sea floor on death and contributed to the same sedimentary rocks.
  8. Some of the carbon dioxide was removed by photosynthesis and the resulting organic matter was buried before it could decay, forming fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas).
  9. The combined effect of dissolving, carbonate formation and fossil-fuel burial reduced atmospheric CO₂ from being the dominant gas to a trace gas (~0.04%, ~400 ppm) today.
  10. Nitrogen (N₂) is largely unreactive and poorly soluble, which is why it accumulated and is now the most abundant atmospheric gas (~78%).
  11. A full-mark "Explain how CO₂ decreased" answer must include cooling → oceans form → CO₂ dissolves → carbonates/fossil fuels form. Stopping at "CO₂ dissolved" is the most common partial-credit answer.
  12. This SP is frequently combined with 8.2.1.3 (oxygen increase by photosynthesis) in 6-mark extended-response questions on the evolution of the atmosphere.
8.2.1.3 How Oxygen Increased F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Photosynthesis is the process that increased the amount of oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.
  2. The balanced symbol equation is: 6CO₂ (g) + 6H₂O (l) → C₆H₁₂O₆ (aq) + 6O₂ (g).
  3. The word equation is: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen.
  4. The same reaction that releases O₂ also removes CO₂ — both changes happen together, so a full answer must mention both gases.
  5. The first photosynthesisers were algae in the oceans, which evolved around 2.7 billion years ago.
  6. Land plants evolved later and accelerated the rise of oxygen as they spread across continents.
  7. The rise in oxygen was gradual, taking around 2 billion years to reach roughly modern levels.
  8. Today's atmosphere is approximately 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and 0.04% carbon dioxide.
  9. Other processes (CO₂ dissolving in oceans, formation of carbonate rocks and fossil fuels) also lowered CO₂ — but this spec point focuses on photosynthesis.
  10. Explain questions are typically worth 4–6 marks; a Level 3 answer must link photosynthesis to both the rise in O₂ and the fall in CO₂.
  11. On 6-mark data-table questions, identify the gas trends from the data first, then explain each one using photosynthesis.
  12. Avoid the trap of writing "plants made oxygen" without explaining the reaction — name the process, give the equation, and explain the consequence for the atmosphere.
8.2.1.4 The Test for Oxygen F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The test for oxygen uses a glowing splint (a smouldering wooden splint, not a lit/flaming one).
  2. The positive result is that the glowing splint relights.
  3. Both halves — apparatus (glowing splint) and observation (relights) — are required for full marks on a 2-mark "Describe" question.
  4. The reason a glowing splint relights is that pure oxygen has a much higher concentration of O₂ than air, which accelerates the combustion of the smouldering wood enough to reignite a flame.
  5. Do not confuse the oxygen test with the hydrogen test: hydrogen uses a lit splint and gives a squeaky pop, not a relight.
  6. The four core GCSE gas tests are: oxygen (glowing splint → relights), hydrogen (lit splint → squeaky pop), carbon dioxide (limewater → milky), and chlorine (damp litmus → bleaches white).
  7. Examiners at Foundation tier have flagged that the majority of candidates were unable to recall the test for oxygen in 1CH0/2F June 2019 — treat this as easy marks only if you have memorised it precisely.
  8. Use the exact word "relights" (or "rekindles") — vague descriptions such as "burns brighter" or "catches fire" may not score.
  9. The oxygen test appears on Paper 2 only (Topic 8 content) and applies to both Foundation and Higher tier, Separate Chemistry and Combined Science.
  10. Common practical contexts where the oxygen test is needed: hydrogen peroxide decomposition, electrolysis of water/dilute sulfuric acid at the anode, and photosynthesis experiments with pondweed.
  11. The test cannot distinguish pure O₂ from any oxygen-rich mixture — any gas significantly above ~21% O₂ will relight a glowing splint.
  12. When writing the answer under exam conditions, name the glowing splint first, then state that it relights — this matches the order of marking points in Edexcel mark schemes.
8.2.2.1 Greenhouse Gases F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The greenhouse effect is the natural process by which gases in the atmosphere absorb heat radiated from the Earth and release energy that keeps the Earth warm.
  2. The three greenhouse gases you must know are carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and water vapour (H₂O) — all named in spec point 8.24.
  3. Greenhouse gases absorb infrared radiation emitted by the Earth's surface, not visible light from the Sun — the spec text says "heat radiated from the Earth".
  4. After absorbing infrared, greenhouse gases re-emit energy in all directions, including back to the Earth's surface — this is what keeps the Earth warm.
  5. Nitrogen and oxygen — the most abundant gases in the atmosphere — are not greenhouse gases, because their diatomic molecules do not absorb infrared at the relevant wavelengths.
  6. The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary: without it, average surface temperatures would be far below 0 °C. Do not confuse it with the enhanced greenhouse effect (human-caused).
  7. Methane is present in much smaller amounts than CO₂ but is a stronger greenhouse gas per molecule, which is why livestock, landfill and rice agriculture are climate concerns.
  8. Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas, but its concentration is controlled by temperature, not by direct human emissions.
  9. A correct exam answer should always include: Sun → Earth absorbs → Earth re-radiates as infrared → greenhouse gases absorb → greenhouse gases re-emit back to Earth → Earth kept warm.
  10. Common mark-losing errors: saying greenhouse gases "trap sunlight", saying they "reflect" heat, or confusing the greenhouse effect with the ozone layer or with global warming.
  11. This SP is examined on Paper 2 on both Foundation and Higher tier and frequently combines with later SPs in extended-response questions about human impact and climate change.
8.2.3.1 Evaluating the Evidence F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Correlation is not the same as causation — two variables changing together does not prove one causes the other; a physical mechanism is also needed.
  2. Atmospheric CO₂, fossil-fuel consumption, and global mean temperature all show a strong positive correlation since the Industrial Revolution.
  3. The greenhouse effect provides the mechanism: CO₂ absorbs infrared radiation re-emitted from Earth's surface, warming the lower atmosphere.
  4. Historical CO₂ data comes from ice cores, which trap tiny air bubbles preserving samples of the atmosphere going back over 800,000 years.
  5. Historical temperatures are reconstructed from proxy data — tree rings, isotope ratios in ice, ocean sediments, and pollen records.
  6. The Mauna Loa record (since 1958) is the gold-standard direct measurement of atmospheric CO₂; its remote, high-altitude location avoids local pollution and gives a global background value.
  7. CO₂ has risen from ~280 ppm pre-industrial to over 420 ppm today, a rate of around 1.6 ppm per year — roughly 800 times faster than the natural pre-industrial rate from ice cores.
  8. Location uncertainty — CO₂ and temperature vary across the globe; a single station cannot represent the planet, so a global network is needed.
  9. Historical accuracy uncertainty — older data depends on proxies and decades-averaged ice-core bubbles; older thermometer records are affected by station changes and the urban heat island effect.
  10. Uncertainty does not mean the trend is unknown — even at the wide end of every uncertainty range, CO₂ and temperature are rising and the rise is unprecedented.
  11. When given a CO₂-vs-temperature graph, describe both trends with values, state the correlation type, and note any anomalies (e.g. short-term volcanic cooling).
  12. For an evaluate question, give the evidence for human-caused change, acknowledge the uncertainties, and reach a justified conclusion — answers that only describe or only dismiss will not score full marks.
8.2.3.2 Human Activity & Climate Change F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Human activity is releasing extra carbon dioxide (mainly from burning fossil fuels and deforestation) and extra methane (mainly from livestock farming, landfill and rice paddies) into the atmosphere.
  2. These gases absorb infrared radiation emitted by the Earth, trapping more heat in the lower atmosphere — this is the enhanced greenhouse effect that drives climate change.
  3. Potential effects include rising global temperatures, melting polar ice and glaciers, rising sea levels (from meltwater and thermal expansion), more extreme weather, ecosystem disruption and shifts in food production.
  4. The Edexcel spec uses the word "potential" because climate models predict ranges of outcomes — answers should talk about risk and likelihood, not certainty.
  5. Mitigation strategies include renewable energy, nuclear power, energy efficiency, electrifying transport, carbon capture and storage, afforestation, dietary change (less meat and dairy) and better waste management.
  6. For spec 8.26b, evaluate every mitigation strategy against three criteria — scale, risk, and environmental implications.
  7. No single strategy is enough; a realistic response combines decarbonising electricity, cutting methane, capturing remaining CO₂ and protecting/expanding carbon sinks like forests.
  8. Methane is a more powerful greenhouse gas per molecule than CO₂, but CO₂ is released in much larger quantities, so its overall contribution to warming is greater.
  9. Common errors to avoid: confusing the greenhouse effect with ozone depletion, writing "global warming" when "climate change" is asked for, and ignoring methane or livestock farming when describing human sources.
  10. The mitigation half of the spec is most often tested with Evaluate, Suggest or Explain command words — strong answers acknowledge trade-offs rather than presenting any single solution as a complete fix.
