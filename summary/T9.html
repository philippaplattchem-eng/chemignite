Separate Chemistry 2 — Summary Sheet

9.1.1.1 Flame Tests F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A flame test identifies a metal cation in a solid sample by heating it in a Bunsen flame and observing the colour produced.
  2. The five flame colours you must learn from the spec are: Li⁺ red, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ orange-red, Cu²⁺ blue-green.
  3. The test for any ion must be unique — otherwise two different ions could give the same result and could not be distinguished.
  4. Procedure: clean a nichrome or platinum wire by dipping it in concentrated HCl and heating it in a Bunsen until the flame shows no colour; then dip in solid sample and hold in a non-luminous (blue) Bunsen flame.
  5. Nichrome and platinum are used because they are unreactive and do not colour the flame themselves.
  6. Concentrated HCl cleans the wire by converting residues to volatile chlorides; it also makes the test ion's chloride volatile, giving a stronger colour.
  7. The Bunsen flame must be non-luminous (blue) — a yellow luminous flame would mask the test colour and is cooler.
  8. Flame tests work because the metal ion's electrons are excited to higher energy levels by the heat of the flame, then release energy as light of a characteristic colour when they fall back down.
  9. Common confusion at exam: Li⁺ (red) and Ca²⁺ (orange-red) can look similar — read the spec wording carefully and use a follow-up test (e.g. NaOH (aq)) if needed.
  10. Flame tests detect cations only, not anions; modern labs use flame emission spectroscopy for greater accuracy and to detect mixtures.
  11. From examiner feedback, the most common errors are (a) not learning the colours, (b) confusing cation tests with anion tests, and (c) describing the reactions of alkali metals with water instead of the flame test.
  12. Flame tests are a core practical for Separate Chemistry — Paper 2 questions can ask you to describe the procedure, match ions to colours, or identify an unknown solid from a set of test observations.
9.1.2.1 Tests for Cations F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. One reagent, six cations: sodium hydroxide solution, NaOH (aq), is the single test reagent for Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺ and NH₄⁺.
  2. Five metal cations form insoluble metal hydroxide precipitates with NaOH (aq); only the ammonium ion does not give a precipitate.
  3. Coloured precipitates: Cu²⁺ → blue, Fe²⁺ → green (turns brown on standing in air), Fe³⁺ → orange-brown.
  4. White precipitates: Al³⁺ and Ca²⁺ both give white precipitates — distinguish using excess NaOH: Al(OH)₃ dissolves to give a colourless solution (it is amphoteric); Ca(OH)₂ does not dissolve.
  5. Ammonium test: add NaOH and warm gently; ammonia gas is released, which turns damp red litmus paper blue.
  6. Ionic equations for metal hydroxide precipitations follow the pattern M^n+ + n OH⁻ → M(OH)ₙ — match the number of hydroxides to the charge on the cation.
  7. For NH₄⁺ specifically: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O — and the NH₃ must escape as gas, which is why warming is essential.
  8. Ammonium vs aluminium is a known examiner trap (per the 1CH0/2H June 2019 Examiner Report). Aluminium gives a white precipitate soluble in excess NaOH; ammonium gives no precipitate at all but releases ammonia gas.
  9. Damp red litmus, not dry — ammonia must dissolve in the water on the paper to act as an alkali and turn it blue.
  10. Justify your answer in extended-response identification questions: state the observation, name the cation, and (on Higher tier) write the ionic equation. This was the single biggest mark-loser in the 1CH0/2F June 2019 6-mark question.
  11. Core practical: examiners expect first-hand familiarity with the test procedure — this is one of the practicals that gets repeatedly assessed in Paper 2.
  12. Flame tests can supplement the NaOH test (especially for Ca²⁺ → brick-red flame, Cu²⁺ → blue-green flame), but NaOH precipitation is the spec-required core test for 9.3C.
9.1.3.1 Testing for Ammonia F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The test for ammonia gas is: hold damp red litmus paper in the mouth of the tube; if NH₃ is present, the paper turns blue.
  2. The paper must be damp because litmus only changes colour when the substance being tested can dissolve in the water film on the paper.
  3. The colour change happens because NH₃ is a weak base — it dissolves in the water on the paper, forming NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ ions and raising the pH above 7.
  4. Ammonia is the only common gas at GCSE that turns damp red litmus paper blue — this makes the test diagnostic.
  5. To test for the ammonium ion (NH₄⁺), add sodium hydroxide solution, warm gently, then hold damp red litmus paper in the mouth of the tube — if it turns blue, NH₄⁺ was present.
  6. The ionic equation for the reaction generating ammonia is: NH₄⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → NH₃ (g) + H₂O (l). Warming is needed to drive off the gas.
  7. Ammonium (NH₄⁺) and aluminium (Al³⁺) both give white precipitates with NaOH — the ammonia/warm/litmus test is the confirmatory test that distinguishes them.
  8. In Paper 2 Higher, this content typically appears inside a 5–6 mark qualitative analysis question; the June 2019 paper highlighted ammonium/aluminium confusion as a common error.
  9. Use the command word as a guide: "State" wants the reagent and result (1–2 marks); "Describe" wants the full procedure including warming (2–3 marks).
  10. The most common missed marks are: forgetting "damp", forgetting to warm with NaOH, and writing "red litmus paper" without specifying the colour change red → blue.
9.1.4.1 Tests for Anions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The carbonate test is: add dilute acid → effervescence → pass gas through limewater → cloudy/milky confirms CO₂.
  2. The sulfate test is: acidify with dilute HCl, then add barium chloride → white precipitate of BaSO₄ confirms sulfate. Ionic equation: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄.
  3. The halide test is: acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add silver nitrate → coloured precipitate confirms the halide.
  4. The silver halide colour ladder: AgCl is white, AgBr is cream, AgI is yellow — learn these descriptors exactly; "off-white" and "pale yellow" for bromide are not accepted.
  5. Acidification removes carbonate — without it, carbonate would form a white precipitate with Ba²⁺ or Ag⁺ and give a false positive.
  6. Use HCl for the sulfate test (chloride doesn't interfere with Ba²⁺), and HNO₃ for the halide test (nitrate doesn't interfere with Ag⁺).
  7. Never use H₂SO₄ for the sulfate test (it contains sulfate) and never use HCl for the halide test (it contains chloride — false positive).
  8. For the carbonate test, effervescence alone is not enough — you must include the limewater step to confirm the gas is CO₂.
  9. Use a fresh sample for each anion test to avoid residues from earlier tests interfering with results.
  10. Anion tests usually appear in 6-mark identification questions on Paper 2, paired with cation tests — read carefully so you answer the correct half of the question.
  11. The ionic equation for the carbonate-acid reaction is: CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂.
  12. Examiners specifically flagged the acidification step in the sulfate test as the part candidates most often forget to mention when justifying their identification.
9.1.5.1 Core Practical: Identifying Ions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. This Core Practical synthesises the four ion-test SPs (9.1.1.1 flame tests, 9.1.2.1 NaOH for cations, 9.1.3.1 halide/sulfate/carbonate tests, 9.1.4.1 ammonium test) into one systematic procedure.
  2. The standard order of tests is: flame test → NaOH for cations → warm-with-NaOH for ammonium → anion tests on fresh sample portions.
  3. Always use a fresh sample of the unknown solution for each wet test — adding BaCl₂ contaminates the mixture with chloride, and adding AgNO₃ would then give a false positive.
  4. HCl is used to acidify the sulfate test (it cannot use H₂SO₄, which would itself provide SO₄²⁻ ions); HNO₃ is used to acidify the halide test (HCl would add Cl⁻; H₂SO₄ would oxidise Br⁻ and I⁻).
  5. Ammonium vs aluminium — both can appear to give a white precipitate or no precipitate with NaOH; distinguish by warming and testing the gas with damp red litmus paper (ammonia turns it blue; aluminium gives no gas).
  6. Halide mixtures are resolved by dissolving the silver halide precipitate in ammonia: AgCl dissolves in dilute ammonia; AgBr dissolves only in concentrated ammonia; AgI does not dissolve in concentrated ammonia.
  7. Silver nitrate is the most hazardous reagent in this practical — it stains skin and clothing dark brown on contact, so wear gloves and a lab coat.
  8. The exam appearance is consistently a 6-mark extended-response question on Paper 2 in which a table of test results must be interpreted to identify two or three unknown ionic substances — 1CH0/2H June 2024 Q8(c) and 1CH0/2H June 2019 are the canonical past-paper examples.
  9. Justify identifications with observations — every named ion must be tied to a specific row of the table (e.g. "blue precipitate with NaOH → Cu²⁺"), and at Higher tier balanced ionic equations are expected.
  10. The June 2019 1CH0/2F examiner report flagged that this Core Practical causes great difficulty for candidates despite being a Core Practical — so preparing the test results, colours and reagent choices thoroughly is a high-value revision priority.
  11. Cross-checking improves reliability — when flame test and NaOH test agree (e.g. both indicate Cu²⁺), state the agreement to score the communication mark on a 6-mark question.
  12. The flame test is the quick, non-destructive screen that should always go first — it uses a tiny scrape of solid and does not contaminate the sample for any later wet test.
9.1.6.1 Advantages of Instrumental Methods F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Instrumental methods of analysis use a machine to detect or measure a substance, replacing tests that rely on the human eye observing a colour, precipitate or flame.
  2. The three named advantages of instrumental methods on the Edexcel GCSE specification are sensitivity, accuracy and speed — learn all three by name.
  3. Sensitivity: instruments can detect substances at much lower concentrations (e.g. parts per million) than traditional tests, which need enough substance to give a visible response.
  4. Accuracy: instruments give numerical, calibrated readings, so the result does not depend on the observer's judgement of colour and is reproducible.
  5. Speed: instruments analyse samples in seconds and can be automated for large batches, where traditional tests would take minutes each.
  6. Instrumental methods can resolve mixtures of ions (e.g. distinguish sodium and potassium in the same sample) where a single flame test would be masked by the brighter colour.
  7. The main disadvantage is cost — instruments are expensive to buy and maintain — and a secondary disadvantage is that they typically require calibration against standard solutions before use.
  8. The Edexcel specification explicitly states that no knowledge of the instrument or how it works is required — focus on the why-it's-better content, not on instrument internals or diagrams.
  9. The named instrumental method on the Edexcel GCSE Chemistry specification is flame photometry (spec point 9.1.6.2), and the standard comparator is the traditional flame test for metal ions.
  10. This spec point appears on Paper 2 of the Separate Chemistry award (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H), typically as a short recall part (1–2 marks) or as the lead-in to a multi-part flame-photometry question.
  11. For full marks, pair every named advantage with a one-line justification or example — "more sensitive" alone is weaker than "more sensitive, because the instrument can detect ions at parts-per-million levels that would be invisible in a flame test".
  12. Do not write "instrumental methods are better" as a generic statement — examiners reward the specific named advantage and a concrete reason, not a value judgement.
9.1.6.2 Flame Photometry F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A flame photometer measures the intensity of light emitted at characteristic wavelengths when metal ions are excited in a flame.
  2. The Edexcel specification states no knowledge of how the instrument works is required — you are only assessed on reading and evaluating its data.
  3. Each metal ion emits light at its own characteristic wavelength, so peaks in a spectrum can be matched against reference data to identify which ions are present.
  4. A calibration curve is a graph of light intensity (y-axis) against known ion concentration (x-axis), built from standard solutions of known concentration.
  5. To find the concentration of an unknown: read its intensity on the y-axis, go across to the calibration line, then down to the x-axis — that x-value is the concentration.
  6. The calibration line is typically straight and passes through the origin (zero ion → zero intensity), with intensity directly proportional to concentration.
  7. Flame photometry resolves mixtures of ions that a simple flame test cannot — e.g. sodium and potassium together — because each ion's wavelength is measured separately.
  8. Flame photometry is more accurate, more sensitive, faster, and gives a numerical concentration — whereas a flame test gives only a colour observed by eye.
  9. If a question says a flame test has already been done and asks how the ions are confirmed, the answer is use a flame photometer — not "do the flame test again". This was the most common mistake on 1CH0/2H June 2019, where the most common score was 0.
  10. To explain how a flame photometer confirms both ions in a mixture: it produces peaks at the characteristic wavelengths of each ion, which can be compared with reference data to confirm each ion's identity (and to a calibration curve to give concentration).
  11. Always quote concentrations with the correct units (g/dm³, mol/dm³ or mg/dm³ — check the y-axis label) and read graph values to the nearest small division rather than rounding aggressively.
  12. On graph-reading questions, draw the horizontal and vertical lines on the calibration graph itself so the examiner can see your method even if the final number is slightly out.
9.2.1.1 Alkanes F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only carbon and hydrogen atoms — no other elements. Get this definition word-perfect: the 1CH0/2F June 2019 Examiner Report flagged "hydrocarbon" as the most-confused term in Section 9.
  2. An alkane is a saturated hydrocarbon — only single covalent bonds between carbons, with each carbon at its maximum hydrogen count.
  3. The general formula for alkanes is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Substitute the number of carbons in for n to get the molecular formula.
  4. The four named alkanes are: methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀. Learn these molecular formulae by heart.
  5. In a displayed formula you must draw every C–C bond and every C–H bond as a separate line. Every carbon must have exactly four bonds.
  6. Why alkanes are saturated (2-mark answer): (1) only single bonds between carbon atoms, so (2) each carbon already has the maximum number of hydrogen atoms and the molecule cannot add any more.
  7. Each consecutive alkane differs from the last by CH₂ — this is the homologous series pattern.
  8. Because alkanes have no C=C double bonds, they are relatively unreactive and do not decolourise bromine water (a key contrast with alkenes).
  9. Watch out for the atoms vs molecules trap: hydrocarbons contain hydrogen atoms and carbon atoms, not "hydrogen molecules and carbon molecules" — this exact error was flagged in 1CH0/2F June 2019.
  10. Hydrocarbon ≠ unsaturated. These two terms are tested separately. Answer the question that is actually asked.
  11. Use the general formula to check your drawing: if your displayed propane has 7 hydrogens instead of 8, you have forgotten one.
9.2.2.1 Alkenes F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Alkenes are a homologous series of unsaturated hydrocarbons with general formula CₙH₂ₙ.
  2. A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only hydrogen and carbon atoms — the word atoms (not molecules) matters in the definition.
  3. The three named alkenes for Edexcel GCSE are ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆, and butene C₄H₈ — for butene, only but-1-ene and but-2-ene are required.
  4. The functional group of every alkene is the C=C double bond — this is what makes the molecule reactive.
  5. Alkenes are unsaturated because the C=C double bond can break open and add extra atoms (such as Br₂, H₂, or H₂O) across it.
  6. Alkenes have two fewer hydrogens than the corresponding alkane — compare CₙH₂ₙ (alkene) with CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (alkane).
  7. But-1-ene (CH₃–CH₂–CH=CH₂) and but-2-ene (CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃) are structural isomers of C₄H₈ — same molecular formula, different position of the C=C.
  8. When drawing a displayed formula, every covalent bond must be shown — including both lines of the C=C, all C–C single bonds, and every C–H bond. The total number of hydrogens must equal 2n.
  9. Alkenes decolourise bromine water (orange → colourless) because the C=C adds Br₂ across the double bond — this is the chemical test that distinguishes alkenes from alkanes (covered in 9.2.2.2 Reaction of Alkenes with Bromine).
  10. Alkanes have only single C–C bonds and are saturated and unreactive by comparison — they do not react with bromine water.
  11. The June 2019 1CH0/2F report shows the unsaturated mark is usually scored but the hydrocarbon mark is often lost — learn the precise wording.
  12. Alkenes are hydrocarbons — they contain no oxygen. On complete combustion they form CO₂ and H₂O only (never ethanoic acid or any other oxygenated product).
9.2.2.2 Bromine & Alkenes F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Alkenes react with bromine in an addition reaction: the C=C double bond opens up and one Br atom attaches to each of the two carbons.
  2. The product of ethene + bromine is 1,2-dibromoethane: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂BrCH₂Br.
  3. The same pattern applies to other alkenes: propene + Br₂ → 1,2-dibromopropane; but-2-ene + Br₂ → 2,3-dibromobutane.
  4. Bromine water is an orange/brown solution of bromine dissolved in water — learn this colour exactly.
  5. An alkene turns bromine water from orange/brown to colourless (the bromine is used up in the addition reaction).
  6. An alkane does not react with bromine water — it stays orange/brown (alkanes are saturated, no C=C bond available).
  7. The correct word for the final solution is colourless — not "clear". Clear just means transparent; colourless means no colour at all.
  8. A full answer to the bromine water test needs the pair of observations: what happens with the alkene and what happens (or doesn't happen) with the alkane.
  9. Always state the starting colour (orange/brown) of bromine water as well as the final colour — examiners penalise answers that skip the original colour.
  10. If the command word is describe what you would see, give the observation only; if it is explain, also link the colour change to the C=C double bond opening up to allow addition.
  11. The general pattern for any alkene: CₙH₂ₙ + Br₂ → CₙH₂ₙBr₂ — two Br atoms added across the original double bond.
  12. The reactivity of alkenes (compared with the unreactivity of alkanes) is the central reason alkenes are useful starting materials in industry — for both addition reactions and addition polymerisation.
9.2.3.1 Combustion of Hydrocarbons F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon is its reaction with excess oxygen to give only carbon dioxide and water — there are no other products.
  2. Complete combustion is exothermic — it transfers energy to the surroundings — and is the reason hydrocarbons are used as fuels.
  3. The hydrocarbon is oxidised (oxygen is added to both the carbon and the hydrogen); oxygen is simultaneously reduced — it is a redox reaction.
  4. Balance combustion equations in the order C → H → O. If the O₂ coefficient comes out as a fraction, multiply every coefficient by 2 to clear it.
  5. Methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
  6. Ethene (canonical Edexcel equation): C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O — mole ratio 1 : 3 : 2 : 2.
  7. Propene: 2C₃H₆ + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O. Butene: C₄H₈ + 6O₂ → 4CO₂ + 4H₂O.
  8. Oxygen in an equation must always be written as O₂, never as O — writing O (atomic oxygen) was a specific source of lost marks in 1CH0/2H June 2019.
  9. The products of complete combustion are only CO₂ and H₂O — not ethanoic acid or any other partially oxidised species. The June 2019 ethanoic-acid misconception was flagged by examiners.
  10. At Higher tier, state symbols may be required — under combustion conditions all species are gases, so (g) is normally used throughout, including for water as steam.
  11. Alkenes need slightly less oxygen per molecule than the corresponding alkane (e.g. C₂H₄ needs 3 O₂; C₂H₆ needs 3.5 O₂) because they have fewer hydrogens to convert to water.
  12. Memorise the four alkane and three alkene equations in the tables above — they are high-frequency Paper 2 content and account for the majority of combustion-balancing marks at GCSE.
9.3.1.1 Polymers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A polymer is a substance of high average relative molecular mass made up of small repeating units — get all three parts of that definition into any "what is a polymer" answer.
  2. Polymers are made by joining many monomer molecules together in a chemical reaction called polymerisation.
  3. The repeating unit is the short pattern that appears over and over along the polymer chain; it is what the polymer "inherits" from its monomer.
  4. The repeating unit is written inside square brackets with an n outside the bracket to show that it repeats n times — dropping the n loses marks (1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report).
  5. The relative molecular mass of a polymer chain is approximately n × Mr(repeating unit), and can reach 10⁴ to 10⁶ for real plastics.
  6. The word "average" appears in the definition because real polymer samples contain chains of slightly different lengths.
  7. Polymers are single substances, not mixtures — the monomers have reacted, not just been mixed.
  8. Polymer names follow the pattern poly(monomer name) — keep every part of the name including any "tetra" prefix and the brackets (1CH0/2F June 2019 examiner report).
  9. The high relative molecular mass explains polymer properties: solids at room temperature, often strong and mouldable, low volatility, often insoluble in water.
  10. Spec point 9.3.1.1 is Foundation + Higher and is examined only on Paper 2 of 1CH0 (Separate Chemistry); it usually anchors the start of a longer polymer question rather than appearing as a stand-alone item.
  11. Candidates "generally understand" polymer formation but lose marks through weak technical literacy (1CH0/2H November 2021 examiner report) — practise saying the definition out loud until the wording is automatic.
  12. This spec point is the foundation for the rest of Topic 9.3 — get the definition watertight here and addition polymers (9.3.2), condensation polymers (9.3.3), and natural polymers (9.3.5) all become easier.
9.3.2.1 Formation of Polythene F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Ethene (C₂H₄) is the monomer used to make poly(ethene); it contains a C=C double bond that opens up during polymerisation.
  2. Addition polymerisation joins many ethene molecules into a single long chain with no other product formed.
  3. The product is called poly(ethene) — the IUPAC name, with brackets. "Polythene" and "polyethylene" mean the same substance.
  4. The equation is n CH₂=CH₂ → −(−CH₂−CH₂−)−ₙ, where n is a large number (typically thousands).
  5. The repeating unit is −CH₂−CH₂− inside square (or round) brackets, with two extension bonds and a subscript n.
  6. Inside the repeating unit the C=C of ethene has become a C–C single bond — drawing the double bond still in place is a common mark-loser.
  7. Every atom of the monomer ends up in the polymer, so Mᵣ(repeating unit) = Mᵣ(monomer) and Mᵣ(polymer) = n × Mᵣ(monomer).
  8. Conditions and mechanisms are not required at GCSE — do not waste time writing about catalysts, temperatures or radicals.
  9. Ethene comes industrially from cracking long-chain alkanes from crude oil.
  10. Poly(ethene) is widely used for bags, bottles, films and cable insulation because it is solid at room temperature, flexible, unreactive, waterproof and an electrical insulator.
  11. This SP is Separate Chemistry only (1CH0) and is examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
  12. Examiner reports flag the missing "n" outside the bracket and missing brackets in polymer names as the most common errors — a clearly drawn diagram with all labels in place is the safest way to score.
9.3.2.2 Other Addition Polymers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Addition polymers form when many monomers, each containing a C=C double bond, join end-to-end with no other product formed — all atoms in the monomers end up in the polymer.
  2. The three "other" addition polymers named in 9.19C are poly(propene), poly(chloroethene) (PVC) and poly(tetrafluoroethene) (PTFE), made from propene, chloroethene and tetrafluoroethene respectively.
  3. The monomer of poly(propene) is CH₂=CH–CH₃; its repeating unit is –[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]– n — a methyl group on every alternate carbon.
  4. The monomer of PVC is CH₂=CHCl (chloroethene); its repeating unit is –[CH₂–CHCl]– n — one chlorine on every alternate carbon.
  5. The monomer of PTFE is CF₂=CF₂ (tetrafluoroethene); its repeating unit is –[CF₂–CF₂]– n — all four hydrogens of ethene replaced by fluorine.
  6. The repeating unit is always two carbons wide because it comes from opening out the single C=C bond in the monomer.
  7. You must draw trailing bonds extending through the square brackets on both sides — they show where the unit links to the next one along the chain.
  8. The subscript n outside the brackets is what makes the structure a polymer; missing it is a frequently penalised error (June 2019 Foundation examiner report).
  9. Polymer names are written as poly(monomer) — the brackets and any prefix (e.g. "tetra" in poly(tetrafluoroethene)) are part of the name and must not be dropped.
  10. Conditions and mechanisms (high pressure, radicals, initiation steps) are explicitly not required by 9.19C — focus on structures and names only.
  11. This SP is Separate Chemistry only (1CH0) and appears on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier; on 2F it has appeared as a 3-mark table-completion question (June 2019 Q3(a)).
  12. Command words used here are Draw, State and Name — short factual marks awarded for accuracy of structure and naming, not for extended explanation.
9.3.2.3 Deducing Structures F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Addition polymerisation joins many small monomers (each with a C=C double bond) into one long chain with no other product formed.
  2. The single chemical change to remember: the monomer's C=C double bond becomes a C–C single bond in the repeating unit of the polymer.
  3. Side groups (H, Cl, F, CH₃) stay attached to the same carbons they were on in the monomer.
  4. The number and type of atoms in the monomer equals the number and type of atoms in the repeating unit — nothing is added, nothing is removed.
  5. A correctly drawn repeating unit needs three features: square brackets, continuation bonds on both sides, and a subscript n outside the bracket.
  6. A correctly drawn monomer needs a clear C=C double bond, with the right substituents on each carbon, and every carbon showing four bonds.
  7. To go monomer → polymer: open the double bond, draw the two carbons single-bonded inside square brackets, add continuation bonds and n.
  8. To go polymer → monomer: take one repeat unit, remove brackets/continuation bonds/n, change the single bond between the backbone carbons to a double bond, keep substituents in the same place.
  9. Be careful with polymer names — poly(tetrafluoroethene) needs the "tetra"; the 2019 Foundation Paper 2 examiner report flagged this as a common omission.
  10. The same examiner report flagged students missing out the subscript n after the brackets when drawing poly(ethene) — always check the n is there.
  11. A fast self-check before moving on: count every H, C, Cl, F on both sides of your translation; if the totals don't match, redraw.
  12. Questions on this SP are almost always presented as a table pairing polymer ↔ monomer structures or names; the command words to expect are Draw, Deduce, and State.
9.3.2.4 Uses of Polymers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The four named polymers in 9.21C are poly(ethene), poly(propene), poly(chloroethene) (PVC) and poly(tetrafluoroethene) (PTFE) — all addition polymers made from alkene monomers.
  2. Polymers are useful because they combine strong covalent bonds along the chain (toughness) with weaker forces between chains (which control flexibility and softening); side groups on the chain change how chains pack and therefore how the polymer behaves.
  3. Poly(ethene) is flexible, tough, lightweight and chemically inert — used for plastic bags, cling film and squeezy bottles.
  4. Poly(propene) is stronger and more rigid than poly(ethene), with higher softening temperature and good fatigue resistance — used for crates, ropes, carpets and food containers.
  5. PVC is rigid, durable, weather-resistant and an electrical insulator (and can be made flexible with plasticisers) — used for window frames, drainpipes, water/waste pipes and cable insulation.
  6. PTFE has a very low-friction surface, very high chemical resistance and a high softening temperature — used for non-stick coatings on cookware, stain-resistant fabrics, bearings and gaskets in chemical plants.
  7. The question can be set in either direction: property → use, or use → property. Your answer must explicitly link the property to the use ("because… so that…").
  8. Polymers are chemically inert because their chains are saturated — the C=C double bonds of the monomers are used up during polymerisation, so polymers do not decolourise bromine water and do not react readily with acids or alkalis at room temperature.
  9. Examiners (1CH0/2H — June 2019) reported a common misconception that poly(ethene) "still contains many double bonds" and would react with bromine water faster than ethene — the correct answer is no colour change, because poly(ethene) is saturated.
  10. The command words used for this SP in current Edexcel papers are State, Suggest and Explain; "Explain" requires the property-to-use link to be made explicit.
  11. The same inertness that makes these polymers useful is what makes them hard to dispose of and recycle — link forward to 9.3.4.1 Problems with Polymers and 9.3.4.2 Recycling Polymers.
  12. Quick rule of thumb: flexible/cheap = poly(ethene); strong/rigid = poly(propene); rigid + weather/electrical = PVC; non-stick + heat/chemical resistant = PTFE.
9.3.3.1 Condensation Polymers H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Condensation polymerisation joins monomers together while releasing a small molecule (water) each time a new linkage is formed.
  2. A polyester is a condensation polymer joined by ester linkages (–COO–).
  3. Polyesters are made from two different bifunctional monomers: a dicarboxylic acid (HOOC–R–COOH) and a diol (HO–R′–OH).
  4. The reactive groups are the –COOH group of the carboxylic acid and the –OH group of the alcohol; they react together to form the ester linkage.
  5. Every time an ester linkage forms, one molecule of water (H₂O) is released — the –OH from the acid and the H from the alcohol's –OH combine to leave as water.
  6. The polymer repeat unit contains two ester linkages, so on average two water molecules are released per repeat unit of polyester formed.
  7. In an exam diagram, show the monomers end-to-end, circle the atoms lost as water, and draw the –COO– linkage clearly between the remaining fragments.
  8. Addition vs condensation — addition uses one monomer with a C=C bond and has 100% atom economy; condensation uses two different monomers with two functional groups each and releases water.
  9. Condensation polymers and addition polymers can be distinguished by checking whether a small molecule by-product is released — if yes, it is condensation.
  10. The chain keeps growing because every monomer has two reactive ends, so the polymer can extend in both directions.
  11. This spec point is Higher tier only and only examined on Paper 2 (1CH0/2H) of Separate Chemistry; it does not appear in Combined Science.
  12. The most common exam question is a 3-mark describe-the-differences item comparing addition and condensation polymerisation — practise three distinct comparison points to secure full marks.
9.3.4.1 Problems with Polymers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Polymers are made from monomers (e.g. ethene, propene, chloroethene) which come from crude oil — a finite, non-renewable resource that will eventually run out and has many competing uses.
  2. Most addition polymers are non-biodegradable: their C–C and C–H backbones cannot be broken down by microorganisms, so plastic waste persists in landfill for hundreds of years.
  3. Landfill space is finite, and non-biodegradable polymer waste accumulates rather than decomposing, taking up land and contributing to visual pollution and wildlife harm.
  4. Burning polymers releases carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas contributing to climate change), carbon monoxide (a toxic gas from incomplete combustion), and soot/particulates.
  5. Burning PVC (poly(chloroethene)) releases hydrogen chloride (HCl), which is acidic and contributes to acid rain.
  6. Some polymers, when burned, release additional toxic gases (e.g. nitrogen oxides from nitrogen-containing polymers) — but the GCSE-named examples are CO₂, CO and HCl.
  7. Polymers can in principle be recycled by melting and reforming, but only thermosoftening polymers can be reshaped this way.
  8. Recycling requires polymers to be sorted by type first, because different polymers have different melting points and properties — mixing them would produce a low-quality recycled product.
  9. Sorting is labour-intensive, time-consuming and expensive, which is why much polymer waste still ends up in landfill or is incinerated rather than recycled.
  10. In exam answers, always qualify your examples: name the specific gas (CO, HCl), name the property (non-biodegradable), and link each to a specific consequence (acid rain, toxic, greenhouse effect).
  11. Vague answers like "causes pollution", "harms the environment", "releases harmful chemicals" or "causes landfill" will not score (1CH0/2F — 2019 June examiner report).
  12. The spec sub-divides this point into four distinct issues — (a) starting materials, (b) landfill persistence, (c) combustion gases, (d) sorting — and you should be ready to address any of them by name in a "Describe" or "Explain" question.
9.3.4.2 Recycling Polymers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Recycling polymers means collecting, sorting, cleaning and re-processing waste plastic into new products instead of sending it to landfill or incineration.
  2. The spec command word for 9.24C is evaluate — you must give both advantages and disadvantages and reach a justified conclusion.
  3. The three spec themes you must cover are economic implications, availability of starting materials, and environmental impact.
  4. Crude oil is a finite, non-renewable resource — recycling conserves the hydrocarbon feedstock and extends its supply.
  5. Most polymers are non-biodegradable, so landfill plastic persists for hundreds of years — recycling reduces the mass going to landfill.
  6. Sorting is the biggest practical problem: different polymers melt at different temperatures and cannot be mixed without producing low-quality plastic.
  7. Thermosetting polymers cannot be melted and remoulded, so mechanical recycling does not work for them.
  8. Mechanical recycling (melt and remould) is cheaper but quality drops each cycle; chemical recycling (break back to monomers) gives higher-quality polymer but uses more energy.
  9. Recycling still has costs: collection, sorting, cleaning and re-processing all use energy and water and emit CO₂ — but usually less than making virgin polymer from crude oil.
  10. Vague answers do not score — the June 2019 examiner report criticised responses like "causes pollution" or "harms the environment". Always qualify your point (e.g. "reduces landfill mass, because polymers are non-biodegradable").
  11. For an "evaluate" answer, plan 3–4 advantages and 3–4 disadvantages across at least two of the three spec themes, then close with a one-sentence justified overall judgement.
  12. Recycling is not unlimited — polymer chains shorten slightly each time, so plastic is often downcycled (e.g. PET bottles into polyester fibre) before eventually being discarded.
9.4.1.1 Alcohols F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Alcohols are a homologous series with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH (equivalent to CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O).
  2. The functional group in alcohols is –OH (the hydroxyl group); this is spec point 9.27C.
  3. The four alcohols you must know are methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol — only the 1-ol isomers are assessed.
  4. Each consecutive alcohol differs from the previous one by –CH₂– (Mr difference of 14), which is the defining property of a homologous series.
  5. Every carbon makes 4 bonds, every oxygen makes 2 bonds, every hydrogen makes 1 bond — this rule lets you check any displayed structure.
  6. The –OH group must be drawn as a separate O and H connected by a single line, not as a single "OH" cluster — the displayed formula requires every bond to be shown.
  7. Alcohols are saturated: there are no double bonds anywhere in the molecule.
  8. All four spec alcohols are liquids at room temperature; boiling point increases with chain length (65 °C → 78 °C → 97 °C → 117 °C).
  9. The –OH on carbon 1 rule applies to propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol — placing it in the middle of the chain is a wrong-isomer error.
  10. Math Skill 5b — translating between names/formulae and displayed structures — is the core skill assessed for this spec point.
  11. On Paper 2, the recurring task is to Draw the displayed structure of one of the first four alcohols, showing every covalent bond.
  12. The closest related question reference available is 1CH0/2H June 2024 Q10(b)(i) — a 1-mark naming task about the carbon-containing product of dehydration.
9.4.2.1 Core Practical: Heat of Combustion of Alcohols F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The Core Practical compares the energy released by ethanol, propanol, butanol and pentanol by burning each in turn and measuring either the mass burnt for a fixed temperature rise, or the temperature rise for a fixed mass of fuel.
  2. The independent variable is the alcohol used; the dependent variable is the mass burnt (or temperature rise); key control variables are mass of water, starting temperature, distance from wick to calorimeter, and target temperature rise.
  3. Mass burnt is the difference between the mass of the spirit burner before and after, not the total mass of the burner — confusing these is a top examiner-reported error.
  4. The target is a temperature rise of 30 °C above the starting temperature — not a final reading of 30 °C on the thermometer; this is the other top examiner-reported error.
  5. Apparatus essentials: spirit burner, copper calorimeter, clamp and stand, thermometer (or probe), balance to 0.01 g, measuring cylinder, lid and draught shield.
  6. Safety: alcohols are highly flammable — cap the burner to extinguish, never blow out; wear goggles; use a heatproof mat; allow apparatus to cool before handling.
  7. The general trend is that mass burnt decreases as the carbon chain lengthens — longer-chain alcohols release more energy per gram because more bonds are broken and formed during combustion.
  8. To plot the graph: number of carbon atoms on the x-axis, mass burnt (or temperature rise) on the y-axis; draw a smooth curve of best fit.
  9. Experimental values are always lower than data-book values because of heat loss to surroundings, incomplete combustion, evaporation from the wick and warming of the calorimeter rather than the water.
  10. Improvements: lid on the calorimeter, draught shield, polished metal calorimeter, fixed wick-to-base distance, immediate capping of the burner to stop evaporation losses.
  11. On 1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H Paper 2, this practical appears most commonly as a 6-mark extended-method question or a data-interpretation graph question, with command words Describe, Draw, Plot, Calculate and Explain.
  12. When describing the method in an extended-response question, explicitly state that you stop heating when the temperature has risen by 30 °C and that the mass of fuel burnt is found by subtracting the final from the initial mass of the burner (not just the alcohol).
9.4.3.1 Carboxylic Acids F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The four named carboxylic acids you must recall are methanoic (HCOOH), ethanoic (CH₃COOH), propanoic (C₂H₅COOH) and butanoic (C₃H₇COOH) acid.
  2. The functional group of every carboxylic acid is –COOH (the carboxyl group), per spec 9.30C.
  3. The –COOH group is a carbon double-bonded to one oxygen (C=O on the lone, terminal oxygen) and single-bonded to an –O–H group; the carbon also makes one further single bond to H (methanoic) or to an alkyl chain.
  4. Hydrogen forms only one covalent bond — it can never be double-bonded to oxygen, so any structure showing H=O is wrong.
  5. The most common Edexcel mistake (1CH0/2F June 2019 Examiner Report) is putting the C=O double bond on the wrong oxygen — the one carrying the H — instead of on the lone carbonyl oxygen.
  6. Methanoic acid is the n = 0 exception with only one carbon (HCOOH); it does not have a –CH₃ group. Do not confuse it with ethanoic acid.
  7. Carboxylic acids form a homologous series with general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH (or CₙH₂ₙO₂ overall for n ≥ 1); successive members differ by –CH₂– and Mr increases by 14.
  8. A displayed structure must show every covalent bond — including all C–H bonds and the O–H bond — not just the carbon skeleton.
  9. Common command words on this SP are "Draw", "State" and "Name"; the standard exam task is drawing the displayed structure of a named carboxylic acid on Paper 2.
  10. This SP is Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only — it does not appear on 1SC0 Combined Science papers.
9.4.3.2 Making Ethanoic Acid F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid; the word equation is ethanol + oxygen → ethanoic acid + water.
  2. The symbol equation is CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O — [O] represents an unspecified oxidising agent (you do not need to recall reagents).
  3. The reaction is an oxidation because oxygen is added (the carboxylic acid has one more oxygen atom than the alcohol).
  4. The functional group changes from −OH (alcohol) to −COOH (carboxylic acid), but the rest of the carbon chain is unchanged.
  5. The number of carbon atoms is the same in the alcohol and its oxidation product: ethanol (C2) → ethanoic acid (C2); propanol (C3) → propanoic acid (C3); butanol (C4) → butanoic acid (C4); methanol (C1) → methanoic acid (C1).
  6. The naming pattern is: drop the -anol ending of the alcohol and replace it with -anoic acid for the oxidation product, keeping the same stem (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-).
  7. Vinegar is a dilute aqueous solution of ethanoic acid, made by allowing the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to be oxidised by bacteria in the air.
  8. Wine turns sour when exposed to air because Acetobacter bacteria oxidise the ethanol in the wine to ethanoic acid — the same overall reaction as the lab oxidation.
  9. Ethanoic acid is a weak acid because the −COOH group can release a proton (H⁺) in aqueous solution, forming ethanoate (CH₃COO⁻); ethanol does not do this and is neutral.
  10. Common exam command words for this spec point are "Name", "State" and "Give" — usually 1-mark items on Paper 2.
  11. This spec point is Separate Chemistry only (1CH0) and is examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
  12. Do not change the carbon count when answering: the oxidation product of an n-carbon primary alcohol is always the n-carbon carboxylic acid.
9.4.3.3 Predicting Products F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Members of a homologous series share the same functional group, the same general formula, and differ by a –CH₂– unit between consecutive members.
  2. Because they share a functional group, every member undergoes the same types of reaction — only the size of the product changes.
  3. The relative formula mass increases by 14 each step up the series (the mass of –CH₂–); this is the fastest plausibility check on a predicted formula.
  4. Carboxylic acid + reactive metal → salt + hydrogen; the salt is named by changing "-ic acid" to "-oate" (e.g. propanoic acid + sodium → sodium propanoate + hydrogen).
  5. Carboxylic acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide; works for every carboxylic acid in the series.
  6. Carboxylic acid + alcohol → ester + water; the ester name has the alcohol part first ("-yl") and the acid part second ("-oate"), e.g. methanol + ethanoic acid → methyl ethanoate + water.
  7. Complete combustion of any organic molecule in excess oxygen always gives carbon dioxide and water; balance for chain length only.
  8. Going up a homologous series: boiling point, density and viscosity increase; solubility in water and flammability decrease.
  9. The November 2021 Higher Paper 2 examiner report (1CH0/2H) singled out candidates who named the –COOH group, named esters and sodium salts as the product types, and linked the +14 Mr pattern to trends in boiling point and density.
  10. When answering "Predict and explain" questions, always name the functional group first, then give the product, then justify with the shared-reaction rule.
  11. Do not invent new reactions for higher members — you only need to extend the reaction already in the stem to a longer chain.
  12. Predictions for physical properties of a missing member can be made by interpolation (between the members above and below) or by extrapolation using the +14 Mr step.
9.4.4.1 Fermentation F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Fermentation converts sugars (carbohydrates) into ethanol and carbon dioxide using yeast in aqueous solution under anaerobic conditions at around 30 °C.
  2. The word equation is: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide. The symbol equation is: C₆H₁₂O₆ (aq) → 2C₂H₅OH (aq) + 2CO₂ (g).
  3. Yeast is a single-celled fungus that provides the enzymes which catalyse the reaction — yeast is not the catalyst directly, the enzymes inside the yeast are.
  4. Enzymes are proteins, and proteins denature (lose their shape and function) when heated too strongly — so high temperatures stop the reaction completely.
  5. Around 30 °C is the working compromise: warm enough for the enzymes to be active, cool enough that they are not denatured. At 80 °C the enzymes would denature and no ethanol would be made.
  6. Fermentation is carried out in aqueous solution so that the yeast cells and enzymes can move freely and access the dissolved sugar.
  7. Fermentation is anaerobic — the absence of oxygen is what makes the yeast produce ethanol rather than respiring fully to CO₂ and water.
  8. Fermentation stops when either the sugar is used up or the ethanol concentration becomes high enough (around 15%) to kill the yeast.
  9. Compared to the hydration of ethene, fermentation uses a renewable feedstock, runs at low temperature, is a batch process, and produces an impure aqueous mixture of ethanol that must be distilled to be concentrated — see 9.4.4.2 Purifying the Product.
  10. The high-mark "explain why 30 °C not 80 °C" answer must contain two ideas: (a) yeast provides the enzymes, and (b) those enzymes would denature at 80 °C.
  11. Common exam-losing mistakes: mentioning denaturation without linking it to yeast; mentioning yeast without explaining why 30 °C specifically; saying the mixture is heated "to speed up the reaction" without noting the upper temperature limit.
  12. Fermentation is a Separate Chemistry only topic (1CH0) and is examined on Paper 2 at both Foundation and Higher tier.
9.4.4.2 Purifying the Product F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Fermentation gives only a dilute ethanol/water mixture (around 10–15% ethanol); to obtain a concentrated ethanol solution you use fractional distillation.
  2. The separation works because ethanol boils at 78 °C and water boils at 100 °C — a 22 °C gap.
  3. The apparatus is: round-bottom flask containing the fermentation mixture (with anti-bumping granules) → fractionating column packed with glass beads → thermometer at the top of the column → condenser → collection vessel.
  4. The fractionating column must be vertical, between the flask and the condenser. Its position is the most common thing candidates get wrong.
  5. The column creates a temperature gradient — hot at the bottom, cooler at the top. Water vapour condenses inside the column and runs back down; ethanol vapour passes through and out to the condenser.
  6. The thermometer at the top of the column confirms ethanol is being collected: a steady reading near 78 °C means ethanol vapour is leaving the column.
  7. The condenser cools the ethanol vapour back into liquid; cold water flows through the jacket counter-current to the vapour.
  8. You stop collecting when the thermometer reading rises sharply towards 100 °C — that is water vapour starting to come over.
  9. "Pre-boiling the dilute ethanol" does not help: ethanol has the lower boiling point, so pre-boiling would lose ethanol, not water.
  10. "Putting an ice-pack around the condenser" does not help: separation happens in the column, not the condenser. Extra cooling at the condenser just condenses whatever vapour reaches it.
  11. Fractional distillation can concentrate ethanol up to about 96%, but not 100% — beyond this, ethanol and water form a mixture that boils as a single substance.
  12. This spec point is examined on Paper 2 only (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H), Separate Chemistry only — it is not part of Combined Science (1SC0).
9.5.1.1 Size of Nanoparticles F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A nanoparticle has at least one dimension between 1 nm and 100 nm, i.e. between 1 × 10⁻⁹ m and 1 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  2. 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m; convert nm to m by multiplying by 10⁻⁹, and m to nm by multiplying by 10⁹.
  3. A typical atom is ~1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across — about one tenth of a nanometre.
  4. A small molecule (e.g. H₂O) is ~1 × 10⁻¹⁰ to 1 × 10⁻⁹ m across — at most about 1 nm.
  5. Nanoparticles are roughly 10 to 1 000 times bigger than a single atom across their diameter.
  6. The top of the nanoparticle range (100 nm) is about 1 000 atoms across; the bottom (1 nm) is about 10 atoms across.
  7. Above 100 nm you move into fine particles (PM2.5, up to ~2 500 nm) and coarse particles (dust, up to ~100 000 nm), which behave like bulk material rather than nano.
  8. Bulk material grains are typically larger than 1 × 10⁻⁶ m (1 000 nm and up) — three or more orders of magnitude bigger than a nanoparticle.
  9. Standard form is essential: rewrite "30 × 10⁻⁹" as "3.0 × 10⁻⁸" so the leading number is in the range 1 ≤ A < 10.
  10. The spec point appears on Paper 2 only and is Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only — it is not on Combined Science 1SC0 papers.
  11. The size question is almost always at the start of Paper 2 (Q1 or Q2) and often feeds into a surface-area-to-volume-ratio calculation that follows.
  12. For "Compare" questions, always quote two size values and state the relationship between them (e.g. "100 times larger", "two orders of magnitude bigger").
9.5.1.2 Properties of Nanoparticles F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A nanoparticle has at least one dimension between 1 and 100 nm.
  2. Nanoparticles have very high surface area to volume (SA:V) ratios compared with the bulk form of the same substance.
  3. For a cube of side length l: surface area = 6l², volume = l³, so SA:V = 6/l. As l gets smaller, SA:V gets larger.
  4. A higher SA:V means more atoms are exposed at the surface, which is why nanoparticles tend to be more reactive, more effective catalysts, and more biologically active.
  5. Titanium dioxide nanoparticles are used in sunscreens because they absorb UV light from the sun while staying transparent to visible light — say "UV light", not just "light" or "sunlight" (1CH0/2H — 2019 June examiner report).
  6. Silver nanoparticles are used as antibacterials; nanoparticulate catalysts are used in industry; nanoparticles are also being developed for drug delivery and in sensors and electronics.
  7. The chemistry of a nanoparticulate substance is the same as the bulk substance — it is the size and SA:V that change the useful properties, not the elements involved.
  8. Calculation skills required by Edexcel for this spec point: surface area of a cube (6 × l²), volume of a cube (l³), and simplifying a surface area : volume ratio.
  9. For an 80 nm cube: surface area = 6 × 80² = 38 400 nm² (1CH0/2F — 2024 June Q1(c) style).
  10. For a 60 nm cube: surface area = 21 600 nm², volume = 216 000 nm³, SA:V = 21 600 : 216 000 = 1 : 10 (1CH0/2H — 2022 June Q1(c) style).
  11. "Show that" questions only score full marks if every step of the working is written down, because the final answer is already in the question stem.
  12. Expect this spec point at the very beginning of Paper 2 — Q1 or Q2 — as either a short explanation question or a 3-mark calculation.
9.5.1.3 Risks F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Nanoparticles raise new safety concerns because their tiny size and huge surface-area-to-volume ratio let them behave very differently from the bulk material.
  2. The same properties that make nanoparticles useful (small enough to pass through barriers, reactive enough to do new chemistry) are what make them potentially risky.
  3. Specific risks you should be able to name: penetration through the skin, passage into the bloodstream, catalysis of unexpected reactions inside the body, accumulation in cells, inhalation into the lungs, environmental release, and the novelty of the technology.
  4. For full marks on an "Explain" item you must link a specific risk to a specific consequence — for example, "penetrate the skin and pass into the bloodstream where they may catalyse reactions in cells".
  5. Vague answers like "harms the body" or "is bad for you" score zero — examiner reports are explicit about this.
  6. A half-formed answer such as "they can penetrate the skin" with no consequence scores only 1 of 2 marks.
  7. The novelty argument is also a valid full-marks route: nanotechnology is new, so long-term effects on health are not yet known and unexpected problems may emerge later.
  8. This SP is examined only on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H), often near the start of the paper (Q1 or Q2) as a 2-mark "Explain" item.
  9. The SP is Separate Chemistry only (1CH0) — it is not on Combined Science (1SC0).
  10. There is no calculation in this SP — marks are won purely on the precision and structure of your written answer.
  11. Practise the shape [specific risk] + [linking word] + [specific consequence] until it is automatic; almost every mark scheme on this spec point follows it.
  12. Choose your example to fit the context in the question — sunscreens and cosmetics fit "skin penetration"; medicines and inhaled products fit "bloodstream / lungs"; widespread consumer products fit "novelty / long-term effects unknown".
9.5.2.1 Ceramics, Polymers, Composites & Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The five material classes on this spec point are clay ceramics, glass ceramics, polymers, composites and metals, and you must be able to compare their physical properties using a data table.
  2. Clay ceramics (e.g. porcelain, bricks) are hard, brittle, electrical and thermal insulators with very high melting points, made by firing clay in a kiln.
  3. Glass ceramics (e.g. soda-lime glass) are amorphous SiO₂-based solids — hard, brittle, transparent, and chemically inert, made by melting sand with sodium carbonate and limestone.
  4. Polymers are long-chain molecules with low density, low melting points, flexibility and electrical/thermal insulation; thermosofteners can be re-melted, thermosets cannot because of covalent cross-links.
  5. Composites combine a matrix (binder) with a reinforcement (fibres or particles) to give properties neither component has on its own — e.g. fibreglass (glass fibres in resin), CFRP, concrete (sand + gravel in cement), wood (cellulose fibres in lignin).
  6. Metals are made of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons, giving them high electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability, ductility, high density and high melting points.
  7. Density is lowest in polymers, medium in ceramics and composites, and highest in metals — the underlying reason is how heavy the atoms are and how closely they are packed.
  8. Electrical conductivity is high in metals (delocalised electrons) and essentially zero in ceramics, glass, polymers and most composites.
  9. Hardness is highest in clay ceramics and alloys; brittleness is highest in ceramics and glass; metals and polymers deform rather than shatter.
  10. To choose a material for a job: list the properties the job requires, then use the table to eliminate any material that fails on a property, then name the survivor and justify with values from the table.
  11. Composites work because the matrix and reinforcement do different jobs — the fibres carry the load, the matrix transfers force into them and stops cracks spreading.
  12. In Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H), this spec point is examined with "Compare", "State" and "Describe" command words around a data table, often as a single-mark material-selection question (e.g. 1CH0/2F June 2021 Q1(b) — cable coating).
9.5.3.1 Effective Use of Materials F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The "effective use of materials" question is a data-handling task: you are given a properties table and must pick the material whose property profile matches the demands of a stated use.
  2. The two-step logic is: (a) decide which properties the job demands; (b) compare the data columns and choose the material that satisfies all the demands, not just one.
  3. Full-mark answers quote specific numerical values from the data table, not just words like "high" or "low".
  4. Common properties used: density, melting point, electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, hardness, tensile strength, corrosion resistance, cost.
  5. Cable coatings need an electrical insulator (polymer like PVC), not a conductor — this is the canonical 1CH0/2F June 2021 Q1(b) example.
  6. Water pipes, window frames and outdoor uses prioritise corrosion resistance plus low cost — polymers (PVC) usually win.
  7. Saucepan bases need high thermal conductivity (metals); saucepan handles need low thermal conductivity (polymers). The same kitchen item uses two opposite property profiles.
  8. Aerospace panels prioritise low density plus high strength — aluminium alloys and carbon-fibre composites; steel is too heavy, titanium too expensive for non-critical parts.
  9. Once two materials both meet the technical demands, cost is the tie-breaker — but only after the technical properties are satisfied.
  10. Common errors: picking on one property only, confusing "good conductor" with "good insulator", failing to rule out the wrong materials, using vague language without numbers.
  11. The command word matters: Name is 1 mark; Suggest is 1–2; Explain or Justify is 2–4 and needs explicit data-quoted reasoning.
  12. This spec point is Paper 2 only and Separate Chemistry (1CH0) only — it does not appear on Combined Science (1SC0) or on Paper 1.
