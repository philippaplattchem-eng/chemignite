Groups in the Periodic Table — Summary Sheet

6.1.1.1 Group Classification F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Elements in the same vertical column of the periodic table form a group and share similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons.
  2. Group 1 — alkali metals: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium. One outer-shell electron; form 1+ ions; react with water to give alkaline metal hydroxide solutions.
  3. Group 7 — halogens: fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine. Seven outer-shell electrons; form 1− ions called halides; exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂); react with metals to form salts.
  4. Group 0 — noble gases: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon. Full outer shell; exist as single atoms (monatomic); chemically very unreactive.
  5. Helium has 2 outer-shell electrons (a full first shell); the rest of Group 0 have 8 outer-shell electrons (a full outer shell).
  6. The label "0" in Group 0 reflects that there are no spare outer-shell electrons available for reaction — the shell is already full.
  7. Group 1 elements are metals; Group 7 and Group 0 elements are non-metals.
  8. A halide is the 1− ion (e.g. Cl⁻); a halogen is the Group 7 element (e.g. Cl₂). The two names are not interchangeable.
  9. To classify any element, look up its column number on the periodic table: column 1 → alkali metal, column 7 → halogen, column 0 → noble gas.
  10. Exam questions on this spec point are usually 1-mark recall items on Foundation Paper 2; the marks are awarded for the exact group name, so avoid vague answers like "gases" or wrong terms like "halides".
  11. Hydrogen is shown at the top of Group 1 on most periodic tables, but it is not an alkali metal — it is a non-metal.
  12. Knowing the group instantly tells you the number of outer-shell electrons, the type of ion formed, and the general reactivity, which is why group classification is so powerful in predicting chemistry.
6.1.1.2 Physical Properties in Group 1 F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Group 1 metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) are the alkali metals — soft, reactive metals on the far left of the periodic table.
  2. The two physical properties Edexcel requires you to recall (spec point 6.2) are: they are soft, and they have relatively low melting points.
  3. "Soft" means they can be cut with a knife, exposing a bright silvery surface that quickly tarnishes in air.
  4. "Relatively low melting point" means low compared with most other metals — sodium melts at 98 °C, caesium at only 28 °C, whereas iron melts above 1500 °C.
  5. They share the standard metallic features (shiny when freshly cut, good conductors of heat and electricity) — softness and low melting point are the unusual properties.
  6. The underlying reason for both properties is weak metallic bonding: each Group 1 atom only contributes one delocalised electron to the metallic lattice, so the attraction between ions and the electron sea is weaker than in most metals.
  7. Reactivity and melting point trend in opposite directions down the group (reactivity up, melting point down) — don't link them as cause and effect.
  8. The property changes are mechanical/physical — a soft Group 1 metal is still chemically very reactive (it's not "soft" in the sense of "mild").
  9. Group 1 metals are typically stored under oil — but that's to prevent reaction with air/water, not because of their physical properties.
  10. In Edexcel Paper 2, this content most often appears as a 1-mark MCQ with a "Which row…" stem, asking you to pick the row that correctly lists two Group 1 properties.
  11. If you're asked to state a property of Group 1, default to soft or relatively low melting point — these are the spec-named pair and will always score.
  12. This SP feeds directly into 6.1.1.4 Trends in Group 1 (how the property values change down the group) and contrasts with 1.5.6.1 Properties of Metals (typical metallic behaviour).
6.1.1.3 How Alkali Metals React with Water F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. All Group 1 metals (Li, Na, K) react with cold water to give a metal hydroxide solution + hydrogen gas: 2M (s) + 2H₂O (l) → 2MOH (aq) + H₂ (g).
  2. The metal hydroxide solution is alkaline — it turns universal indicator purple and red litmus blue.
  3. The hydrogen gas gives a squeaky pop with a lit splint.
  4. Lithium reacts steadily — floats, fizzes, no flame, gradually disappears.
  5. Sodium reacts vigorously — floats, melts into a silvery ball, whizzes around, may produce a lilac flame.
  6. Potassium reacts very vigorously — melts, whizzes, always ignites the hydrogen with a lilac flame, may pop or spit.
  7. Reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily.
  8. Rubidium and caesium are predicted to react even more violently — likely explosively — using the same trend.
  9. When describing observations, give one distinct observation per mark — synonyms like fizzing and effervescence only score once (1CH0/2H — 2019 June Examiner Report).
  10. Common 2-mark "describe" questions and 6-mark extended-response questions on Group 1 with water both appear on Paper 2 (1CH0/2F and 1CH0/2H).
  11. Practise writing the balanced symbol equation with state symbols for each of Li, Na and K reacting with water — these are commonly examined directly.
  12. The reactions are exothermic — the heat released is what melts sodium and potassium into balls and ignites the hydrogen gas.
6.1.1.4 Trends in Group 1 F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The alkali metals (Group 1) get more reactive as you go down the group.
  2. The three metals named in the spec are lithium, sodium and potassium — you must know how each reacts with water.
  3. All Group 1 metals react with water to form a metal hydroxide + hydrogen: 2 M + 2 H₂O → 2 MOH + H₂.
  4. The resulting metal hydroxide solution is alkaline (turns universal indicator purple) — this is the origin of the name "alkali metals".
  5. Lithium fizzes gently, floats and gradually disappears.
  6. Sodium melts into a shiny ball (low melting point + exothermic reaction), moves around the surface, fizzes more vigorously than lithium.
  7. Potassium reacts very vigorously; the hydrogen ignites and burns with a lilac flame.
  8. Using the trend, you can predict that rubidium and caesium react even more violently than potassium, with caesium reacting explosively.
  9. The reactivity trend exists because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded as you go down the group, so it is lost more easily.
  10. In "describe what you would see" questions, two synonyms (e.g. fizzing and effervescence) only score one mark — always include observations from different categories (gas, movement, ignition, fate of metal, solution).
  11. Typical mark tariffs: 2 marks for predicting a single metal's behaviour, up to 6 marks for an extended question covering all of Li → Cs.
  12. The most-tested command words here are describe and predict — practise writing both styles of answer.
6.1.2.1 Group 1: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Every Group 1 atom has one electron in its outer shell — that is why all Group 1 metals react by losing one electron to form a 1+ ion.
  2. Reactivity in Group 1 increases down the group (Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs).
  3. Going down the group, the number of occupied shells increases (Li: 2 shells, Na: 3, K: 4).
  4. The outer electron is therefore further from the nucleus in the heavier alkali metals.
  5. There are also more inner-shell electrons providing shielding between the nucleus and the outer electron.
  6. The electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker in the heavier alkali metals.
  7. So the outer electron is lost more easily, which is why reactivity increases down the group.
  8. Although nuclear charge also increases down the group, the effects of greater distance and more shielding outweigh it.
  9. To score full marks, refer specifically to the outer electron — not "electrons" in general.
  10. Refer to the number of shells, not "more outer shells" — every Group 1 atom has just one outer shell.
  11. A typical 3-mark answer compares shell counts, links to distance/shielding, and concludes that the outer electron is lost more easily.
  12. This is the mirror-image idea to Group 7, where reactivity decreases down the group because atoms there gain an electron rather than lose one.
6.2.1.1 Physical Properties in Group 7 F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The halogens are Group 7 of the periodic table; the three you must recall by colour and state are chlorine, bromine and iodine.
  2. All halogens exist as diatomic molecules (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂) held together by a covalent bond.
  3. At room temperature: chlorine is a yellow-green gas, bromine is a red-brown liquid, iodine is a grey-black solid.
  4. Iodine sublimes when gently warmed and gives a purple vapour.
  5. Going down Group 7, melting point and boiling point increase, and the state changes from gas → liquid → solid.
  6. The trend in melting and boiling points is caused by intermolecular forces becoming stronger as molecules get larger — not by stronger covalent bonds.
  7. More energy is needed to overcome the stronger intermolecular forces, so melting and boiling points rise.
  8. Colour gets darker down the group: pale yellow fluorine → yellow-green chlorine → red-brown bromine → grey-black iodine → very dark astatine.
  9. To predict the melting or boiling point of a halogen not in a data table, look at the jumps between consecutive elements and continue the same-sized jump (Maths Skills 1d and 2c).
  10. Astatine, sitting below iodine, is predicted to be a dark solid with a melting point well above 100 °C (often estimated around 200–300 °C).
  11. The Group 7 melting-point trend is the opposite direction to the Group 1 trend — be careful when comparing groups.
  12. Mark schemes are strict on colours: vague answers like "dark" or "coloured" do not score; use the specific colour words yellow-green, red-brown, grey-black, purple vapour.
6.2.1.2 Testing Chlorine F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The chemical test for chlorine gas (Cl₂) is to hold damp blue litmus paper in the gas; it turns red and then is bleached white.
  2. The bleached white observation is the part of the answer that proves the gas is specifically chlorine — turning red alone is not enough.
  3. The paper must be damp because chlorine has to dissolve in water on the paper to form hydrochloric acid (HCl) and chloric(I) acid (HOCl); the HOCl is the bleaching agent.
  4. Chlorine is the diatomic gas Cl₂; chloride is the dissolved Cl⁻ ion. They have completely different tests — never use silver nitrate to test for chlorine gas, and never use damp litmus to test for chloride ions.
  5. The test for chloride ions (a different spec point) is dilute nitric acid followed by silver nitrate solution, giving a white precipitate of silver chloride.
  6. Other acidic gases (HCl, SO₂, CO₂) also turn damp blue litmus paper red, but only chlorine bleaches it.
  7. Do not confuse the chlorine test with the carbon dioxide test (limewater turning milky) — this confusion was specifically called out in the 1CH0/2H 2019 June examiner report.
  8. The question appears as a 2-mark "Describe" question in the middle of Paper 2, at both Foundation (e.g. 1CH0/2F 2024 Q9(b)) and Higher tier (e.g. 1CH0/2H 2019 Q9(a)).
  9. A full-mark answer needs two pieces of information: the apparatus/condition (damp blue litmus paper) and the positive result (bleached/turns white).
  10. The test is the same regardless of tier or qualification (1CH0 or 1SC0) — there is no Higher-only extension for this spec point.
  11. Chlorine itself is a pale yellow-green gas; if a question describes the gas as colourless, it cannot be chlorine even if it turns damp blue litmus paper red.
  12. Read the stem carefully: if you are told the substance is a gas, it is the element (chlorine); if it is in solution, it is the ion (chloride). Matching the test to the species is the single biggest mark-saver on this spec point.
6.2.2.1 Reactions with Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Halogens (Group 7: F, Cl, Br, I, At) react with metals to form metal halides, which are ionic salts.
  2. In every reaction the metal loses electrons to form a positive ion, and each halogen atom gains one electron to form a 1− halide ion.
  3. The halogen ending changes to -ide in the product (e.g. chlorine → chloride, bromine → bromide).
  4. With Group 1 metals the product has a 1:1 metal-to-halide ratio (e.g. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl); with Group 2 metals the ratio is 1:2 (e.g. Mg + Cl₂ → MgCl₂).
  5. Halogens exist as diatomic molecules (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂), so symbol equations always need balancing for the X₂ unit.
  6. Typical observations include a bright flame, white or coloured smoke, and a solid metal halide product; the coloured halogen gas or liquid is used up.
  7. Reactivity decreases down Group 7: fluorine reacts most vigorously, iodine least vigorously, with the same metal.
  8. The trend exists because going down the group the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is harder for a halogen atom to attract the extra electron it needs.
  9. You can predict the reaction of any halogen with a metal using the pattern: write the word equation, balance the charges (Group 1 → 1+, Group 2 → 2+, halide → 1−), then balance the symbol equation.
  10. Astatine, below iodine, is predicted to react very slowly with metals but would still form a metal astatide with the same formula pattern (e.g. 2Na + At₂ → 2NaAt).
  11. At Foundation tier you are most likely to see word equations and short recall ("State" / "Write") questions worth 1–2 marks; at Higher tier you should expect balanced symbol equations with state symbols and prediction tasks.
  12. Metal halides are ionic solids — they are not gases or liquids at room temperature, and their formula is determined by balancing the positive and negative charges.
6.2.2.2 Hydrogen Halides F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The halogens chlorine, bromine and iodine react with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides: HCl, HBr and HI.
  2. Hydrogen and all the halogens (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂, F₂) exist as diatomic molecules — always write them with a subscript 2 in equations.
  3. The balanced equations are H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr and H₂ + I₂ → 2HI.
  4. Hydrogen halides are colourless gases at room temperature and dissolve readily in water.
  5. When a hydrogen halide dissolves in water it releases H⁺ (aq) ions, producing an acidic solution with a pH below 7.
  6. The naming pattern is "hydro- + halide stem + -ic acid": HCl → hydrochloric acid, HBr → hydrobromic acid, HI → hydroiodic acid.
  7. The solution formed is not a hydroxide — "bromine hydroxide" is a common wrong answer; the correct name is hydrobromic acid.
  8. Reactivity with hydrogen decreases down Group 7: chlorine reacts explosively in sunlight, bromine needs a hot catalyst, iodine reacts only partially and reversibly.
  9. Using this pattern, fluorine (above chlorine) reacts even more vigorously to form HF, which gives hydrofluoric acid in water.
  10. Astatine (below iodine) is predicted to react very slowly with hydrogen, forming HAt and a weakly acidic solution.
  11. On Paper 2, expect short "State" questions (name the acid, 1 mark) or "Write" questions (balanced equation, 1–2 marks).
  12. Two recurring exam errors to avoid: writing reactants as monatomic (e.g. "H + Cl") and naming the acidic solution as a hydroxide.
6.2.2.3 Displacement Reactions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A displacement reaction is one in which a more reactive halogen takes the place of a less reactive halogen in a solution of its halide.
  2. Reactivity in Group 7 decreases down the group, so the order is chlorine > bromine > iodine > astatine.
  3. The halide solutions used in the test (KCl, KBr, KI) are all colourless before the halogen is added; any colour at the end of the experiment comes from the halogen that has been displaced.
  4. Chlorine is pale green/yellow, bromine is orange, and iodine in solution is brown — learn these colours, because identifying the displaced halogen by colour is how you earn observation marks.
  5. Chlorine displaces both bromide and iodide; bromine displaces iodide only; iodine displaces nothing — the triangular pattern of "yes" reactions is your evidence for the order.
  6. Potassium ions are spectator ions — they do not displace anything, and writing that "potassium displaced the halogen" is a marked error in examiner reports.
  7. Iodine is the least reactive of Cl/Br/I, not the most, even though it gives the most strongly coloured (brown) solutions; the brown colour means iodine was produced, so it was the loser in the reaction.
  8. Astatine is below iodine in Group 7, so it is predicted to be less reactive than iodine and will not displace chloride, bromide or iodide from their potassium halide solutions; it would itself be displaced by chlorine, bromine or iodine from a solution of potassium astatide.
  9. You must be able to write balanced symbol equations (e.g. Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂) and ionic equations (e.g. Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂) with correct (aq) state symbols.
  10. A "describe experiments" 6-marker on this spec point rewards: stating the reactants, giving the method, presenting a results table of expected observations, and a concluding sentence linking the pattern to the reactivity order.
  11. Practical experience matters — examiner reports note that candidates who had carried out the displacement reactions practically wrote far better answers; if you can, draw or visualise the colour changes as you revise.
  12. Copying the stem of the question without adding your own reasoning gains no credit; always link observation to rule to conclusion.
6.2.3.1 Halogen Displacement & Redox H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Halogen displacement reactions are redox reactions because electrons are transferred from the halide ion to the halogen molecule.
  2. OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
  3. The free halogen (X₂) is reduced — it gains electrons to form X⁻, making it the oxidising agent.
  4. The halide ion (X⁻) is oxidised — it loses an electron to form X₂, making it the reducing agent.
  5. For Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂: chlorine is reduced (Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻); bromide is oxidised (2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻).
  6. For Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂: chlorine is reduced; iodide is oxidised.
  7. For Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂: bromine is reduced; iodide is oxidised.
  8. The K⁺ ions are spectator ions — they appear unchanged on both sides and are removed from the ionic equation.
  9. Electrons must balance: the number lost in oxidation = the number gained in reduction.
  10. Reactivity decreases down Group 7 because each halogen atom becomes less able to attract an extra electron (less easily reduced).
  11. In an "Explain why this is a redox reaction" question, name both the oxidised species and the reduced species and refer to electron transfer.
  12. Examiners reward answers written from practical experience of the displacement reactions; do not just copy descriptive details from the question stem.
6.2.4.1 Group 7: Reactivity & Electronic Configurations F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Halogens (Group 7) all have 7 electrons in their outer shell, so they react by gaining one electron to form a 1− halide ion with a full outer shell.
  2. The electronic configurations you must know are F = 2,7; Cl = 2,8,7; Br = 2,8,18,7; I = 2,8,18,18,7.
  3. Reactivity decreases down Group 7 — fluorine is the most reactive, iodine the least.
  4. This is the opposite of the Group 1 trend, where reactivity increases down the group.
  5. The reason for the trend is that, going down the group, the outer shell is further from the nucleus and there is more shielding from inner-shell electrons.
  6. So the attraction between the nucleus and an incoming electron becomes weaker down the group, and the halogen captures an electron less easily.
  7. Although nuclear charge also increases down the group, the effects of extra shells and extra shielding outweigh the extra protons.
  8. A halogen's reactivity is essentially a measure of how strongly it can pull in an extra electron — fluorine pulls hardest.
  9. The classic exam mistake is inverting the reasoning and treating Group 7 like Group 1; remember that gaining and losing electrons respond to shielding in opposite ways.
  10. Edexcel often asks this as an "Explain" question worth up to 6 marks, sometimes combined with Group 1, and expects you to state the configurations, mention gaining an electron, and refer to distance from the nucleus and shielding in your answer.
  11. Make sure your answer ends with a clear ranking or identification of the most/least reactive halogen — don't stop at the abstract trend.
  12. Keep reactivity (a chemical trend) separate from physical-property trends like boiling point (which increases down Group 7) — they're driven by different ideas.
6.3.1.1 Group 0 (Noble Gases) F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Group 0 (the noble gases) contains helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon — all on the far right of the periodic table.
  2. Noble gases are chemically inert: they do not normally react with any other element.
  3. The reason is that every noble gas atom has a full outer shell of electrons.
  4. A full outer shell is a stable electronic configuration, so the atom has no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons.
  5. Because chemical reactions involve electron transfer or sharing, noble gases do not react.
  6. Helium has the configuration 2 — its first shell is full at 2 electrons (not 8). It is still a noble gas.
  7. Neon has the configuration 2, 8 — full second shell.
  8. Argon has the configuration 2, 8, 8 — full third shell (at GCSE level).
  9. Other elements react because losing, gaining or sharing electrons lets them reach the same stable configuration as a noble gas.
  10. For a 2-mark "explain" question, both marks are usually: (i) full outer shell, and (ii) does not need to lose / gain / share electrons (so does not react).
  11. The command word state typically asks for a 1-mark recall; explain typically asks for a 2-mark reasoned answer linking electrons to inertness.
  12. Inertness from 6.14 is the underlying reason for the uses of noble gases covered in 6.3.1.2 — always link the electron explanation to the practical context if the question asks about a use.
6.3.1.2 Using Noble Gases F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The noble gases are inert because they have a full outer electron shell, so they do not need to gain, lose or share electrons.
  2. The three properties the spec asks you to link to uses are inertness, low density, and non-flammability.
  3. Helium is used in balloons and airships because it is less dense than air (so the balloon rises) and non-flammable (so it is safer than hydrogen).
  4. Helium is also used as a liquid coolant in MRI scanners, where its inertness and very low boiling point are the relevant features.
  5. Argon fills filament light bulbs because it is inert — it does not react with the hot tungsten filament, so the filament lasts much longer than it would in air.
  6. Argon is used as a shielding gas in welding because, being inert, it stops oxygen and nitrogen in the air reacting with the hot molten metal.
  7. Neon is used in advertising signs and indicator lights because it is inert (so the gas is not consumed) and glows red-orange when a high voltage is passed through it.
  8. Krypton and xenon are used in some specialist lighting (flash photography, car headlights) for the same combination of inertness and characteristic emitted light.
  9. Only helium is used for its low density at GCSE level — every other noble gas use is justified by inertness (sometimes with non-flammability).
  10. For an "Explain why…" question, always give both the property and the consequence — for example, "argon is inert, so it does not react with the hot filament".
  11. Common mark losses come from naming the property without linking it to the use, or from confusing inert (does not react) with non-flammable (does not burn).
  12. On 1CH0 Paper 2 this spec point usually appears as a 1- or 2-mark short-answer question — keep your responses concise and explicit about the property–use link.
6.3.1.3 Trends in Group 0 F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Going down Group 0, relative atomic mass, density and boiling point all increase smoothly with no anomalies.
  2. The noble gases are monatomic — they exist as single atoms held together only by weak forces between atoms.
  3. Bigger atoms have more electrons, so the forces between them are stronger, so more energy is needed to boil them.
  4. Helium has the lowest density of any element — that is why helium balloons float.
  5. Argon is denser than air and very unreactive — used to shield hot welds and fill lamp bulbs.
  6. All Group 0 boiling points are negative °C — never quote a positive boiling point for a noble gas at GCSE.
  7. Interpolation means estimating a value between two known values; extrapolation means estimating a value beyond the known range.
  8. To predict a missing property, identify the two neighbours in the table and choose a value in the range between them (averaging works well).
  9. For a "suggest" question, any value strictly between the two neighbouring values, with the correct unit, will usually score the mark.
  10. The skill is flagged Maths 1d (orders of magnitude) and 2c (extracting information from tables) — common on Paper 2, 1 mark each.
  11. The canonical exam pattern is 1CH0/2F 2019 Q2(d): given a partial table of noble-gas densities, suggest the density of krypton (1 mark).
  12. The trends explain the uses of noble gases — low density (helium), high density and inertness (argon), low boiling point with bright emission (neon).
