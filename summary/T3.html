Chemical Changes — Summary Sheet

3.1.1.1 Defining Acids & Bases F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An acid is a substance that releases (is a source of) hydrogen ions, H+, when dissolved in water — e.g. HCl (aq) → H+ (aq) + Cl− (aq).
  2. An alkali is a soluble base that releases (is a source of) hydroxide ions, OH−, when dissolved in water — e.g. NaOH (aq) → Na+ (aq) + OH− (aq).
  3. The acid only behaves as an acid in solution (i.e. once water is present) — pure dry HCl gas does not turn dry litmus red.
  4. A base is any substance that neutralises an acid (reacts with H+). All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis — some bases (e.g. copper(II) oxide) are insoluble in water.
  5. Common laboratory acids: hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃). Common alkalis: sodium hydroxide (NaOH), potassium hydroxide (KOH), calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂), ammonia solution.
  6. Sulfuric acid releases two H+ ions per molecule; calcium hydroxide releases two OH− ions per formula unit.
  7. The ionic equation for neutralisation is always the same: H+ (aq) + OH− (aq) → H₂O (l). The spectator ions form the salt.
  8. When asked to explain why an acid is acidic, you must name hydrogen ions (H+) explicitly — saying "it dissociates" alone is not enough (November 2020 examiner report).
  9. When asked to explain a pH change during neutralisation, refer to H+ reacting with OH− (i.e. the neutralisation reaction itself), not just changing ion concentrations (June 2019 examiner report).
  10. Acidic solutions have pH below 7; alkaline solutions have pH above 7; pure water and neutral solutions have pH 7.
  11. The "real" species in acidic solution is the hydronium ion, H₃O+ — but at GCSE level you can simply write H+ (aq).
  12. The H+/OH− definition is the foundation of the whole acids topic — pH, indicators, neutralisation, titration, and reactions of acids all rely on it.
3.1.1.2 The pH Scale F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The pH scale runs from 0 to 14 and measures how acidic or alkaline an aqueous solution is.
  2. A neutral solution has a pH of exactly 7 — examples include pure water and sodium chloride solution.
  3. Acidic solutions have a pH lower than 7; the lower the pH, the more acidic the solution.
  4. Alkaline solutions have a pH higher than 7; the higher the pH, the more alkaline the solution.
  5. The lower the pH, the higher the concentration of H⁺ ions in the solution; the higher the pH, the higher the concentration of OH⁻ ions.
  6. Universal indicator changes colour across the scale: red (strong acid) → orange → yellow → green (neutral) → blue → purple (strong alkali).
  7. The pH of a solution can be measured with universal indicator (colour comparison) or a pH probe / meter (digital, more accurate, works on coloured solutions).
  8. A salt solution like NaCl (aq) is neutral because the H⁺ from the acid and OH⁻ from the alkali combined during neutralisation, leaving only the salt and water.
  9. (Higher tier) The pH scale is logarithmic — each pH unit represents a tenfold change in H⁺ concentration.
  10. When asked to "give the pH" of a neutral solution, the answer is 7 — not "around 7" or "neutral".
  11. Common Paper 1 exam style: 1-mark recall ("Give the pH…") or graph-reading ("Use the graph to find the pH…").
  12. The pH scale links directly to indicators, neutralisation, and the chemistry of acids and bases — make sure you can identify acidic/neutral/alkaline at a glance from any pH value given.
3.1.1.3 Indicators F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An indicator is a substance that changes colour in acidic or alkaline solution; the three named for Edexcel GCSE are litmus, methyl orange and phenolphthalein.
  2. Litmus is red in acid, purple in neutral, and blue in alkali — usually used as red or blue litmus paper for quick acid/alkali tests.
  3. Methyl orange is red in acid and yellow in alkali, passing through orange around pH 4 — used in titrations where the end-point is acidic.
  4. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in alkali, changing around pH 9 — used in titrations where the end-point is alkaline.
  5. Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators that gives a continuous range of colours from red (strong acid) through green (neutral) to purple (strong alkali); it is used to estimate pH.
  6. To measure pH accurately you use a pH probe (electronic pH meter), not litmus paper and not universal indicator.
  7. Litmus paper only tells you acid/neutral/alkali — it does not give a pH value.
  8. Indicators change colour because their protonated and deprotonated forms have different colours; high H⁺ concentration shifts the colour toward the acid form, high OH⁻ concentration shifts it toward the alkali form.
  9. In exam answers, always give both starting and final colour when asked for a colour change — naming only one end usually loses a mark.
  10. Methyl orange and phenolphthalein are used in titrations because they give a sharp single colour change; universal indicator is not suitable for titrations because the change is gradual.
  11. Indicator questions on 1CH0 Paper 1 typically appear embedded inside titration or neutralisation contexts as 1–2 mark short-answer or table-completion items, using the command words "State" or "What colour".
  12. Examiner feedback from 2019 (Higher tier) explicitly told candidates to learn the colours — there is no way around pure recall on this spec point.
3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The five solubility rules are: (a) all sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble; (b) all nitrates are soluble; (c) chlorides are soluble except silver and lead; (d) sulfates are soluble except lead, barium and calcium; (e) carbonates and hydroxides are insoluble except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium.
  2. Rules (a) and (b) have no exceptions — these are the easiest to apply.
  3. Rule (e) is inverted relative to the others — the default for carbonates and hydroxides is insoluble.
  4. To predict a precipitate, write the two possible products formed by swapping partners and check each against the rules; if either is insoluble, that compound is the precipitate.
  5. Common precipitates to recognise: AgCl (white), BaSO₄ (white), CaCO₃ (white), Cu(OH)₂ (pale blue), PbI₂ (yellow).
  6. An ionic equation shows only the ions that combine to form the precipitate; spectator ions are cancelled out.
  7. The choice of salt-preparation method depends directly on solubility: titration for soluble Na/K/NH₄ salts, insoluble-base method for other soluble salts, precipitation for insoluble salts.
  8. Examiners flagged in June 2019 that candidates mixed up copper oxide and copper carbonate — both are insoluble bases that react with acid to give a blue solution, but only the carbonate fizzes (releases CO₂).
  9. When asked to explain a solubility difference, frame the answer in terms of ions being free to move (soluble → aqueous) versus locked in a lattice (insoluble → solid).
  10. State symbols are essential — every equation involving these rules should show (aq) for soluble species and (s) for insoluble ones, with a space between the formula and the state symbol.
  11. Typical mark tariffs are 2–4 marks, with "Describe" and "Explain" the most common command words.
  12. Solubility rules underpin qualitative tests for halide ions (silver nitrate → AgCl/AgBr/AgI precipitates) and sulfate ions (barium chloride → BaSO₄ precipitate) — they are not a stand-alone topic but a tool used across the whole of Section 3 and the qualitative analysis sections.
3.1.11.1 Preparing an Insoluble Salt F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An insoluble salt cannot be made by neutralising an acid in solution — you must use a precipitation reaction between two soluble salts.
  2. Identify whether a salt is insoluble using the solubility rules from 3.1.10.1 Solubility Rules — only then choose the precipitation method.
  3. Step 1 — mix two aqueous solutions whose ions combine to give the insoluble salt as a precipitate.
  4. Step 2 — filter the mixture; the precipitate is the residue on the filter paper, the soluble by-product is in the filtrate.
  5. Step 3 — wash the residue with distilled water (not tap water) to remove soluble impurities.
  6. Step 4 — dry the residue in a warm oven, a desiccator, or in air to obtain a pure, dry sample.
  7. Example: BaCl₂ (aq) + Na₂SO₄ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s) + 2 NaCl (aq); ionic equation Ba²⁺ (aq) + SO₄²⁻ (aq) → BaSO₄ (s).
  8. Example: Pb(NO₃)₂ (aq) + 2 KI (aq) → PbI₂ (s) + 2 KNO₃ (aq); ionic equation Pb²⁺ (aq) + 2 I⁻ (aq) → PbI₂ (s).
  9. Choose reactants so that both starting solutions are soluble AND the by-product remains soluble in the filtrate — usually a sodium, potassium, ammonium or nitrate salt is a safe partner.
  10. Distinguish the three salt-preparation routes: acid + insoluble reactant (filter excess, evaporate filtrate); titration (find exact volumes, evaporate); precipitation (filter, wash, dry).
  11. Never use tap water for washing — it contains ions that would contaminate the pure salt.
  12. Typical exam ask: "Describe" question, 2–4 marks, one mark per step (mix / filter / wash / dry), often coupled with obtaining the soluble by-product by evaporation.
3.1.2.1 Hydrogen Ions & pH H only
Key Takeaways
  1. This spec point is Higher tier only and is examined on Paper 1 (1CH0/1H) for both Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0).
  2. The pH scale measures the concentration of hydrogen ions, H⁺ (aq), in a solution: the higher [H⁺], the lower the pH.
  3. For alkaline solutions, the higher the [OH⁻], the higher the pH — because [H⁺] and [OH⁻] are linked through the water equilibrium and rise/fall in opposite directions.
  4. The pH scale is logarithmic: each pH unit corresponds to a tenfold (×10) change in [H⁺].
  5. A pH change of n units corresponds to an [H⁺] change of 10ⁿ — for example, a pH change of 3 units means a 1,000-fold change in [H⁺].
  6. pH down by 1 = [H⁺] up by ×10; pH up by 1 = [H⁺] down by ×10.
  7. In exam questions, work out the pH difference first, then raise 10 to that power, then check the direction demanded by the wording.
  8. When writing about pH changes during a reaction, always refer to hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions by name — not "atoms", "particles", or just "acid" (a common error flagged by examiners on 1CH0/1H, June 2019).
  9. State that H⁺ ions are reacting with OH⁻ ions or being neutralised — describing the chemical action scores more marks than just saying "there are fewer of them".
  10. Questions on this spec point are typically 1-mark MCQs or short "State…" / "By what factor" items found in the mid-to-late sections of Paper 1 Higher.
  11. Common pH-to-factor conversions to memorise: 1 unit = ×10, 2 units = ×100, 3 units = ×1000, 4 units = ×10,000.
  12. A solution at pH 1 has 10 times more H⁺ ions than a solution at pH 2, and 1,000,000 times more H⁺ ions than a solution at pH 7.
3.1.3.1 Core Practical: Investigating pH F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The practical investigates the change in pH when powdered calcium hydroxide or calcium oxide is added to a fixed volume of dilute hydrochloric acid.
  2. Both bases react with HCl to form calcium chloride and water; the reaction is neutralisation, removing H⁺ ions from solution.
  3. As base is added, the pH increases because the concentration of H⁺ ions falls — saying only "the pH goes up" is not enough at Higher tier.
  4. The plot of pH against mass (or spatula measures) of base added is an S-shaped curve: flat-low, steep through the equivalence point, then flat-high around pH 12.
  5. The pH plateaus near 12 because calcium hydroxide is only sparingly soluble — adding more base does not raise the pH further.
  6. Independent variable = mass/spatula measures of base; dependent variable = pH; controls include acid volume, acid concentration, particle size, and stirring time.
  7. A pH probe is more accurate than universal indicator paper, which only gives whole-number pH values from a colour chart.
  8. Graph-plotting marks reward correct axis choice, sensible scales using more than half the grid, accurate point plotting, and a smooth curve of best fit — not dot-to-dot straight lines.
  9. Magnesium hydroxide and barium hydroxide are valid exam variants of the same practical — method, variables, and graph shape are unchanged.
  10. The reaction with calcium oxide is exothermic; add slowly and wear goggles to avoid splashes from the warming mixture.
  11. To gain the top explanation mark, you must state that the concentration of H⁺ ions decreases as base is added — not just that the pH changes.
  12. Do not copy wording (e.g. "in excess") straight from the question stem; examiners give no credit for restating the stem without chemistry.
3.1.4.1 Acid Strength & Concentration H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Concentration describes the amount of acid dissolved per unit volume of solution (mol/dm³ or g/dm³); strength describes the fraction of dissolved acid molecules that dissociate into ions.
  2. Concentration and strength are independent — all four combinations (dilute strong, concentrated strong, dilute weak, concentrated weak) are possible.
  3. A strong acid fully dissociates in water to release all of its hydrogen ions: HCl (aq) → H⁺ (aq) + Cl⁻ (aq). Use a single arrow.
  4. A weak acid only partially dissociates in water, setting up an equilibrium: CH₃COOH (aq) ⇌ H⁺ (aq) + CH₃COO⁻ (aq). Use an equilibrium arrow (⇌).
  5. Common strong acids: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃. Common weak acids: ethanoic, citric, carbonic.
  6. At the same concentration, a strong acid has a lower pH (more H⁺) than a weak acid, because more of its molecules have dissociated to release H⁺.
  7. The pH of any acid solution depends on the concentration of H⁺ ions present, which depends on both the concentration of the acid and its strength.
  8. At the same concentration, a strong acid reacts faster than a weak acid (more H⁺ available), but the eventual amount of product is the same if the moles of acid are equal.
  9. To score full marks on a "strong acid" definition, you must say it fully dissociates AND that it produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) — the second half is where most marks are lost.
  10. To score full marks on a "dilute" definition, you must say a small amount of acid AND in a large volume of water (or per unit volume of solution).
  11. Strength is a fixed property of the acid; concentration can be changed by adding or removing water.
  12. This spec point is Higher tier only and appears on Paper 1 (1CH0/1H), typically as a 2–4 mark "Explain" question.
3.1.5.1 Bases F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A base is any substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only (spec 3.9).
  2. An alkali is a soluble base — a base that dissolves in water to release hydroxide ions, OH⁻ (aq) (spec 3.10).
  3. Every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali. Solubility is the test that separates the two.
  4. The general reaction is acid + base → salt + water, and the name of this reaction is neutralisation.
  5. Common alkalis include sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide (limewater), and ammonia solution.
  6. Common insoluble bases include copper(II) oxide, iron(III) oxide, and most transition-metal hydroxides — these are bases but not alkalis.
  7. Metal carbonates also act as bases: they neutralise acids to give a salt, water, and carbon dioxide.
  8. The ionic equation for any acid–alkali neutralisation reduces to H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l).
  9. State symbols matter: alkalis are (aq), insoluble bases such as CuO are (s), and water produced in neutralisation is (l).
  10. Ammonia (NH₃) is unusual — it has no OH⁻ ion in its formula but it forms an alkaline solution and gives ammonium salts (NH₄⁺) when neutralised.
  11. The 2019 Higher Paper 1 examiner report flagged candidates failing to identify acid + base reactions as neutralisation — always use the word "neutralisation" when the command word is "state", "give", or "name".
  12. Questions on this spec point are usually 1-mark recall items, often embedded inside larger questions on salt preparation, pH, or equation balancing.
3.1.6.1 Reactions of Acids F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Acids react with metals to give a salt and hydrogen; the squeaky-pop test confirms the gas.
  2. Acids react with metal oxides to give a salt and water; the solid dissolves and the solution often changes colour.
  3. Acids react with metal hydroxides to give a salt and water; soluble hydroxides are alkalis and the reaction has no visible solid change.
  4. Acids react with metal carbonates to give a salt, water and carbon dioxide; CO₂ turns limewater milky.
  5. The salt's anion always comes from the acid: HCl → chloride, H₂SO₄ → sulfate, HNO₃ → nitrate.
  6. A neutralisation reaction is a reaction between an acid and a base, producing a salt and water and releasing heat.
  7. Acid + metal is not a neutralisation — a metal is not a base.
  8. The essential ionic equation for every acid–alkali neutralisation is H⁺ (aq) + OH⁻ (aq) → H₂O (l).
  9. To derive the ionic equation, split aqueous species into ions and cancel the spectator ions that appear unchanged on both sides.
  10. Always include correct state symbols ((s), (l), (g), (aq)) and balance metals → other non-metals → H → O.
  11. When asked for an observation, describe what you would see (bubbling, solid dissolving, colour change) — not the underlying chemistry.
  12. Build the formula of an unfamiliar salt from the metal cation and the acid anion; never copy a reactant into the product side of an equation.
3.1.7.1 Test for Hydrogen & Carbon Dioxide F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The test for hydrogen uses a lit splint held at the mouth of the test tube — the positive result is a squeaky pop.
  2. A "glowing splint" is the test for oxygen, not hydrogen — confusing the two is the single biggest cause of lost marks on this spec point.
  3. The chemistry behind the hydrogen test is the rapid combustion 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, which produces a sharp pressure pulse heard as the pop.
  4. The test for carbon dioxide uses limewater (calcium hydroxide solution, Ca(OH)₂ (aq)) — the positive result is the limewater turning cloudy / milky.
  5. The balanced equation for the CO₂ test is Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O — the cloudiness comes from the insoluble white CaCO₃ precipitate.
  6. A 2-mark "Describe" question on either test needs two separated marking points: the method/reagent and the positive observation.
  7. The four GCSE gas tests (hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, chlorine) should be revised together as a comparison table — confusing the reagents loses marks.
  8. Chlorine is tested with damp blue litmus paper, which is bleached white — do not confuse this with the CO₂ test.
  9. Examiner reports highlight that the method is part of the marking point: a wrong method (e.g. glowing splint instead of lit splint) blocks any marks for the result, even if the result phrase is correct.
  10. CO₂ extinguishes a flame, but this is not the named spec test — limewater is. Always default to limewater on CO₂ questions.
  11. Acceptable wording for the CO₂ result is "cloudy" or "milky" — both score. "Turns white" alone is risky.
  12. These tests appear most often as early-paper, 1–2 mark questions on Paper 1, frequently inside a wider question about reactions of acids with metals or carbonates.
3.1.8.1 Core Practical: Preparing Copper Sulfate F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The reaction is CuO (s) + H₂SO₄ (aq) → CuSO₄ (aq) + H₂O (l) — an insoluble metal oxide neutralising a dilute acid to give a soluble salt plus water.
  2. The acid is warmed gently using a water bath for safety, even heating, and temperature control — direct Bunsen heating risks spitting and bumping.
  3. Copper(II) oxide is added a little at a time and stirred until it is in excess — visible as unreacted black solid in the bottom of the beaker.
  4. Adding excess insoluble base guarantees that all the acid reacts, so no acid contaminates the final product.
  5. The excess unreacted copper oxide is removed by filtration; the blue copper sulfate solution passes through as the filtrate.
  6. Because acid + metal oxide → salt + water, and the excess oxide has been filtered out, the filtrate contains only salt and water — nothing else to contaminate the crystals.
  7. Heat the filtrate gently to concentrate it, then stop heating before the solution evaporates to dryness — this is the single most-penalised point in Edexcel exam reports.
  8. Leave the concentrated solution to cool slowly so large, regular blue crystals of hydrated copper(II) sulfate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) form.
  9. Dry the crystals between filter paper or in a warm place — do not dry them in an oven, as this can drive off the water of crystallisation.
  10. In the November 2021 examiner report, candidates lost marks for saying "evaporate all the water" and for omitting the slow cooling stage — always include both "concentrate, do not evaporate to dryness" and "leave to cool and crystallise" in your answer.
  11. This method works only because copper oxide is insoluble and copper sulfate is soluble — that solubility difference is what makes filtration a useful separation.
  12. The command word is almost always "Describe"; full procedure questions are worth up to 6 marks (e.g. 1CH0/1H November 2020 Q9(b)(ii)) while crystallisation-only questions are worth around 2 marks (e.g. 1CH0/1H June 2022 Q6(b)).
3.1.9.1 Prepare a Salt by Titration F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Use titration to prepare a soluble salt when both reactants (the acid and the base/alkali) are soluble — you cannot filter off an excess of a dissolved reactant.
  2. The aim of the titration is to find the exact volume of acid that neutralises a fixed volume of alkali, so the final salt solution contains no excess of either reactant.
  3. Apparatus must be named correctly: burette (delivers acid in measured drops), pipette + pipette filler (delivers a fixed volume of alkali), conical flask (holds the alkali), white tile (background for the colour change), evaporating basin (for Stage 2).
  4. A suitable indicator is phenolphthalein (colourless ↔ pink) or methyl orange (red ↔ yellow). Universal indicator is not suitable because its colour change is gradual.
  5. Stage 1: pipette the alkali into a conical flask, add indicator, fill the burette with acid, record the initial reading, add acid drop-by-drop while swirling until the indicator changes colour, record the final reading. The volume added is the titre.
  6. Repeat the titration to obtain concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³) and take the mean — improves reliability.
  7. Stage 2: repeat the titration with the same volume of alkali and exactly the mean titre of acid, but without indicator — otherwise the dye contaminates the salt.
  8. Evaporate the resulting salt solution to the point of crystallisation, then allow it to cool slowly so crystals form. Filter or decant, then dry the crystals.
  9. The two stages must be linked in any extended-response answer — the June 2019 1F examiner report explicitly criticised candidates who described only one stage.
  10. Why not add the indicator in Stage 2: indicators are coloured organic compounds that would crystallise out with the salt and stop it being pure.
  11. Why evaporate to the point of crystallisation rather than to dryness: many soluble salts (including ammonium salts) decompose or lose water of crystallisation if heated too strongly.
  12. The titration calculation (c = n/v, molar ratios) is not required for this spec point — it sits with 5.9C / 5.10C (Separate Chemistry only). For 3.1.9.1 you only need to describe the method.
3.2.1.1 Electrolysis F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An electrolyte is an ionic compound that is either molten or dissolved in water — its ions must be free to move.
  2. Electrolysis is the use of electrical energy from a direct-current (d.c.) supply to decompose an electrolyte into simpler substances.
  3. Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity because their ions are locked in fixed positions in the lattice and cannot move.
  4. Molten or aqueous ionic compounds do conduct because their ions are free to drift through the liquid.
  5. The anode is the positive electrode; the cathode is the negative electrode. Remember PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.
  6. Cations are positively charged ions and migrate to the negative cathode.
  7. Anions are negatively charged ions and migrate to the positive anode.
  8. At the cathode, cations gain electrons (reduction). At the anode, anions lose electrons (oxidation).
  9. Inside the electrolyte, the current is carried by moving ions, not by electrons. Electrons only flow through the external wires.
  10. A direct current is essential — alternating current would swap the electrode charges every cycle and prevent decomposition.
  11. The June 2019 1CH0/1F examiner report identifies confusion over electrode and ion charges as the single biggest exam pitfall — label everything on the diagram before answering.
  12. To define electrolysis for 2 marks, you must mention both the use of electrical energy / d.c. supply and the decomposition of an electrolyte (molten or aqueous ionic compound).
3.2.2.1 Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Aqueous electrolysis is different from molten electrolysis because water itself partly ionises into H⁺ and OH⁻, so there are four ions competing — not two — and the discharge rules decide which one wins.
  2. Cathode rule: if the dissolved metal is more reactive than hydrogen, hydrogen gas (H₂) is produced; if it is less reactive than hydrogen, the metal is deposited.
  3. Anode rule: if a halide ion (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) is present in reasonable concentration, the halogen is produced; otherwise, oxygen is produced from OH⁻.
  4. Copper chloride, CuCl₂ (aq): copper at the cathode (Cu is below H in reactivity), chlorine at the anode.
  5. Sodium chloride, NaCl (aq): hydrogen at the cathode (Na is above H in reactivity), chlorine at the anode; the solution left behind is sodium hydroxide.
  6. Sodium sulfate, Na₂SO₄ (aq): hydrogen at the cathode, oxygen at the anode — net effect is electrolysis of water; the salt is not used up.
  7. Dilute sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄ (aq): hydrogen at the cathode, oxygen at the anode — again, net electrolysis of water; the acid is not used up.
  8. At the cathode the half-equation for hydrogen discharge is 2H⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂ (g); at the anode the half-equation for oxygen discharge is 4OH⁻ (aq) → O₂ (g) + 2H₂O (l) + 4e⁻.
  9. When water is electrolysed (cases c and d), you get twice the volume of hydrogen as oxygen because each H₂ molecule needs 2 electrons but each O₂ molecule needs 4.
  10. Test for hydrogen: squeaky pop with a lit splint. Test for oxygen: relights a glowing splint. Test for chlorine: damp blue litmus turns red, then bleaches white.
  11. Polyatomic anions like SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻ and CO₃²⁻ are not discharged at the anode because the central atom is already in its highest oxidation state; OH⁻ from water is oxidised instead.
  12. Trap from 1CH0/1H November 2021: if a question asks for the electrode reaction, write the half-equation and name the species — do not drift into a long discussion of reactivity unless the question explicitly asks why a metal is or is not discharged.
3.2.3.1 Electrolysis of Molten Compounds F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Electrolysis is the breakdown of an ionic compound using an electric current; a molten ionic compound conducts because its ions are free to move.
  2. The cathode is negative and attracts positive cations; the anode is positive and attracts negative anions.
  3. At the cathode, metal cations gain electrons (reduction) and form the pure metal element.
  4. At the anode, non-metal anions lose electrons (oxidation) and form the non-metal element — often a diatomic gas (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂, O₂).
  5. Molten electrolysis is simpler than aqueous electrolysis because no water is present, so only the metal and non-metal ions take part.
  6. Molten lead bromide (PbBr₂) is the demonstration named in spec 3.25e — done in a fume cupboard because Br₂ vapour is toxic.
  7. At the cathode of molten PbBr₂: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb. At the anode: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻. Overall: PbBr₂ → Pb + Br₂.
  8. Inert graphite electrodes are used so the electrodes themselves do not react with the products.
  9. The same rules apply to any binary ionic compound: e.g. NaCl → Na + Cl₂; ZnO → Zn + O₂; Al₂O₃ → Al + O₂; KI → K + I₂.
  10. Electrons gained at the cathode must equal electrons lost at the anode when half-equations are combined.
  11. Reactive metals such as Na, K, Mg, Ca and Al are extracted industrially by electrolysis of their molten compounds because they are too reactive to be reduced by carbon.
  12. Always check whether a compound is described as molten or aqueous before predicting products — the two situations give different answers.
3.2.4.1 Electrolysis & Redox H only
Key Takeaways
  1. Electrolysis is a redox process: at the cathode (negative electrode) reduction occurs, and at the anode (positive electrode) oxidation occurs.
  2. OILRIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain of electrons.
  3. Cations (positive ions) migrate to the cathode and are reduced; anions (negative ions) migrate to the anode and are oxidised.
  4. In a cathode half-equation, electrons appear on the left (gained). In an anode half-equation, electrons appear on the right (lost).
  5. The number of electrons in a half-equation equals the size of the ion's charge multiplied by the coefficient — e.g. 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ uses 2 electrons because two Br⁻ ions each lose one.
  6. Common cathode reductions to know: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na; 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂; Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu; Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb; Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Zn; Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al.
  7. Common anode oxidations to know: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻; 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻; 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻; 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻.
  8. Always check atoms balance first, then balance charge with electrons — and never write electrons as e²⁻ or subtract them from one side.
  9. According to the 1CH0/1H June 2019 examiner report, the five most common errors are: writing the reverse reaction, including chloride in a cathode equation, putting a −2 charge on the electron, writing "Zn2" without the ²⁺ superscript, and subtracting electrons rather than adding them.
  10. This spec point appears only on Paper 1 Higher (1CH0/1H), typically as a 2-mark "write" or "complete" half-equation question, with longer 3–4 mark explanations linking electrode polarity to electron transfer.
  11. Mathematical Skill 1c (ratios) underpins balancing the stoichiometric coefficients and the electrons in every half-equation.
  12. Remember the umbrella mnemonics: PANIC (Positive is ANode, Negative Is Cathode) for electrode polarity, and OILRIG for the electron direction of redox.
3.2.5.1 Core Practical: Electrolysis of Copper(II)Sulfate F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The core practical is the same electrolyte (CuSO₄ (aq)) electrolysed twice — once with inert (carbon) electrodes and once with copper electrodes — to compare the observations.
  2. The supply must be DC, otherwise polarity flips and nothing is deposited.
  3. With inert (carbon) electrodes: copper deposits on the cathode (mass increases), oxygen gas is given off at the anode, and the solution fades from blue to colourless.
  4. With copper electrodes: copper deposits on the cathode (mass increases), the copper anode dissolves (mass decreases), and the solution stays the same shade of blue.
  5. With copper electrodes, the mass gained at the cathode equals the mass lost at the anode, because copper is transferred through the solution.
  6. The solution stays blue with copper electrodes because Cu²⁺ ions are added at the anode at the same rate as they are removed at the cathode — Cu²⁺ concentration is constant.
  7. Measure mass by weighing each electrode dry, before and after, on a balance reading to at least 0.01 g; dry gently to avoid knocking off freshly deposited copper.
  8. Plot mass change against current (or time); the line is straight, passes through the origin, and its gradient gives the mass deposited per amp (or per second).
  9. Common candidate errors flagged in examiner reports: thinking both electrodes gain mass, confusing electrons with ions, and failing to explain why the solution stays blue with copper electrodes.
  10. Wear eye protection; use a low-voltage DC supply; copper(II) sulfate is an irritant and any added dilute acid is corrosive.
  11. Command words to expect: Draw the apparatus (2 marks), Describe the observations (2–3 marks), Explain in terms of ions (3–6 marks, often a 6-mark question).
  12. For 6-mark questions, pair each of the three observations (cathode change, anode change, solution colour) with its ion-and-electron explanation.
3.2.5.2 Explaining the Electrolysis of Copper(II)Sulfate F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. With copper electrodes, copper(II) sulfate solution undergoes electrolysis in which copper transfers from the anode to the cathode through the solution.
  2. Cathode half-equation: Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu (s) — the cathode gains mass as copper is deposited (reduction).
  3. Anode half-equation: Cu (s) → Cu²⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ — the anode loses mass as copper atoms dissolve (oxidation).
  4. The mass gained by the cathode equals the mass lost by the anode — copper transfers one-for-one across the cell.
  5. The blue colour of the solution does not fade, because Cu²⁺ ions are removed at the cathode at the same rate as they are added at the anode.
  6. This is fundamentally different from electrolysis with inert electrodes (graphite/platinum), where O₂ is produced at the anode, the blue colour fades, and the solution becomes acidic.
  7. The same process is used industrially to purify copper: impure copper is the anode, pure copper is the cathode, the electrolyte is copper(II) sulfate (acidified).
  8. More reactive impurities (Zn, Fe, Ni) dissolve into the solution but stay there as ions — they are not deposited on the cathode.
  9. Less reactive impurities (Ag, Au, Pt) do not dissolve. They drop from the anode and collect as anode sludge at the bottom of the cell — this is not impure copper, and is valuable enough to be sold separately.
  10. The refined copper at the cathode is over 99.99% pure, suitable for electrical wiring.
  11. Common exam errors flagged by examiners: claiming both electrodes gain mass; calling the anode sludge "impure copper"; confusing electrons and ions; failing to explain why the solution colour stays the same.
  12. Electrons flow through the external wires; ions move through the solution. The mass changes are caused by ions, not by the electrons themselves.
