States of Matter & Mixtures — Summary Sheet

2.1.1.1 The Three States of Matter F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The particle model describes substances as being made of tiny particles whose arrangement, movement and relative energy determine the state of matter.
  2. In a solid, particles are in a regular arrangement, all touching, vibrating about fixed positions; the forces between them are strongest and their relative energy is lowest.
  3. In a liquid, particles are in an irregular arrangement, mostly touching, sliding past each other; the forces are weaker than in a solid and the relative energy is greater than in a solid but less than in a gas.
  4. In a gas, particles are far apart in a random arrangement, moving quickly in random directions in straight lines; the forces between them are negligible and the relative energy is highest.
  5. Solids have a fixed shape and fixed volume because the particles cannot move from place to place; liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of the container; gases have neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume.
  6. The state a substance is in at a given temperature is decided by comparing the temperature with the melting and boiling points: below MP = solid, between MP and BP = liquid, above BP = gas.
  7. Particle-model diagrams must show particles as identical circles — regular and touching for a solid, irregular but touching for a liquid, far apart and random for a gas — with arrows indicating movement.
  8. The simple particle model is a useful but simplified picture: real particles are not solid spheres, they are not all the same size, and the gaps and forces are not drawn to scale (limitations developed in 1.5.7.1 Limitations of Models).
  9. State-of-matter questions almost always appear at the start of Foundation tier Paper 1 (e.g. 1CH0/1F June 2019 Q1(c) — identify the solid at 20 °C from a melting/boiling point table) and use the command words Give, Name, Use and Predict.
  10. When discussing the forces broken on changing state, you are always talking about the forces between particles, not the bonds inside them — covalent bonds inside molecules do not break on melting or boiling.
  11. Always describe energy in relative terms (greater than / less than another state), never as an absolute number — the spec wording is "relative energy".
  12. Examiners noted in the 1CH0/1F June 2019 report that candidates need to practise interpretation of heating and cooling curves, a linked skill developed in 2.1.1.2 Changes of State.
2.1.1.2 Interconversions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. There are six named interconversions between the three states of matter: melting (solid → liquid), freezing (liquid → solid), evaporating / boiling (liquid → gas), condensing (gas → liquid), sublimation (solid → gas), and deposition (gas → solid).
  2. Every change of state is a physical change — no new substance is made, mass and atoms are conserved, and the change can be reversed by reversing the temperature.
  3. A chemical change makes one or more new substances and is much harder to reverse — burning magnesium in oxygen is a chemical change; melting magnesium is a physical change.
  4. Heating changes (melting, evaporating/boiling, sublimation) absorb energy so that particles can overcome the forces holding them together. Cooling changes (freezing, condensing, deposition) release energy as those forces re-form.
  5. During melting and boiling the arrangement of particles becomes more disordered, the movement becomes faster and less restricted, and the energy of the particles increases.
  6. On a heating curve, the temperature rises during each sloping section (where one state is being heated) but stays constant during each flat plateau (where the substance is changing state).
  7. The plateaus are flat because the heat energy supplied is being used to break the forces between particles, not to make the particles move faster — so the temperature does not rise until the change of state is complete.
  8. The first plateau on a heating curve is melting (at the melting point); the second is boiling (at the boiling point). On a cooling curve, the first plateau is condensing and the second is freezing.
  9. Sublimation (solid → gas without melting) and deposition (gas → solid without first condensing to a liquid) are the two "skip the liquid" changes — examples are dry ice subliming and frost forming on a cold window.
  10. The June 2019 1CH0/1F examiner report explicitly recommends practising interpretation of heating and cooling curves — examiners want you to read off melting and boiling points from plateaus, not just recite the six names.
  11. Spec 2.3 maps onto Mathematical Skill 5b — being able to visualise and represent particle arrangements in 2D/3D and read off temperatures and states from a labelled heating or cooling curve.
  12. Questions on this spec point appear almost exclusively at the front of Foundation Paper 1 (Q1 or Q2) as 1-mark Give/Name questions — easy marks if the six names and their directions are learned cold.
2.1.1.3 Predicting Physical State F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Predicting physical state is a three-rule decision based on the stated temperature T and the melting point (mp) and boiling point (bp) of the substance.
  2. If T < mp, the substance is a solid. The temperature is too low to break the particles out of their fixed positions.
  3. If mp < T < bp, the substance is a liquid. There is enough energy to flow but not enough to separate into a gas.
  4. If T > bp, the substance is a gas. The particles can move freely apart and fill the container.
  5. At exactly the melting point or boiling point, the substance is changing state — both states are usually acceptable answers.
  6. Always compare temperatures on a number line — –183 °C is colder than –114 °C, not warmer. The negative sign matters.
  7. The smaller number in a "mp / bp" row is always the melting point; if the order looks reversed, check the column headings before trusting them.
  8. Mercury and bromine are the two famous liquid at room temperature exceptions — do not assume "metal = solid" without checking the data.
  9. The command words for this SP are nearly always predict or give the letter — keep the answer short and exactly in the format asked for.
  10. Mathematical skill 1d covers comparing numbers; 4a covers reading the same information off a heating curve, where the flat plateaus give the mp and bp.
  11. This spec point appears at the very front of Foundation Paper 1 (Q1 or Q2) as a 1-mark question — per the 1CH0/1F June 2019 Examiner Report, most candidates score the mark.
  12. The 30-second routine — read temperature, compare with mp, compare with bp, pick state, format the answer — should turn this into a guaranteed mark on every Paper 1 you sit.
2.2.1.1 Pure Substance vs Mixture F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. In everyday language, "pure" means nothing added or unadulterated (e.g. "pure orange juice"). In chemistry, "pure" means a sample of a single element or single compound only.
  2. A mixture contains two or more elements or compounds that are not chemically combined — the components can be present in any proportion and keep their own properties.
  3. Everyday examples like orange juice, sea water, air and steel are all mixtures in chemistry, even when there is "nothing added".
  4. Pure substances have fixed, characteristic physical properties — including a sharp melting point and a sharp boiling point.
  5. Pure substances melt at one specific temperature (a sharp melting point); mixtures melt over a range of temperatures.
  6. The reason is that in a pure substance every particle is identical and is held by identical forces, so the whole sample needs the same energy to melt; in a mixture, different particles need different amounts of energy and melt at slightly different temperatures.
  7. Adding impurities to a pure substance usually lowers and broadens its melting point.
  8. On a heating curve, a pure substance shows a flat horizontal plateau at the melting point, while a mixture shows a sloping line during melting.
  9. A sharp melting point only proves the sample is pure — to identify which pure substance it is, you must compare its melting point with a known literature value.
  10. Pure substances can usually only be broken apart by chemical reactions, whereas mixtures can usually be separated by physical methods (filtration, distillation, chromatography).
  11. Melting and boiling point measurements are used in the lab as a purity test — sharp transition at the literature value indicates a pure sample.
  12. When answering "State whether the sample is pure or impure and justify" questions (as in 1CH0/1H June 2023 Q9(a)(ii)), always pair the observation (sharp temperature vs range) with the conclusion (pure vs impure) — that pairing is what the mark scheme rewards.
2.2.2.1 Simple Distillation F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Simple distillation separates a dissolved solute from its solvent — for example, pure water from sea water — by evaporating the solvent and then condensing the vapour back to a pure liquid in a different part of the apparatus.
  2. It works whenever the components have a very large difference in boiling point, so that only the lower-boiling component enters the gas phase at the heating temperature used.
  3. The apparatus consists of a round-bottomed flask (with anti-bumping granules), a thermometer, a side-arm leading to a water-cooled condenser, and a beaker to catch the distillate. The collection end must be open to the atmosphere.
  4. The thermometer bulb sits at the side-arm exit so that it measures the temperature of the vapour about to enter the condenser, not the temperature of the boiling liquid. This is the most frequently examined detail of the entire spec point (1CH0/1F — June 2019 Examiner Report).
  5. The thermometer reading tells you the boiling point of whichever substance is currently distilling over — for pure water, it holds steady at about 100 °C.
  6. The condenser cools the vapour back to a liquid using counter-current cold water — water in at the bottom (near the collection beaker), out at the top (near the flask) — so the water jacket stays full and cooling stays efficient.
  7. Anti-bumping granules are added to the flask before heating to give smooth controlled boiling and prevent violent eruptions of vapour.
  8. The dissolved solid (e.g. salt) is left behind in the original flask because its boiling point is far higher than the heating temperature and it cannot enter the vapour phase.
  9. Do not heat to dryness and do not seal the receiving end — both errors can damage the apparatus.
  10. Choose simple distillation under spec 2.8 when the question gives you a solution and asks for the pure solvent. If the two components are both liquids with similar boiling points, use fractional distillation instead.
  11. A flawed-setup diagram-correction question (e.g. 1CH0/1F June 2024 Q3(c)(i), 2 marks) usually requires you to identify a misplaced thermometer, a reversed water flow through the condenser, or a sealed receiving end.
  12. On 2-mark "explain" answers, always give both the correction and the reason — e.g. "Move the thermometer bulb up to the side-arm exit so it measures the temperature of the water vapour."
2.2.2.2 Fractional Distillation F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Fractional distillation separates a mixture of two or more miscible liquids that have different boiling points — for example, ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C), or the many hydrocarbons in crude oil.
  2. The mixture is heated in a flask; the liquid with the lower boiling point evaporates first and rises up the fractionating column as a vapour.
  3. The fractionating column is the key piece of apparatus that distinguishes fractional from simple distillation. It is packed with glass beads (or fitted with plates) to provide a large surface area for vapour to evaporate and condense repeatedly.
  4. There is a temperature gradient along the column — hot at the bottom (near the flask), cooler at the top (near the thermometer).
  5. The higher-boiling-point liquid condenses lower down the column and drips back into the flask, while the lower-boiling-point vapour continues to rise and is collected at the top.
  6. The vapour passes into the condenser, where it is cooled by water flowing counter-current in the outer jacket, and condenses to a liquid (the distillate or fraction).
  7. The thermometer, mounted at the top of the column with its bulb level with the side-arm, signals which liquid is currently distilling — the reading stays close to that liquid's boiling point while it is being collected, then rises when the next fraction starts.
  8. The three concepts examiners want to see in a 3-mark answer are heating, evaporation and condensing, tied together by the idea of a temperature gradient (1CH0/1H — June 2019 Examiner Report).
  9. Always name the process ("fractional distillation") at the start of an extended answer — easy marks are lost when the technique is described but not named (1CH0/1H — June 2019 Examiner Report).
  10. The industrial application of this technique is the separation of crude oil into fractions in a refinery column — the same principle, scaled up, with many fractions tapped off at different heights up the column.
  11. Choose fractional distillation (not simple) whenever the mixture has two or more liquids that both evaporate and you need to separate them from each other — simple distillation is for separating a single solvent from a solution.
  12. The water flow through the condenser must go in at the bottom and out at the top (counter-current cooling) so that the condenser is at a uniform low temperature along its whole length.
2.2.2.3 Filtration F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid (or from a solution) by passing the mixture through filter paper.
  2. The filtrate is the liquid that passes through the paper; the residue is the solid left behind on the paper.
  3. Filter paper works because it is a size filter — pores let molecules and dissolved ions through, but trap solid particles.
  4. Filtration does not separate a dissolved solute from its solvent; dissolved particles pass straight through with the filtrate.
  5. The classic separation sand + salt uses filtration then crystallisation: filter out the sand, then evaporate the filtrate to recover the salt.
  6. The standard apparatus is: filter funnel, filter paper folded into a cone, and a conical flask (or beaker) to catch the filtrate.
  7. When drawing the apparatus, draw the funnel as open at the top, draw the filter paper clearly inside it, and label both the funnel and the paper separately.
  8. The most common errors flagged by the November 2020 Higher tier examiner report are closed funnels, missing filter paper and labelling the funnel as "filter".
  9. Filtration is also used to collect a precipitate from a precipitation reaction, then the residue is washed with fresh solvent and dried.
  10. There are no calculations for spec point 2.7 — filtration is assessed by description, diagrams and explanation only.
  11. Choose filtration only when one component is insoluble and the other is a liquid or a solution; if both are soluble, both are solids without a liquid, or both are liquids, filtration will not work.
  12. Use the keywords dissolve, filter, filter paper, filtrate and residue in your written answers — examiners reward the right vocabulary used in the right place.
2.2.2.4 Crystallisation F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Crystallisation separates a soluble solid from its solution by removing some of the solvent and then cooling the saturated solution so that pure crystals form.
  2. Use crystallisation when you want to recover the solid, not the solvent; if you want the solvent (e.g. pure water from sea water), use simple distillation instead.
  3. The procedure has six key stages: pour into evaporating basin → heat gently to concentrate → stop heating when crystals just start to form → leave to cool slowly → separate crystals from mother liquor by filtration or decanting → dry between filter papers or in a warm oven.
  4. The most heavily examined misconception is heating the solution to dryness. You must heat only until the solution is saturated — the November 2021 examiner report flagged this as the main reason candidates lost marks.
  5. The product is purer when the crystals grow slowly from a saturated solution as it cools, because slow cooling lets the particles arrange themselves regularly and excludes dissolved impurities; impurities stay in the mother liquor.
  6. A water bath gives gentler heating than a Bunsen burner and is safer for solutes that might decompose at higher temperatures.
  7. Drying is done by pressing crystals between filter papers or by leaving them in a warm oven on a low setting — either method scores credit (1CH0/1H, November 2020 examiner report).
  8. Crystallisation is almost always paired with filtration in salt-preparation experiments: filtration removes the insoluble impurity (such as excess metal oxide or carbonate), and crystallisation then recovers the dissolved salt from the filtrate.
  9. On the Foundation paper, crystallisation often appears as a 6-mark "describe in detail" question (e.g. June 2019 Q7(d), ammonium sulfate by titration + crystallisation) — these are levels-marked, so include every stage to climb into the top level.
  10. On the Higher paper, watch out for questions that say "obtain a pure dry sample of [named salt]". Check the solubility first: insoluble products (such as barium sulfate, May 2024) are recovered by filtration and washing; soluble products are recovered by crystallisation.
  11. Key words to use in your answer: "concentrate", "saturated", "cool slowly", "crystals", "filter (or decant)", "dry between filter papers (or in a warm oven)". Key words to avoid: "boil dry", "evaporate all the water".
  12. Crystallisation is universally Foundation + Higher and appears on Paper 1 only, for both the Separate Chemistry (1CH0) and Combined Science (1SC0) qualifications.
2.2.2.5 Paper Chromatography F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Paper chromatography separates soluble substances in a mixture; substances that do not dissolve in the chosen solvent will not move from the baseline.
  2. The mobile phase is the solvent that moves up the paper; the stationary phase is held within the chromatography paper. Each substance partitions between the two and travels at its own characteristic rate.
  3. Substances that are more soluble in the solvent and less strongly attracted to the paper travel further up the chromatogram.
  4. The baseline must be drawn in pencil — pencil graphite does not dissolve in the solvent, so it stays in place and does not contaminate the chromatogram.
  5. The sample spots must be placed above the solvent level, otherwise the sample dissolves straight into the solvent in the tank instead of being carried up the paper.
  6. The chromatography tank must be sealed to keep the atmosphere inside saturated with solvent vapour and prevent the solvent evaporating from the paper.
  7. The solvent front must be marked immediately in pencil before the paper dries, so that the distance the solvent travelled can be measured.
  8. A pure substance produces only one spot on a chromatogram in any solvent; a sample that produces multiple spots must be a mixture.
  9. Two substances with the same R_f value in the same solvent are the same substance — this is how unknown components are identified against reference samples. R_f calculations themselves are covered in 2.2.3.1 Interpreting Chromatograms.
  10. Edexcel examiners report (1CH0/1F — 2019 June) that candidates can recall the sequence of steps well — make sure the marks on procedural questions are secured before the exam.
  11. The 1CH0/1H 2020 November report flagged that candidates struggled to suggest improvements to the chromatography experiment — practise phrasing modifications such as narrower capillary tubes, longer run times, or a more suitable solvent.
  12. Common command words on this SP at Foundation include Identify and Which (1 mark); at Higher, Explain, Calculate and Suggest dominate (1–2 marks each).
2.2.3.1 Interpreting Chromatograms F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A pure substance produces one spot on a developed chromatogram; a mixture produces two or more spots, one for each component.
  2. The number of spots from a sample equals the number of components in that sample — so the sample showing the most spots contains the most components (e.g. the most dyes).
  3. An unknown substance is identified by running it on the same chromatogram, in the same solvent, at the same time as one or more known standards, and matching both Rf value and colour.
  4. The baseline is the pencil line on which spots are placed before development; the solvent front is the furthest distance the solvent has travelled when the chromatogram is removed.
  5. The Rf value is defined as: Rf = distance moved by substance / distance moved by solvent front, both measured from the baseline to the centre of the spot.
  6. Rf values are always between 0 and 1, are dimensionless (no units), and are conventionally quoted to two significant figures in Edexcel mark schemes.
  7. The 2019 June Foundation paper specifically penalised candidates who could not recall the Rf formula or who failed to give the answer to 2 s.f. — that exact value, 0.29, was the expected response.
  8. If a spot does not move from the baseline in a particular solvent, the explanation needed for a 2-mark "explain" question is that a different solvent is required because the original solvent did not dissolve that substance.
  9. Two different substances can give the same Rf value in one solvent and different Rf values in another, which is why colour must also be matched for a reliable identification.
  10. Chromatogram questions appear in the front half of both Foundation and Higher Paper 1, with the command words Calculate, Explain and Identify dominating; typical tariffs are 1–2 marks per part.
  11. For "Calculate the Rf value" questions, the two marks split predictably into (i) correct substitution into the formula and (ii) the final value quoted to the correct sig figs — so showing working is essential even when the question seems simple.
  12. A spot at the solvent front line (Rf ≈ 1) or stuck on the baseline (Rf = 0) is still a spot — make sure you count it when deciding whether a sample is pure or a mixture.
2.2.4.1 Core Practical: Investigating Inks F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. The investigation answers what is this ink made of? by combining two techniques: simple distillation to recover and identify the solvent, and paper chromatography to separate and identify the dyes.
  2. In Part A (distillation), the thermometer reading at the side-arm gives the boiling point of the solvent — this is how you identify it (around 100 °C for water, around 78 °C for ethanol). The dyes are left behind in the flask because their boiling points are far higher than the heating temperature.
  3. The thermometer bulb sits at the side-arm exit, the condenser uses counter-current cold-water flow, and the receiving end is open to the atmosphere — sealing it can shatter the glassware.
  4. In Part B (chromatography), an ink is spotted on a pencil baseline above the solvent level; the solvent rises by capillary action through the paper, carrying the dyes up at different rates.
  5. The baseline is drawn in pencil (not pen) because pen ink would itself dissolve and run up the paper, contaminating the chromatogram. The tank is covered to stop the solvent evaporating.
  6. A single coloured spot on the chromatogram means a single dye; multiple spots mean a mixture of dyes. A dye is identified by matching its position with a reference dye run on the same paper or by calculating its Rf value.
  7. Rf = distance moved by dye / distance moved by solvent front, with both measured from the baseline. The answer is always between 0 and 1 and is quoted to 2 significant figures — examiners specifically flagged this rounding rule in the 1CH0/1F June 2019 Examiner Report.
  8. If a dye stays on the baseline, the current solvent has failed to dissolve it. A different solvent is needed because it can dissolve the dye, allowing it to be carried up the paper — this is the reasoning step that the 1CH0/1H November 2020 discriminator question was looking for.
  9. Independent variable: the ink. Dependent variable: the number and position of the coloured spots (or the Rf values). Control variables: solvent, paper type, run distance, temperature, spot size, that the tank is covered.
  10. Safety: wear goggles, treat ethanol and other organic solvents as flammable (use a heating mantle if needed), always include anti-bumping granules, never seal the receiving end, and never heat the distillation flask to dryness.
  11. Past exam appearances include 1CH0/1F June 2019 Q4 (Foundation, Rf calculation and chromatogram interpretation) and 1CH0/1H November 2020 Q5(b) (Higher, Rf calculation plus solvent-reasoning discriminator).
  12. On any 2-mark "explain" or "suggest" sub-part, give both the change and the reason — for example, "Use a different solvent, because the new solvent can dissolve the dye that the original solvent could not, so the dye can travel up the paper and appear on the chromatogram."
2.2.5.1 Purifying Water F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Potable water is water that is safe to drink; it is not the same as chemistry-pure water — potable water usually still contains dissolved ions.
  2. Pure water (chemistry) contains only H₂O molecules; nothing dissolved. Distilled and deionised water are both chemistry-pure.
  3. Fresh water (ground or waste) is made potable by three named steps in order: sedimentation → filtration → chlorination. (Screening to remove twigs and leaves is a preliminary step.)
  4. Sedimentation lets large insoluble particles settle out under gravity; filtration traps smaller insoluble particles in a sand/gravel bed; chlorination kills bacteria and microbes.
  5. Filtration removes insoluble solid particles only — it does not remove dissolved salts and it does not kill or remove bacteria. The November 2021 Higher report explicitly penalises saying filtration removes bacteria or "impurities".
  6. Chlorination kills bacteria (microbes / pathogens). It does not "clean the water" or "remove impurities" — the November 2021 Higher report explicitly does not award those phrasings.
  7. The three-stage process does not remove dissolved salts; that is why tap water is potable but not chemistry-pure.
  8. Sea water is made potable by simple distillation: boil the water, condense the vapour in a separate vessel; dissolved salts are left behind in the original flask. The distillate is both potable and chemistry-pure.
  9. Distillation cannot be used for the public water supply because it has a very high energy cost — it takes a lot of energy to evaporate water, so desalination is only used where fresh water is unavailable.
  10. Water for chemical analysis must contain no dissolved salts — dissolved ions already in the water would react with test reagents and give a false positive (e.g. tap water gives a white precipitate with AgNO₃ even when the sample being tested has no chloride).
  11. Distilled water or deionised water (made by passing tap water through an ion-exchange resin) is used in the lab; tap water is not.
  12. On an "explain why potable water is not pure water" question (e.g. 1CH0/1F — June 2022 Q3(a)(iv)), pair (i) potable contains dissolved ions (cite the data table given) with (ii) pure water is only H₂O — both halves are needed for the 2 marks.
