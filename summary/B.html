Bonding & Types of Substance — Summary Sheet

1.3.1.1 Ions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An ion is an atom or group of atoms with a positive or negative charge, caused by an imbalance between the numbers of protons and electrons.
  2. Forming an ion changes only the electrons — protons and neutrons in the nucleus stay exactly the same as in the parent atom.
  3. A cation (positive ion) has lost one or more electrons; an anion (negative ion) has gained one or more electrons.
  4. The size of the charge equals the number of electrons transferred: a 2+ ion has lost 2 electrons; a 3− ion has gained 3 electrons.
  5. Protons = atomic number (Z) — this is true for both atoms and ions.
  6. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number (A − Z) — also true for both atoms and ions.
  7. Electrons = atomic number − charge (with sign) — this is the only rule where the charge matters. For Mg²⁺ that gives 12 − (+2) = 10 electrons; for O²⁻ that gives 8 − (−2) = 10 electrons.
  8. Many common ions (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, O²⁻, F⁻) reach the electronic configuration of a noble gas — a useful sanity check on your electron count.
  9. Polyatomic ions (e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺, OH⁻) carry the charge across the whole group; treat them as a unit when writing formulae.
  10. The most common exam mistake (flagged in the 1CH0/1H November 2021 Examiner Report) is treating an ion as if it were a neutral atom — always check for the charge symbol before counting protons.
  11. The second most common mistake is adjusting electrons in the wrong direction; writing "electrons = Z − charge (signed)" before you start avoids this.
  12. Exam questions on this spec point are usually short (1–3 marks) and appear early in Paper 1; the typical task is a calculate question asking for protons, neutrons or electrons given the mass number, atomic number and charge.
1.3.1.2 Formation of Ions F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Atoms form ions to achieve a full outer shell of electrons — the same stable arrangement as the nearest noble gas.
  2. Metals in groups 1 and 2 lose their outer electrons to form positive ions (cations); non-metals in groups 6 and 7 gain electrons to form negative ions (anions).
  3. The size of the charge on the ion equals the number of electrons transferred: group 1 → 1+, group 2 → 2+, group 6 → 2−, group 7 → 1−.
  4. In an ionic compound the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge — this fixes the ratio of cations to anions in the formula.
  5. NaCl forms from a 1:1 transfer of one electron from Na (2,8,1) to Cl (2,8,7), giving Na⁺ (2,8) and Cl⁻ (2,8,8).
  6. MgO forms from a 1:1 transfer of two electrons from Mg to O, giving Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, both with the configuration 2,8.
  7. MgCl₂ needs two Cl atoms per Mg atom because each Cl only accepts one electron and Mg has two to give away; Na₂O needs two Na atoms per O for the same reason in reverse.
  8. To get marks on an "explain how ions form" question, you must say which atom loses electrons, which gains, how many, and what charge each ion ends up with — never just restate the starting electron configurations.
  9. Ionic bonding is not the same as covalent bonding — atoms transfer electrons, they do not share them; dot-and-cross diagrams should show separate ions in brackets with charges outside, not a shared pair.
  10. The mathematical skill behind every formula in this spec point is simple ratio balancing: find the smallest whole numbers of cations and anions whose charges cancel out.
1.3.2.1 Ionic Bonds F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An ionic bond is the strong electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  2. Ionic bonds form between a metal (which loses electrons) and a non-metal (which gains electrons).
  3. A cation is a positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons; an anion is a negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains one or more electrons.
  4. Ionic bonding involves the transfer of electrons, not sharing — this is the key distinction from covalent bonding.
  5. Both ions reach a full outer shell (noble gas configuration), which is energetically stable and is the driving force for bond formation.
  6. The group number of the element tells you the size of the ion's charge: Group 1 = +1, Group 2 = +2, Group 6 = −2, Group 7 = −1.
  7. In a dot and cross diagram, each ion is drawn separately in square brackets with its charge written in the top-right; metal ions typically show an empty outer shell, while non-metal ions show a full outer shell of eight.
  8. The total positive charge must balance the total negative charge — this controls the formula (NaCl: 1:1; MgO: 1:1; CaF₂: 1:2; Na₂O: 2:1).
  9. When charges do not match (e.g. Ca²⁺ + F⁻), more than one of an ion is needed and each must be drawn separately with its own brackets and charge.
  10. A common exam mistake is drawing a shared pair between a metal and non-metal — this is a covalent diagram and will not score; always show transfer for ionic compounds.
  11. Larger ionic charges produce stronger electrostatic attractions, which is why MgO has a much higher melting point than NaCl.
  12. The same spec point can be examined as a short explain question, a complete-the-diagram task, or as part of an extended response combining bond formation with lattice structure.
1.3.2.2 Ionic Lattices F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An ionic lattice is a giant, regular, 3D arrangement of oppositely-charged ions that extends in all directions.
  2. Positive ions (cations) and negative ions (anions) alternate throughout the lattice — every positive ion is surrounded by negative ions, and vice versa.
  3. The lattice is held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely-charged ions — these forces are the ionic bonds.
  4. The forces act in all directions throughout the lattice, not just between one pair of ions.
  5. The ratio of positive to negative ions in the lattice matches the chemical formula of the compound (NaCl is 1:1; MgCl₂ is 1:2).
  6. A dot-and-cross diagram shows where the ions come from (electron transfer), not the structure of the solid — the structure of the solid is the lattice.
  7. The lattice structure explains why ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points: a large amount of energy is needed to break the many strong electrostatic attractions.
  8. Higher-charge ions (e.g. Mg²⁺ and O²⁻) form lattices with stronger electrostatic forces and so have higher melting points than compounds with lower-charge ions (e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻). (Higher tier)
  9. In a 2-mark "describe the structure" answer, you must include both the arrangement (regular 3D lattice of alternating positive and negative ions) and the forces (strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely-charged ions).
  10. The June 2019 1CH0/2F examiner report flagged that candidates routinely omit the word lattice and fail to mention that positive and negative ions alternate — including both phrases is the simplest way to upgrade an answer.
  11. Mathematical skill 5b (visualise and represent 2D and 3D forms) applies — you should be able to recognise and sketch a simple lattice diagram and read off the ion ratio.
  12. Lattice structure is foundational: it is reused to explain melting points, boiling points, electrical conductivity, and the brittleness of ionic compounds in later spec points.
1.3.3.1 Naming Ionic Compounds F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. An ionic compound's name is always written with the cation first (metal or ammonium) and the anion second.
  2. An ending of –ide means the anion contains only one element (e.g. chloride, oxide, sulfide, nitride, hydride).
  3. An ending of –ate means the anion is a polyatomic oxoanion — it contains oxygen as well as another non-metal (e.g. sulfate SO₄²⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻).
  4. Hydroxide (OH⁻) is the conventional exception: it has –ide ending despite containing oxygen, because the O–H pair does not count as an oxoanion.
  5. Sulfide (S²⁻) and sulfate (SO₄²⁻) are different ions — the extra oxygens in sulfate change the formula, the charge balance, and any equation you are writing.
  6. The ammonium ion NH₄⁺ behaves as a cation: compounds such as ammonium chloride, ammonium sulfate and ammonium nitrate use the standard –ide/–ate anion rules for the second word.
  7. Transition metals with variable charge use a Roman numeral, e.g. iron(II) chloride (Fe²⁺) and iron(III) chloride (Fe³⁺); the anion ending is unchanged.
  8. When an –ate anion is repeated in a formula, use brackets: Ca(NO₃)₂, not CaNO₃₂.
  9. To build a formula from a name, identify the two ions and balance their charges so total positive equals total negative.
  10. To build a name from a formula, identify the cation, classify the anion as single-element (–ide), oxoanion (–ate) or hydroxide, and combine.
  11. Naming errors compound (no pun intended) into other questions — a wrong name carried into a word equation, a balanced equation, or a calculation often loses the marks for the equation and the calculation, even if the chemistry is otherwise correct.
  12. Spelling matters: "sulfate" not "sulphate" is the spelling used in current Edexcel GCSE assessments, and Edexcel accepts both spellings on the mark scheme, but consistency within an answer is the safer habit.
1.3.3.2 Deducing Formulae F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Ionic compounds are electrically neutral, so the total positive charge must equal the total negative charge in the formula.
  2. Group 1 ions are 1+, group 2 ions are 2+, group 3 (Al) is 3+, group 6 (O) is 2−, group 7 (halides) are 1−.
  3. The six named polyatomic anions you must recognise are hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, plus the oxide O²⁻ and halides as monatomic anions.
  4. Ammonium NH₄⁺ is a 1+ polyatomic cation — treat it like a metal cation when balancing.
  5. To deduce a formula, "swap and drop" the magnitudes of the charges as subscripts, then simplify to the smallest whole-number ratio.
  6. A polyatomic ion taken more than once must be put in brackets before the subscript: Ca(OH)₂, Mg(NO₃)₂, (NH₄)₂SO₄, Fe₂(CO₃)₃.
  7. Polyatomic ions taken only once do not need brackets: NaOH, K₂SO₄, NaNO₃.
  8. Potassium sulfate is K₂SO₄, not KSO₄ — two 1+ potassium ions are needed to balance one 2− sulfate ion (the most-cited Edexcel error on this SP).
  9. Transition metal charges are not predictable — Edexcel will tell you the ion, and the Roman numeral in the name (e.g. iron(III)) gives the charge.
  10. Always simplify to the smallest ratio: magnesium oxide is MgO, not Mg₂O₂.
  11. The formula is written cation first, then anion, with all subscripts outside any brackets.
  12. Practise across all six anion families (oxides, hydroxides, halides, nitrates, carbonates, sulfates) with group 1, 2 and 3 cations plus the common transition metal ions — this skill is implicitly tested in every balanced equation and mole calculation.
1.4.1.1 Covalent Bonding F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two atoms.
  2. Covalent bonds form between non-metal atoms only.
  3. Each atom contributes one electron to the shared pair; both nuclei attract the same pair, holding the atoms together.
  4. Atoms form covalent bonds to achieve a full outer shell (2 for H, 8 for most other small non-metals).
  5. The result of covalent bonding is a molecule — a discrete, electrically neutral particle with a fixed number of atoms.
  6. Bonds can be single (1 shared pair), double (2 shared pairs, e.g. O=O), or triple (3 shared pairs, e.g. N≡N). More shared pairs = stronger and shorter bond.
  7. Covalent molecules are held together by strong covalent bonds inside the molecule, but only weak intermolecular forces between separate molecules.
  8. The typical size of an atom is about 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (~0.1 nm); small molecules are a few times larger.
  9. Nanoparticles (10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁷ m) are 10–1000 times larger than individual atoms — useful for comparing scales.
  10. The standard exam contrast is ionic vs covalent: transfer vs share, metal+non-metal vs non-metal+non-metal, lattice vs molecule.
  11. Always use the phrase "shared pair of electrons" in your definition — dropping "pair" is a common reason to lose marks.
  12. Bond-type confusion (e.g. drawing NaCl as a covalent molecule) is a documented candidate error in past Edexcel papers; always check whether you are dealing with a metal-non-metal or a non-metal-non-metal pair before deciding the bond type.
1.4.2.1 Dot & Cross Diagrams for Molecules F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms — drawn as the overlap region in a dot-and-cross diagram.
  2. Use dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other, so the source of every electron is obvious.
  3. Show outer-shell electrons only — never draw inner shells in a GCSE molecular dot-and-cross.
  4. Each H needs 2 electrons in its outer shell; every other small atom at GCSE needs 8.
  5. Count outer-shell electrons at the start (Group number) and again at the end (around each atom) to catch miscounts before you submit.
  6. Hydrogen (H₂): 1 single bond, 0 lone pairs.
  7. Hydrogen chloride (HCl): 1 single bond, 3 lone pairs on Cl.
  8. Water (H₂O): 2 single bonds (one to each H), 2 lone pairs on O.
  9. Methane (CH₄): 4 single bonds around the central C, 0 lone pairs.
  10. Oxygen (O₂): 1 double bond (2 shared pairs), 2 lone pairs on each O.
  11. Carbon dioxide (CO₂): 2 double bonds (one to each O), 2 lone pairs on each O, 0 lone pairs on C.
  12. Covalent ≠ ionic: if the question pairs a metal with a non-metal (e.g. Na + Cl), draw an ionic transfer diagram with brackets and charges — not a shared-pair covalent one.
1.5.1.1 Classifying Substances F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Substances are classified by their structure and bonding into four types: ionic, simple molecular (covalent), giant covalent and metallic.
  2. Ionic compounds form between metals and non-metals, contain a giant lattice of oppositely charged ions, have high melting and boiling points, often dissolve in water, and conduct only when molten or in solution.
  3. Simple molecular substances are made of small molecules with strong covalent bonds inside the molecule but weak intermolecular forces between molecules, giving low melting and boiling points and no electrical conductivity.
  4. Giant covalent substances (diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide) have a huge network of atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout — very high melting points, insoluble in water, and do not conduct (except graphite, which has delocalised electrons in its layers).
  5. Metallic substances are lattices of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons, which gives them high melting points, malleability and electrical conductivity as solids.
  6. Only metallic substances (and graphite) conduct electricity as solids; only ionic compounds switch from non-conducting solid to conducting liquid or solution — these two patterns are the most reliable classification fingerprints.
  7. Melting and boiling points depend on the forces broken on melting — strong electrostatic forces or covalent bonds give high melting points, weak intermolecular forces give low ones.
  8. Ionic compounds dissolve in water because water can pull the lattice apart; giant covalent and metallic substances cannot be pulled apart by water and are insoluble.
  9. Conductivity needs mobile charge carriers — delocalised electrons (metals, graphite) or freely moving ions (molten or dissolved ionic compounds).
  10. The classic exam task is a 6-mark levelled question: deduce structure and bonding from a properties table, and link each property to the structure and bonding to reach level 3.
  11. The most common mistake (per the November 2021 1CH0/1H examiner report) is mixing up the properties of ionic and simple molecular substances — always check the conductivity pattern first.
  12. Always identify the bonding type for each substance before describing the properties — without classification, the maximum mark is capped at level 2.
1.5.2.1 Comparing Ionic & Covalent Compounds F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Ionic compounds are giant 3D lattices of oppositely charged ions held together by strong electrostatic attractions — they are not made of molecules.
  2. Simple molecular compounds are made of small, discrete molecules held together by weak intermolecular forces — the covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong but are not what melts or boils.
  3. Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points because a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions across the lattice.
  4. Simple molecular compounds have low melting and boiling points because only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules need to be overcome.
  5. Ionic compounds do not conduct as solids because the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice.
  6. Ionic compounds do conduct when molten or dissolved because the lattice has broken apart and the ions are now free to move and carry charge.
  7. Simple molecular compounds do not conduct electricity in any state because there are no ions and no delocalised electrons — the molecules themselves are uncharged.
  8. Saying "the weak covalent bonds break" when CO₂ boils is the single most common Higher-tier error — it is the intermolecular forces that are overcome.
  9. The charge carriers in molten or dissolved ionic compounds are ions, not electrons — saying "free electrons" is wrong for ionic compounds.
  10. The phrase "free to move" is essential when describing conductivity — saying ions are "free" alone is not enough.
  11. To deduce the bonding from a property table: high MP + conducts only when molten/dissolved → ionic; low MP + does not conduct in any state → simple molecular.
  12. A full explanation of any property must name the structure (lattice or molecules), the forces (electrostatic attractions or intermolecular forces) and how those forces are overcome or leave particles free to move.
1.5.3.1 Diamond & Graphite F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Diamond and graphite are both giant covalent substances made of pure carbon — they are different allotropes of the same element.
  2. In diamond, each carbon forms four strong covalent bonds in a tetrahedral 3D network with no weak points.
  3. In graphite, each carbon forms three strong covalent bonds in flat hexagonal layers; the layers are held to each other by weak intermolecular forces.
  4. Diamond is hard because deforming it would require breaking many strong covalent bonds across a 3D network.
  5. Graphite is soft and slippery because layers can slide over one another — only the weak forces between layers need to be overcome, not the strong covalent bonds within the layers.
  6. Both diamond and graphite have very high melting points because melting requires breaking strong covalent bonds across a giant structure — this is true even for graphite, despite it being soft.
  7. Diamond does not conduct electricity because all four outer electrons on every carbon are used in covalent bonds — there are no delocalised electrons and no ions.
  8. Graphite does conduct electricity because each carbon has one delocalised electron (the fourth outer electron, not used in bonding) that is free to move along the layer and carry charge.
  9. Diamond is used in cutting tools because its 3D network of strong covalent bonds makes it hard enough to scratch other materials.
  10. Graphite is used to make electrodes because it conducts electricity (delocalised electrons) and has a very high melting point (strong covalent bonds in the layers).
  11. Graphite is used as a lubricant because layers slide over one another (weak intermolecular forces between layers), reducing friction.
  12. The exam discriminator is always the same: identical atoms in different arrangements give different properties — every full-mark answer must link structure to bonding to property.
1.5.4.1 Fullerenes & Graphene F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Fullerenes and graphene are allotropes of carbon — different structural forms of the same element, alongside diamond and graphite.
  2. C₆₀ (buckminsterfullerene) is a hollow molecule of exactly 60 carbon atoms arranged in 20 hexagons and 12 pentagons — like a football.
  3. Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical fullerenes — a hexagonal sheet of carbon rolled into a long hollow tube.
  4. Graphene is a single layer of graphite — one atom thick, with carbons arranged in a flat hexagonal sheet.
  5. In every fullerene and in graphene, each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds to other carbons, leaving one delocalised electron per atom.
  6. Graphene conducts electricity because its delocalised electrons are free to move across the sheet and carry charge — the same principle as in graphite and metals.
  7. Graphene is very strong because of the large number of strong covalent bonds across the hexagonal sheet; it is also very light because the sheet is only one atom thick.
  8. Graphene has a very high melting point because large amounts of energy are needed to break the many strong covalent bonds inside the sheet.
  9. Solid C₆₀ has a low melting point because individual C₆₀ molecules are held together only by weak intermolecular forces — the covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong but do not break on melting.
  10. Saying "the weak covalent bonds break" when C₆₀ melts is the same classic error as for CO₂ — what's overcome is the intermolecular forces between molecules.
  11. Uses of fullerenes include drug delivery (small molecules trapped inside the C₆₀ cage), lubricants (rolling C₆₀ molecules) and catalyst supports (large surface area of nanotubes); graphene and nanotubes are used in electronics and strong composite materials.
  12. A complete "Explain why graphene conducts" answer needs three points: three covalent bonds per carbon → one delocalised electron per carbon → electrons free to move and carry charge.
1.5.5.1 Polymers F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A polymer is a very large molecule made by joining many small molecules called monomers.
  2. Polymerisation is the reaction in which many monomers join together to form a single long polymer molecule.
  3. The named example you must know is poly(ethene), made from many ethene (C₂H₄) monomers.
  4. A simple polymer like poly(ethene) consists of a long chain of carbon atoms joined by single covalent bonds, with hydrogen atoms attached.
  5. Ethene has a C=C double bond; when poly(ethene) forms, this double bond opens up and new single C–C bonds form between former-monomer units.
  6. The bonds along the polymer chain are covalent — polymers are covalent substances, not ionic.
  7. The names poly(ethene), polythene and polyethylene all refer to the same substance; "poly(ethene)" is the safest spelling in an Edexcel exam.
  8. Poly(ethene) is a solid at room temperature because the very long chains interact with their neighbours over many points, giving total intermolecular forces much larger than those between small molecules like ethene.
  9. To calculate the Mr of a length of poly(ethene), multiply the Mr of the ethene monomer (28) by the number of monomers (n) that have joined — no atoms are lost in addition polymerisation, so the masses add up exactly.
  10. At this spec point you are not expected to draw the repeat unit, write a balanced equation, or distinguish addition from condensation polymerisation — that content belongs to Section 9.
  11. The most common errors are confusing the monomer with the polymer, leaving out hydrogen atoms in mass calculations, and assuming water is released when ethene polymerises (it is not — this is addition polymerisation, and all atoms are conserved).
1.5.6.1 Properties of Metals F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. A metal is a giant regular arrangement of positive metal ions surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons.
  2. Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons, acting throughout the whole structure.
  3. Metals conduct electricity because the delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge — the ions stay in place.
  4. Metals are malleable and ductile because the layers of positive ions can slide over each other while the surrounding electron sea keeps the structure held together in every direction.
  5. Metals have high melting and boiling points because a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attractions between the ions and the delocalised electrons.
  6. Most metals are dense because the ions are packed closely together, and shiny because the surface electrons reflect light.
  7. Most non-metals have low boiling points (often gases or low-melting solids), low densities, and are poor conductors — graphite is the famous exception.
  8. The word most in spec 1.42 matters: mercury is a liquid metal, graphite is a conducting non-metal, and iodine is a shiny non-metallic solid.
  9. When explaining a metal's property, name the structure (layers of positive ions), the bonding (sea of delocalised electrons, strong electrostatic attractions), and the feature doing the work (electrons free to move, or layers sliding past one another).
  10. On compare questions, always use comparative wording — higher, lower, stronger, more conductive. Words like "good" or "better" without an explicit "than" comparator do not score (1CH0/1F — June 2019 Examiner Report).
  11. Never just quote numbers back from a data table without comparing them — the comparison itself is what earns the mark.
  12. The charge carriers in a metal are electrons; in a molten or aqueous ionic compound they are ions. Do not mix the two pictures up.
1.5.7.1 Limitations of Models F+H
Key Takeaways
  1. Every chemical model is a simplified picture that emphasises one feature and hides others — no single model can show everything at once.
  2. To answer a "limitations" question for full marks you must say both what each model does show and what it fails to show.
  3. Dot and cross diagrams show electron transfer/sharing and the origin of bonding electrons, but do not show 3D shape, real bond angles, atomic sizes or electron motion.
  4. Ball and stick models show 3D atom positions, bond angles and overall molecular shape, but atoms are not really hard spheres, bonds are not rigid sticks, and the gaps between atoms are exaggerated.
  5. 2D structural formulae show connectivity, bond types and functional groups quickly, but are flat and so cannot show 3D shape or real bond angles.
  6. 3D space-filling models show relative atomic sizes and the overall shape and volume of the molecule, but obscure the bonds and do not show electron density or motion.
  7. The right model depends on the question — choose the model that highlights the feature being asked about.
  8. Electrons in real bonds are not static dots; they are constantly moving regions of electron density between atoms.
  9. Atoms in real molecules vibrate and bonds can rotate — static models cannot show this.
  10. The 1CH0/2H June 2022 examiner report specifically flagged that candidates failed to explicitly state what models cannot show — describing only what is shown loses marks.
  11. The command word Describe with a 6-mark tariff at the end of a Higher tier paper is the most common setting for this spec point.
  12. A safe full-mark strategy: for each model, write one sentence on what it shows and one sentence on what it fails to show.
